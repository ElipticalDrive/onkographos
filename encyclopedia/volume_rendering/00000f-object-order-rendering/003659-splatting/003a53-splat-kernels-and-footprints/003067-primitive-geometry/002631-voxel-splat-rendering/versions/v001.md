# Voxel Splat Rendering

Also called: Interactive Splat Rendering with Alpha/Gamma Control, Interactive value selection rendering

## Overview
Voxel Splat Rendering is an object-space, front-to-back volume rendering technique that projects 3D voxels onto the 2D screen as weighted splats (square or circular primitives) to synthesize a final image [S3, unofficial]. It solves the problem of efficiently visualizing large, sparse volumetric datasets by allowing interactive selection of specific voxel subsets (e.g., based on color or spatial bounds) and fine-tuning visual appearance via alpha and gamma parameters [S2, unofficial]. Unlike traditional raycasting, it avoids per-ray traversal by treating each voxel as an independent contributor to screen-space pixels, making it suitable for real-time interaction with out-of-core data [S2, S3, unofficial].

## How it works

### Inputs and Outputs
**Inputs:**
*   A 3D volumetric dataset where each voxel contains position, color, and optionally a surface normal [S1][S2, unofficial].
*   Camera parameters: Eye position, view direction, and projection matrix [S1].
*   Rendering parameters: Point size (splat diameter), alpha value, gamma value, and anti-aliasing settings [S2, unofficial].
*   Selection criteria: A set of active colors/voxelsets and an optional bounding box for spatial culling [S2, unofficial].

**Outputs:**
*   A 2D color image (and optionally depth/normal buffers) representing the volume from the current viewpoint [S1][S2, unofficial].

### Data Structures
*   **Voxel:** Contains position $(x, y, z)$, color $(R, G, B)$, and a normal vector $(N_x, N_y, N_z)$ [S1][S2, unofficial].
    *   *Note:* Normals may be quantized to one of 240 pre-defined directions stored in a lookup table, requiring only 1 byte per voxel for normal storage [S2, unofficial].
*   **Voxel Set:** A subset of voxels selected by color or spatial bounds, retrieved from disk in real-time [S2, unofficial].
*   **Screen Buffer:** Accumulates the final color values. If alpha-blending is disabled, a separate buffer may store normals for shading [S2, unofficial].

### Algorithm Steps

1.  **Voxel Selection and Retrieval:**
    *   Identify voxels belonging to the active voxelset based on user-selected colors or spatial bounding box constraints [S2, unofficial].
    *   Retrieve and decode only the selected voxels from storage [S2, unofficial].

2.  **Front-to-Back Sorting:**
    *   Sort the selected voxels by their depth relative to the camera eye position to ensure correct front-to-back blending [S3, unofficial].

3.  **Projection and Splat Generation:**
    *   For each voxel, compute its screen-space position and axis-aligned bounding rectangle (AABB) [S1].
    *   The voxel is projected as a splat whose contribution is highest at the center and drops off away from it [S3, unofficial].
    *   The splat is rendered as a quad (two triangles) or a point primitive with a fixed or distance-dependent diameter [S1][S2, unofficial].

4.  **Weight Calculation (View-Dependent Blending):**
    *   To blend multiple camera views or reduce artifacts, compute a weight for each voxel based on its orientation relative to the eye and the source camera [S1].
    *   Let $V_{toEye}$ be the unit vector from the voxel center to the eye.
    *   Let $N$ be the unit normal at the voxel center [S1].
    *   Let $V_{toCamera[i]}$ be the unit vector from the voxel center to the position of camera $i$ [S1].
    *   Compute intermediate dot products:
        $$
        dotV = V_{toEye} \cdot V_{toCamera[i]}
        $$
        $$
        dotN = N \cdot V_{toCamera[i]}
        $$
    *   Compute view and normal weights using a power exponent of 4 [S1]:
        $$
        weightV = \begin{cases} dotV^4 & \text{if } dotV > 0.0 \\ 0.0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
        $$
        $$
        weightN = \begin{cases} dotN^4 & \text{if } dotN > 0.0 \\ 0.0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
        $$
    *   The raw weight for camera $i$ is:
        $$
        weight[i] = weightV \times weightN
        $$
    *   Normalize the weights by dividing by the sum of all weights:
        $$
        weight_{final}[i] = \frac{weight[i]}{\sum_{j} weight[j]}
        $$
    *   *Note:* The power function reduces artifacts in transitions between dominant textures [S1].

5.  **Rasterization and Blending:**
    *   **With Alpha-Blending (Volume View):**
        *   Render the splats using alpha blending.
        *   Apply gamma correction to reduce the contribution of points from tissues with differing color densities [S2, unofficial].
        *   The final color $C_{final}$ is accumulated using standard front-to-back alpha blending:
            $$
            C_{final} = C_{src} \cdot \alpha_{src} + C_{dst} \cdot (1 - \alpha_{src})
            $$
            where $\alpha_{src}$ is modulated by the rendering alpha parameter and the computed weights [S1][S2, unofficial].
    *   **Without Alpha-Blending (Surface View):**
        *   Render splats without blending.
        *   Use the stored normals to shade the points (e.g., using Phong shading) to create the illusion of a shaded surface, provided point density is high enough [S2, unofficial].

### Pseudocode

```python
def render_voxel_splats(voxels, eye_pos, cameras, params):
    """
    Render voxels as splats.
    voxels: List of Voxel objects with pos, color, normal.
    eye_pos: Vec3, camera eye position.
    cameras: List of Vec3, positions of source cameras for weighting.
    params: Dict with 'alpha', 'gamma', 'point_size'.
    """
    # 1. Sort voxels front-to-back relative to eye
    sorted_voxels = sort_by_depth(voxels, eye_pos)
    
    # Initialize output buffer
    output_buffer = initialize_buffer()
    
    for voxel in sorted_voxels:
        # 2. Project voxel to screen space
        screen_rect = project_to_screen(voxel.pos, eye_pos)
        
        # 3. Compute weights for blending from multiple cameras
        total_weight = 0.0
        weighted_color = Vec3(0, 0, 0)
        
        for cam_pos in cameras:
            # Vectors
            v_to_eye = normalize(eye_pos - voxel.pos)
            v_to_cam = normalize(cam_pos - voxel.pos)
            normal = voxel.normal
            
            # Dot products
            dot_v = dot(v_to_eye, v_to_cam)
            dot_n = dot(normal, v_to_cam)
            
            # Weight calculation with power 4
            weight_v = pow(dot_v, 4) if dot_v > 0.0 else 0.0
            weight_n = pow(dot_n, 4) if dot_n > 0.0 else 0.0
            weight = weight_v * weight_n
            
            total_weight += weight
            
            # Accumulate weighted color
            weighted_color += voxel.color * weight
            
        # Normalize weights
        if total_weight > 0:
            weighted_color /= total_weight
        else:
            weighted_color = voxel.color
            
        # 4. Apply Gamma and Alpha
        # Gamma correction: typically gamma=2.2 for display, but here used to adjust density contribution
        adjusted_color = pow(weighted_color, 1.0 / params['gamma'])
        
        # 5. Rasterize Splat
        # Render quad/triangles covering screen_rect
        # Use alpha blending if params['alpha'] > 0
        if params['alpha'] > 0:
            alpha = params['alpha']
            blend_into_buffer(output_buffer, screen_rect, adjusted_color, alpha)
        else:
            # Surface view: shade using normal
            shaded_color = shade_point(voxel.pos, voxel.normal, eye_pos, adjusted_color)
            rasterize_splat(output_buffer, screen_rect, shaded_color)

def blend_into_buffer(buffer, rect, color, alpha):
    """
    Standard front-to-back alpha blending for the region defined by rect.
    """
    for pixel in rect:
        src_color = color
        dst_color = buffer[pixel]
        # Simple alpha blend
        buffer[pixel] = src_color * alpha + dst_color * (1.0 - alpha)
```

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Close-Range Views:** At close range, voxels project to large areas, appearing as rectangular blocks rather than smooth primitives, causing pixelation artifacts [S1].
*   **Single-Pixel Quads:** GPU rasterization is not optimized for very small quads (approx. 1 pixel size at HD resolution for giga-voxel datasets), though performance remains acceptable [S1].
*   **Aliasing:** Minor aliasing artifacts occur, similar to non-anti-aliased triangle models [S1].
*   **Color Ambiguity:** Different tissues may share similar colors, leading to irrelevant points being selected. Using a bounding box for spatial culling helps mitigate this by limiting the decoded voxelset [S2, unofficial].
*   **Normal Quantization:** Using only 240 pre-defined normals (1 byte storage) may reduce shading accuracy compared to continuous normals [S2, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics

*   **Time Complexity:** $O(N \log N)$ or $O(N)$ depending on sorting implementation, where $N$ is the number of selected voxels. Projection and rasterization are linear in the number of splats.
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(N)$ for storing the active voxel set in memory. Out-of-core retrieval allows handling datasets larger than RAM [S2, unofficial].
*   **Performance:** Acceptable real-time performance on standard GPUs, even for giga-voxel resolutions, provided the number of active splats is manageable [S1].
*   **Strengths:**
    *   Interactive value selection via slice clicking [S2, unofficial].
    *   Simple implementation using standard GL commands [S2, unofficial].
    *   Effective for sparse datasets where many voxels are empty or irrelevant [S1].
*   **Limitations:**
    *   Artifacts at close range (blockiness) [S1].
    *   Less accurate than raycasting for complex internal structures due to the approximation of voxel contribution as a splat [S3, unofficial].
    *   Performance degrades if the number of selected voxels is very high [S2, unofficial].

## Background

Voxel Splat Rendering is an object-space, front-to-back volume rendering method where voxels are projected onto the screen as contributions that are highest at the center and drop off away from it [S3, unofficial]. The general concept of splatting was first published in [20] (cited in [S3, unofficial]). The specific implementation described in the notes, featuring interactive value selection, alpha/gamma control, and out-of-core rendering, was developed for visualizing very large datasets, such as medical or biological scans, where different tissues share similar colors [S2, unofficial]. It is often compared to Fourier Volume Rendering and Maximum Intensity Projection in terms of visual output but differs in its primitive-based projection approach [S3, unofficial].
