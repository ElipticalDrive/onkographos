# Volumetric textures with textured slices

Also called: 3D layer mapping on surface, Textured slices for vegetation

## Overview
Volumetric textures with textured slices render a 3D layer (such as continuous vegetation or fur) on a surface by mapping a 3D data set as a texture pattern onto a series of parallel slices [S1]. It solves the problem of efficiently rendering dense, heterogeneous volumetric layers that would otherwise require excessive polygon counts or suffer from popping artifacts in point-based methods [S1]. It sets itself apart from sibling methods by generating fragments that are "really 3D" at their proper depth, providing perfect parallax and leveraging hardware texture filtering for smooth antialiasing, which polygonal data lacks [S1]. Unlike view-aligned slicing, it does not force slices to face the camera, allowing for more natural surface-conforming geometry but requiring careful viewpoint adaptation to prevent gaps [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** A 3D texture volume representing the layer material (e.g., tree canopy density, fur color); a proxy surface geometry (typically a terrain mesh or ground plane); and the viewer's position and orientation [S1].
*   **Output:** A rasterized image of the volumetric layer with correct depth, parallax, and filtering [S1].

**Data Structures:**
*   **3D Texture:** A 3D texture object storing the volumetric data [S1].
*   **Slice Geometry:** A set of polygons (quads or triangles) representing the intersection of the volume with parallel planes, defined in world or object space [S1].
*   **Pre-composited Images (Variant):** A pyramid of 2D images generated by Porter-Duff compositing pairs of original layers, used in multi-resolution variants [S2].

**Steps:**
1.  **Proxy Geometry Generation:** Define a series of parallel planes intersecting the volume [S1]. The spacing and orientation depend on the specific variant (see Subtopics) [S1, S2].
2.  **Intersection and Tessellation:** Compute the intersection of the slicing planes with the proxy surface or volume bounds [G]. Tessellate these intersections into triangles or quads [G]. The vertices of these polygons are positioned at their correct 3D coordinates within the volume [S1].
3.  **Texture Coordinate Assignment:** Map the 3D position of each polygon vertex to 3D texture coordinates $(u, v, w)$ corresponding to the 3D texture volume [S1]. This ensures that the texture pattern is sampled according to the physical position in the layer [S1].
4.  **Slicing Direction Selection (Optional):** If using the MN98 variant, select one of three slicing directions based on the viewpoint: one horizontal (parallel to the ground) and two vertical (orthogonal to the ground, following u or v texture axes) [S1].
5.  **Rendering and Compositing:** Render the slice polygons back-to-front relative to the viewer [S1]. Use hardware alpha blending to composite the fragments [S1]. The standard blending equation is:
    $$ C_{dest} = C_{src} \cdot A_{src} + C_{dest} \cdot (1 - A_{src}) $$
    where $C_{src}$ and $A_{src}$ are the color and opacity from the texture sample, and $C_{dest}$ is the current frame buffer color [G]. This implements the discrete volume rendering integral when slices are processed in correct depth order [S1].
6.  **Viewpoint Adaptation:** Adjust the slice spacing or direction dynamically based on the viewer's distance and angle to prevent visible gaps between slices at grazing angles [S1].

**Formulas:**
*   **Screen Projection for Multi-resolution (C2 Criterion):** To determine the number of layers $n$ such that the projected screen distance $l$ of a texel height $H$ is limited by a threshold:
    $$ l = F \times H \times \sin(a) $$
    $$ F \times H \times \sin(a) < n $$
    where $F$ is a focal length factor, $H$ is the texel height, $a$ is the angle of sight, and $n$ represents the layer count threshold [S2].
*   **Minimum Sections (C1 Criterion):** If the texel is seen nearly axially or the view deviates little:
    $$ n > \frac{H \times \tan(a)}{d_0} $$
    where $d_0$ is a constant threshold [S2].
*   **Final Layer Count:**
    $$ n_{final} = \max(C1, C2) $$
    where $C1$ and $C2$ are the results from the respective criteria [S2].

**Parameters:**
*   **Slicing Spacing:** Determined by the criteria above or fixed for simple variants [S2].
*   **Thresholds $d_0, d_1$:** Constants defining quality limits [S2].
*   **Focal Length $F$:** Camera parameter [S2].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Grazing Angles:** If slicing is not adapted to the viewpoint, gaps appear between slices at grazing angles, revealing the background incorrectly [S1].
*   **Heterogeneous Data:** The "fins" variant (LPFH01) fails for heterogeneous data like trees because it assumes homogeneity (like fur) [S1].
*   **Degenerate Quality:** When the viewing angle is very acute, the primary slicing direction may produce degenerated quality; alternate directions must be introduced [S2].
*   **Sorting:** Slices must be rendered in correct depth order (back-to-front) for proper alpha blending [S1].

    planes = []

## Subtopics

### Surface-parallel slicing with fins
Uses only slicing planes parallel to the surface and adds fins (edges extruded along the normal) near the silhouette to cover gaps [S1]. It works well for homogeneous data like fur but fails for heterogeneous data like trees [S1].

### 3D slicing with direction switching
Switches between three slicing directions: one horizontal (parallel to the ground) and two vertical (orthogonal to the ground, following u or v texture parameterization) [S1]. This allows adaptation to the viewpoint to minimize visible gaps [S1].

### Multi-resolution Layer Selection
Reduces the number of display layers while maintaining visual quality by balancing realism and computing speed [S2]. It uses two criteria (C1 and C2) to determine the minimum number of layers $n$ based on distance and angle [S2]. It pre-composites intermediate images using Porter-Duff compositing to allow dynamic selection of layer count based on viewpoint [S2]. When the viewing angle is acute, it introduces two alternate directions of sections to avoid degenerated quality [S2].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Rendering time is proportional to the number of slice polygons [G]. The number of polygons is significantly lower than point-based methods because each pattern instance covers a portion of the forest containing several trees [S1]. Multi-resolution variants add pre-computation cost but reduce runtime polygon count [S2].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storage for the 3D texture volume and the slice geometry [G]. Multi-resolution variants require additional memory for the pyramid of pre-composited images [S2].
*   **Performance:** Hardware acceleration is leveraged for texture filtering, which is smoothly managed by the texture hardware [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Perfect parallax (fragments are at proper depth), smooth hardware filtering, fewer polygons than point-based methods [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Requires careful viewpoint adaptation to prevent gaps at grazing angles [S1]. Slicing direction selection can be complex [S1]. Not suitable for highly heterogeneous data if using simplified variants like fins [S1].
*   **When to Use:** Rendering continuous vegetation layers, fur, or other homogeneous volumetric textures where parallax and filtering quality are important [S1].
*   **When Not to Use:** When the data is highly heterogeneous and simple slicing fails to capture details (e.g., individual trees with complex geometry) [S1]. When real-time performance is critical and pre-computation is not feasible [S2].

## Background
The volumetric textures approach was first introduced in ray-tracing and later adapted to hardware rendering [S1]. It was developed to address the drawbacks of point-based and line-based methods, which suffer from popping artifacts when distance adaptation is done by suppressing elements, and lack proper antialiasing due to opaque primitives [S1]. The method is particularly adapted to layers of continuous vegetation covering a landscape [S1]. Real-time volume rendering tools like Volumizer generally prefer slices facing the view direction for better antialiasing, but this complicates adaptive slicing for 3D layers mapped on surfaces [S1].
