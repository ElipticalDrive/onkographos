# Projective Texture Mapping

Also called: Plenoptic Surface Texture Mapping, Projective Space Texture Mapping

## Overview
Projective texture mapping assigns solid textures to volume objects by projecting the 3D geometry onto a 2D intermediate template (plenoptic surface) and mapping texture coordinates from this template to the volume, avoiding explicit $uv$-parameterization of the volume geometry [S1, unofficial]. It differs from [2D Texture-Based Volume Rendering](916-2d-texture-based-volume-rendering.md) by synthesizing or warping detail to match local data and surface geometry using key points, rather than reconstructing existing detail [S1, unofficial]. It distinguishes itself from [Cabral Texture Mapping](7257-cabral-texture-mapping.md) by using a plenoptic surface to handle complex topologies without requiring mesh filling or handling topological holes [S1, unofficial].

## Shared principles
The method relies on a **plenoptic surface** $P$, which is a regular bounding shape (sphere, cylinder, or box) enclosing the volume, with its center of projection located within the volume data [S1, unofficial]. The core mechanism involves two inverse projections:
1.  **Forward Projection (Template Generation):** The volume surface $S$ is projected outwards onto the interior of $P$, and $P$ is unwound to a 2D intermediate template $T$ [S1, unofficial]. This template is indexed by integral coordinates $(x, y)$ corresponding to resolution intervals $\Delta u, \Delta v$ on the continuous parameterization $u, v \in [0, 1]$ [S1, unofficial].
2.  **Inverse Projection (Texturing):** For any point in the volume, a ray is cast through the point and the projection center to intersect $P$. The intersection yields continuous coordinates $(u, v)$ on the plenoptic surface, which are mapped to coordinates in a 2D texture image [S1, unofficial].

The resolution of the morphed texture image directly impacts accuracy; higher resolutions reduce aliasing artifacts known as the "cliff effect" [S1, unofficial]. The cliff effect occurs when a single pixel projected onto a surface almost parallel to the direction of projection is smeared over a large area [S1, unofficial].

## Subtopics

### [Multi-constraints-based projective texture mapping](12075-multi-constraints-based-projective-texture-mapping.md)
This method places textures on iso-surfaces by warping a source texture to align with user-defined key points on an intermediate template, then rendering via ray-casting. It solves the problem of ill-conditioned direct projection by using spatial and semantic constraints (transfer functions) to control texture position. Unlike traditional mesh-based mapping, it does not require filling topological holes; regions with artefacts receive the same texture as their radial neighborhood. It uses data-dependent triangulation for interpolation to retain edge quality, superior to standard bilinear interpolation.

### [Projective Space Texture Mapping with Multi-Resolution Templates](13236-projective-space-texture-mapping-with-multi-resolution-templ.md)
This technique assigns solid textures to any position within a volume using plenoptic projection functions as pseudo-procedure functional models. It connects 3D positions to 2D projective positions on a plenoptic surface, and then to integral coordinates in a morphed texture image. It is distinguished by its use of multi-resolution intermediate templates generated via forward projection (DVR/DSR) and inverse projection to mitigate aliasing artifacts.

### High-Resolution Patching
**Definition:** A technique to overcome resolution limitations by directly importing a high-resolution image (patch) for a specific portion of the morphed texture image during texture mapping [S1, unofficial].
**Steps:** Import a high-resolution texture image (patch) during texture mapping, with or without morphing, to replace or composite with the low-resolution area [S1, unofficial]. Fine-tune the inverse projection to an extremely small area to digitally composite textures using different texture images (e.g., cliff texture and horse leg texture) [S1, unofficial].
**Details:** In a horse-on-cliff experiment, the paint area in the low-resolution texture was $66 \times 20$ pixels, while the high-resolution horse image was $256 \times 166$ pixels. Using the high-res patch preserved details, whereas the low-res patch suffered from alias artifacts and detail loss [S1, unofficial]. The projective space texture is defined in continuous 3D space, allowing the inverse projection to be fine-tuned to an extremely small area to composite textures [S1, unofficial].

### MVS-Texturing
**Definition:** Classical texturing baseline that projects training images as textures onto a CAD mesh [S2, unofficial].
**Pitfalls:** Produces patchwork textures with visible seams. Fails on wide-baseline views, causing abrupt transitions, misalignment artifacts, holes, and missing parts. Test-time refinement causes divergence [S2, unofficial].
**Metrics:** Six-scene average PSNR: 11.77, mPSNR: 14.64, fgPSNR: 6.83 [S2, unofficial].

## Background
The method originates from the work of P. Shen and P. Willis, published in "Texture Mapping Volume Objects" [S1, unofficial]. It addresses the limitations of traditional texture mapping on volumetric data, which often requires explicit parameterization or suffers from distortion on complex geometries. The approach leverages the concept of plenoptic surfaces to provide a unified framework for projecting textures onto arbitrary volume shapes.
