# MIP-Splatting

Also called: Alias-free 3D Gaussian Splatting, Anti-Aliased 3D Gaussian Splatting, MS 3DGS, MS-GS, Multi-Scale Gaussians, Multi-scale 3D Gaussian Splatting

## Overview
MIP-Splatting is a rendering method for 3D Gaussian Splatting that eliminates aliasing artifacts caused by resolution changes, focal length variations, or camera distance changes [S1][S7, unofficial]. It addresses the failure of standard 3D Gaussian Splatting's dilation filter to suppress high-frequency artifacts when the sampling rate differs from training conditions [S1][S7, unofficial]. Unlike standard 3DGS which suffers from aliasing and speed deterioration at low resolutions, MIP-Splatting achieves significant PSNR improvements and rendering speedups by combining a 3D smoothing filter with a 2D Mip filter and multi-scale Gaussian selection [S3][S7, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs:** A set of 3D Gaussians $\{G_k\}$ optimized from input images, camera parameters (intrinsics $K$, extrinsics $R, t$), and a target rendering resolution/scale.
**Outputs:** A rendered 2D image.

**Data Structures:**
Each Gaussian $G_k$ stores:
- Position $p_k \in \mathbb{R}^3$
- Covariance matrix $\Sigma_k \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$ (parameterized by rotation $O_k$ and scaling $s_k$)
- Opacity $\alpha_k \in [0, 1]$
- Color features (e.g., Spherical Harmonics coefficients)
- Level of detail index $l_k \in \{1, \dots, l_{max}\}$
- Maximum pixel coverage $S_{k,max}$ and minimum pixel coverage $S_{k,min}$ [S3].

**Parameters:**
- Number of detail levels: 4 levels ($l_{max}=4$), corresponding to 1x, 4x, 16x, and 64x downsampled resolutions [S3].
- Selective rendering threshold $S_T = 2$ pixels [S3].
- Opacity threshold $\sigma_T = 1/255$ [S3].
- Relative maximum coverage $S_{rel,max} = 1.5$ [S3].
- Relative minimum coverage $S_{rel,min} = 0.5$ [S3].
- Decay coefficient $\lambda_1 = 0.95$ [S3].
- Decay coefficient $\lambda_2 = 1.05$ [S3].
- 3D smoothing filter variance scalar $s_{3D} = 0.2$ [S7, unofficial].
- 2D Mip filter variance scalar $s_{2D} = 0.1$ [S7, unofficial].
- Voxel grid resolution for aggregation: $(400/l_m)^3$ [S3].
- Iteration interval for recomputing maximal sampling rate: $m=100$ [S7, unofficial].

**Method Steps:**

1.  **3D Smoothing Filter (Training):**
    To constrain the maximal frequency of the 3D representation to meet the Nyquist limit of the training data, a 3D Gaussian low-pass filter $G_{low}$ is applied to each primitive $G_k$ before projection [S7, unofficial].
    - Calculate the world space sampling interval $\hat{T}$ at depth $d$ for focal length $f$: $\hat{T} = d/f$.
    - Calculate the sampling frequency $\hat{\nu} = 1/\hat{T} = f/d$ [S7, unofficial].
    - The regularized 3D Gaussian $G_k(x)_{reg}$ is the convolution of $G_k$ and $G_{low}$, resulting in a covariance $\Sigma_k + \hat{\nu}_k^s \cdot I$, where $s$ is the scalar hyperparameter controlling filter size [S7, unofficial].
    $$ G_k(x)_{reg} = \frac{\sqrt{|\Sigma_k|}}{\sqrt{|\Sigma_k + \hat{\nu}_k^s \cdot I|}} e^{-\frac{1}{2}(x-p_k)^T (\Sigma_k + \hat{\nu}_k^s \cdot I)^{-1} (x-p_k)} $$
    - Recompute the maximal sampling rate every $m$ iterations as centers remain stable [S7, unofficial].

2.  **Multi-Scale Aggregation (Training):**
    After a warm-up stage (first 1,000 iterations), coarse-level Gaussians are introduced by aggregating fine-level Gaussians that are too small [S3].
    - For all levels $l_m$ from 2 to $l_{max}$:
        1.  Render all Gaussians from levels $[1, l_m-1]$ at $4^{l_m-1}$ times downsampled resolution for all training cameras to calculate pixel coverage $S_k$ [S3].
        2.  Identify "small" Gaussians where $S_k < S_T$ [S3].
        3.  Bin small Gaussians into a voxel grid of size $(400/l_m)^3$ [S3].
        4.  For each voxel, perform average pooling on position, rotation, spherical harmonics, opacity, and scaling of contained Gaussians to create a new Gaussian [S3].
        5.  Calculate the average pixel coverage $S_{avg}$ of the aggregated Gaussians [S3].
        6.  Enlarge the scaling of the new Gaussian by factor $S_T / S_{avg}$ to target pixel coverage $S_T$ [S3].
        7.  Assign the new Gaussian to level $l_m$ [S3].
    - Train the model with both original and downsampled images [S3].

3.  **Selective Rendering (Inference):**
    Gaussians are selected based on their pixel coverage relative to the current render resolution [S3].
    - Calculate pixel coverage $S_k$ for all Gaussians at the current render scale [S3].
    - Update $S_{k,max}$ and $S_{k,min}$ if the rendering downsample scale equals the scale when the Gaussian was created:
        $$ S_{k,max}' = \max(\lambda_1 S_{k,max}, S_k) $$
        $$ S_{k,min}' = \min(\lambda_2 S_{k,min}, S_k) $$
    - A Gaussian is selected for rendering if:
        $$ \frac{S_k}{S_{k,max}} \le S_{rel,max} \quad \land \quad \left( \frac{S_k}{S_{k,min}} \ge S_{rel,min} \lor S_k \ge S_T \right) $$
    - Render only the selected Gaussians [S3].

4.  **2D Mip Filter (Rendering):**
    Replace the standard 2D dilation operation with a 2D Mip filter that approximates the physical box filter of a camera sensor [S7, unofficial].
    - Project the 3D Gaussian to ray space via local affine transformation $\Sigma''_k = J_k \Sigma'_k J_k^T$ [S7, unofficial].
    - The 2D Mip-filtered Gaussian uses a covariance $\Sigma_{k2D} + sI$, where $s$ is chosen to cover a single pixel in screen space [S7, unofficial].
    $$ G_{k2D}(x)_{mip} = \frac{\sqrt{|\Sigma_{k2D}|}}{\sqrt{|\Sigma_{k2D} + sI|}} e^{-\frac{1}{2}(x-p_k)^T (\Sigma_{k2D} + sI)^{-1} (x-p_k)} $$
    - This prevents dilation artifacts when zooming out and erosion when zooming in [S7, unofficial].

**Pseudocode:**

```python
def aggregate_small_gaussians(G_all, l_max, S_T):
    """
    Aggregates small Gaussians into larger ones for coarser levels.
    G_all: List of all Gaussians.
    l_max: Maximum level index (e.g., 4).
    S_T: Selective rendering threshold (2 pixels).
    """
    for l_m in range(2, l_max + 1):
        # Render all Gaussians from levels [1, l_m-1] at 4^(l_m-1) downsampled resolution
        # Calculate pixel coverage S_k for all Gaussians
        S_k = calculate_pixel_coverage(G_all, scale=4**(l_m-1))
        
        # Select small Gaussians
        G_small = [G for G in G_all if S_k[G.id] < S_T]
        
        # Voxel grid resolution
        voxel_res = 400 / l_m
        voxel_size = (voxel_res, voxel_res, voxel_res)
        
        # Bin Gaussians into voxels
        voxels = create_voxel_grid(voxel_size)
        for G in G_small:
            voxel_idx = get_voxel_index(G.position, voxel_size)
            voxels[voxel_idx].append(G)
            
        # Aggregate and insert
        for voxel in voxels:
            if not voxel:
                continue
            # Average pooling of attributes
            G_new = average_pool_gaussians(voxel)
            
            # Calculate average pixel coverage of aggregated group
            S_avg = mean([S_k[G.id] for G in voxel])
            
            # Enlarge scaling to target S_T
            scale_factor = S_T / S_avg
            G_new.scale = G_new.scale * scale_factor
            
            # Assign to level l_m
            G_new.level = l_m
            insert_into_scene(G_new)

def selective_render(G_all, render_scale):
    """
    Selects Gaussians for rendering based on pixel coverage.
    """
    # Calculate current pixel coverage
    S_k = calculate_pixel_coverage(G_all, scale=render_scale)
    
    # Update max/min coverage for Gaussians at their creation scale
    for G in G_all:
        if G.level == get_creation_scale(render_scale):
            S_k_max[G.id] = max(0.95 * S_k_max[G.id], S_k[G.id])
            S_k_min[G.id] = min(1.05 * S_k_min[G.id], S_k[G.id])
            
    # Selection logic
    S_rel_max = 1.5
    S_rel_min = 0.5
    S_T = 2.0
    
    selected_Gs = []
    for G in G_all:
        sk = S_k[G.id]
        sk_max = S_k_max[G.id]
        sk_min = S_k_min[G.id]
        
        cond1 = (sk / sk_max) <= S_rel_max
        cond2 = (sk / sk_min) >= S_rel_min
        cond3 = sk >= S_T
        
        if cond1 and (cond2 or cond3):
            selected_Gs.append(G)
            
    return render_gaussians(selected_Gs, render_scale)

def apply_2d_mip_filter(G_projected, pixel_variance_s):
    """
    Applies 2D Mip filter to a projected 2D Gaussian.
    G_projected: 2D Gaussian with covariance Sigma_k2D.
    pixel_variance_s: Variance to cover a single pixel.
    """
    Sigma_k2D = G_projected.covariance
    # Add variance to covariance
    Sigma_reg = Sigma_k2D + pixel_variance_s * Identity(3)
    
    # Compute normalization factor for opacity preservation
    det_orig = determinant(Sigma_k2D)
    det_reg = determinant(Sigma_reg)
    C = sqrt(det_orig / det_reg)
    
    G_projected.opacity = G_projected.opacity * C
    G_projected.covariance = Sigma_reg
    
    return G_projected
```

## Subtopics

### [2D Mip Filter](9593-2d-mip-filter.md)
The 2D Mip Filter mitigates aliasing artifacts in 3D Gaussian Splatting when rendering from camera distances or focal lengths larger than those encountered during training. It extends the standard 2D Gaussian filter by introducing a view-dependent opacity compensation factor that prevents excessive blurring as Gaussians become smaller relative to the pixel grid. Unlike the basic 2D Gaussian filter which dilates the covariance matrix uniformly, this method dynamically adjusts opacity based on the ratio of the original to the dilated covariance determinant.

### LinPrim Anti-Aliasing
An anti-aliasing strategy combining a 3D smoothing filter and a bounding box expansion technique to limit high frequencies and prevent aliasing/dilation artifacts [S9]. It applies a 3D smoothing strategy to limit how small primitives can become based on visibility from training views, ensuring each primitive is visible from at least one pixel [S9]. It also expands the bounding box vertices outward, parallel to the screen, to enlarge the footprint and increase the minimum appearance size, acting as a coarse band-limiting approximation [S9]. It cannot use Mip-Splatting's 2D Mip filter because LinPrim primitives do not rely on modifying the screen space Gaussian distribution [S9].

### Anti-Aliasing via Kernel Adaptation
Adapts the kernel bandwidth to prevent aliasing when the volume is undersampled relative to the screen (low magnification or perspective) [S10, unofficial]. It calculates the Jacobian $J$ of the local perspective distortion for each point and computes the ray-space ellipsoid $G_{JMV}$ using the Jacobian to stretch the kernel appropriately [S10, unofficial]. Swan '97 and Mueller '98 suggest linearly increasing splat kernel size and linearly decreasing splat amplitude [S10, unofficial].

### Integration with Mip-Splatting and Scaffold-GS
Mip-Splatting and Scaffold-GS employ conventional projection of 3D-GS, resulting in larger approximation error compared to the proposed method [S11]. The proposed method can be seamlessly integrated with them to reduce their projection error [S11].

## Operating characteristics
**Time and Memory Complexity:**
- Rendering time is not linearly reduced at very low resolutions because all Gaussians must be splatted even if filtered later, preventing linear speedup [S3].
- At 64x scale, rendering time improves significantly: e.g., 4.9ms vs 103.3ms (3DGS) on the 360 dataset [S3].
- Memory overhead is low, with fewer than 5% additional Gaussians added [S3].

**Measured Performance:**
- On the Blender dataset, Mip-Splatting achieves PSNR of 32.81 at Full Res, 34.49 at 1/2 Res, 35.45 at 1/4 Res, and 35.50 at 1/8 Res [S7, unofficial].
- On the 360 dataset at 64x scale: PSNR 25.35 vs 15.23 (3DGS), LPIPS 0.066 vs 0.256 (3DGS) [S3].
- On Tank and Temples at 64x scale: PSNR 19.67 vs 13.88 (3DGS), LPIPS 0.034 vs 0.447 (3DGS) [S3].
- On Deep Blending at 64x scale: PSNR 25.70 vs 17.75 (3DGS), LPIPS 0.036 vs 0.379 (3DGS) [S3].

**Strengths and Limitations:**
- Achieves 13%-66% PSNR and 160%-2400% rendering speed improvements at 4x-128x scale rendering [S3].
- Eliminates dilation/erosion artifacts when zooming in/out [S6, unofficial].
- Unlike EWA splatting, which limits frequency bandwidth empirically, Mip-Splatting's 3D filter is determined by training data [S7, unofficial].
- Unlike Mip-NeRF, it does not require multi-scale images for supervision [S7, unofficial].

**When to use:**
- Novel View Synthesis (NVS) in virtual reality, cinematography, and robotics, specifically addressing out-of-distribution generalization where training and testing occur at different sampling rates [S7, unofficial].
- When rendering at resolutions or focal lengths significantly different from training data [S1][S7, unofficial].

## Background
MIP-Splatting was introduced by Yu et al. in 2024 (CVPR 2024) [S3][S6, unofficial]. It addresses the aliasing problem in 3D Gaussian Splatting, which arises because 3DGS samples the scene at pixel centers, ignoring the footprint of the pixel itself [S5][S7, unofficial]. Standard 3DGS systematically underestimates the scale parameter of 3D Gaussians during optimization, leading to erosion effects when zooming in because dilated 2D Gaussians become smaller in screen space [S7, unofficial]. Zooming out leads to dilation artifacts because the projected 2D Gaussians reduce in size while the same amount of dilation is applied [S7, unofficial]. The method builds on the principles of multi-scale filtering and anti-aliasing in computer graphics, such as EWA splatting and Mip-NeRF, but applies them to the 3D Gaussian Splatting pipeline [S3][S7, unofficial].
