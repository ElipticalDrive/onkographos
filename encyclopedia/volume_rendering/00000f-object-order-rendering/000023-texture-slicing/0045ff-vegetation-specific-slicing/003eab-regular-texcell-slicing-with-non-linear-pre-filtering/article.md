# Regular Texcell Slicing with Non-Linear Pre-filtering

Also called: Regular texcells, Slices parallel to ground

## Overview
Regular Texcell Slicing is a volume rendering technique that represents volumetric data (specifically forest scenes) as a stack of parallel, ground-aligned planar slices, enabling efficient hardware compositing and non-linear pre-filtering [S1]. It solves the problem of rendering large-scale volumetric textures with high performance and correct anti-aliasing by constructing a MIP-map pyramid of 2D textures, where coarser levels are generated by blending finer levels using the volume rendering transparency equation [S1]. Unlike view-aligned or silhouette-specific methods, regular texcells maintain slices parallel to the ground plane, which avoids CPU-side polygon generation for the bulk of the scene but requires specific opacity adjustments at grazing angles to prevent sampling artifacts [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** A 3D volume geometry (e.g., polygonal forest) and a camera view.
*   **Output:** A set of alpha-premultiplied 2D textures organized in a MIP-map pyramid, rendered as textured quads.

**Data Structures:**
The method uses a Level of Detail (LOD) pyramid consisting of levels $S_i$ for $i = 0 \dots N$.
*   **Level $S_N$ (Finest):** Contains $n = 2^N$ slices. Each slice is a 2D texture of size $L \times L$.
*   **Level $S_i$ (Coarser):** Contains $2^i$ slices. Each slice is a 2D texture of size $L \cdot 2^{N-i} \times L \cdot 2^{N-i}$.
*   **Texture Format:** Alpha-premultiplied color ($\bar{C}$). The RGB channels store $C \cdot A$, where $C$ is the color and $A$ is the opacity [S1].
*   **MIP-mapping:** Each 2D texture in every level is MIP-mapped to support anisotropic filtering in the slice plane [S1].

**Algorithm Steps:**

1.  **Generate Finest Level ($S_N$):**
    Render the original 3D volume geometry using an orthographic camera looking down (parallel to the ground). For each of the $n = 2^N$ slices, set the near and far clipping planes at distance $\delta/2$ above and below the slice's center location, where $\delta$ is the uniform spacing between slices. This captures the volume data at the highest resolution [S1].

2.  **Generate Coarser Levels ($S_i$ for $i < N$):**
    Construct levels from finest to coarsest. For each level $S_i$, generate its slices by blending pairs of adjacent slices from the finer level $S_{i+1}$. The $j$-th slice of level $S_i$ is computed by blending slice $2j$ and slice $2j+1$ from level $S_{i+1}$ using the standard volume rendering transparency equation for alpha-premultiplied colors:
    $$ \bar{C}_{result} = \bar{C}_{front} + (1 - A_{front}) \cdot \bar{C}_{back} $$
    where $\bar{C}_{front}$ and $A_{front}$ are the color and opacity of the front slice (closer to the viewer), and $\bar{C}_{back}$ is the color of the back slice. After blending, MIP-map the resulting 2D texture [S1].

3.  **Rendering with Opacity Adjustment:**
    Render the slices in back-to-front order. At grazing view angles, the distance between slices along the view ray increases, causing coarse sampling and potential parallax errors (visible gaps). To correct the total opacity, the opacity of each slice must be scaled by a vertex factor $\pi_A$ [S1]:
    $$ \pi_A = sz \cdot \frac{\vec{N} \cdot \vec{z}}{|\vec{N} \cdot \vec{V}|} $$
    where:
    *   $sz$ is the vertical scaling factor (ratio of mapped forest height to original sample height).
    *   $\vec{N}$ is the terrain normal.
    *   $\vec{z}$ is the vertical unit vector $(0, 1, 0)$.
    *   $\vec{V}$ is the view direction vector.
    
    The factor $\pi_A$ is interpolated across the slice geometry and modulates the texture's opacity during rasterization. The adjusted opacity $A(dl)$ for a path length $dl$ is approximated as:
    $$ A(dl) \approx \frac{dl}{\delta} A(\delta) $$
    Values greater than 1 are clamped to 1 [S1].

4.  **Aperiodic Tiling:**
    To avoid texture repetitiveness, use at least 8 distinct edge-matching patterns for the texcells. These patterns may contain precomputed shading and shadows, which constrains them from being rotated [S1].

```python
def generate_texcell_pyramid(volume_geometry, num_levels_log2, slice_size):
    """
    Generates the LOD pyramid for regular texcell slicing.
    """
    N = num_levels_log2
    n = 2 ** N
    delta = calculate_slice_spacing(volume_geometry)
    
    # Step 1: Generate finest level SN
    # Render orthographic slices looking down
    finest_slices = []
    for j in range(n):
        slice_y = j * delta
        near_clip = slice_y - delta / 2
        far_clip = slice_y + delta / 2
        
        # Render volume with clipping planes to get 2D texture
        texture = render_orthographic_slice(volume_geometry, near_clip, far_clip, slice_size)
        # Ensure alpha-premultiplied
        texture = premultiply_alpha(texture)
        finest_slices.append(texture)
    
    pyramid = [finest_slices]
    
    # Step 2: Generate coarser levels Si
    for i in range(N - 1, -1, -1):
        current_level_slices = []
        next_level_slices = pyramid[-1]
        num_slices_in_level = 2 ** i
        
        for j in range(num_slices_in_level):
            # Blend pair from finer level
            front_tex = next_level_slices[2 * j]
            back_tex = next_level_slices[2 * j + 1]
            
            # Transparency equation for alpha-premultiplied colors
            # C_bar_result = C_bar_front + (1 - A_front) * C_bar_back
            result_color = front_tex.color + (1.0 - front_tex.opacity) * back_tex.color
            result_opacity = front_tex.opacity + back_tex.opacity * (1.0 - front_tex.opacity)
            
            # Create new texture with blended data
            blended_tex = create_texture(result_color, result_opacity, slice_size * (2 ** (N - i)))
            
            # MIP-map the resulting texture
            mipmap_tex = generate_mipmaps(blended_tex)
            current_level_slices.append(mipmap_tex)
        
        pyramid.append(current_level_slices)
    
    return pyramid

def render_texcell_slice(slice_geometry, texture, view_direction, terrain_normal, vertical_scaling):
    """
    Renders a single texcell slice with opacity adjustment.
    """
    # Calculate opacity multiply factor pi_A
    # pi_A = sz * (N . z) / |N . V|
    dot_N_z = dot(terrain_normal, vertical_vector)
    dot_N_V = dot(terrain_normal, view_direction)
    
    if abs(dot_N_V) < epsilon:
        # Avoid division by zero for parallel view/normal
        opacity_factor = 1.0
    else:
        opacity_factor = vertical_scaling * dot_N_z / abs(dot_N_V)
    
    # Clamp opacity factor if necessary (though usually < 1 for grazing, 
    # the approximation A(dl) ~ (dl/delta)A(delta) implies scaling)
    # Note: The source says values > 1 are clamped in the opacity calculation context.
    opacity_factor = min(opacity_factor, 1.0)
    
    # Render with modulate mode for opacity
    render_with_modulated_opacity(slice_geometry, texture, opacity_factor)
```

**Parameters:**
*   **$N$:** Logarithm of the number of slices in the finest level. Typical values depend on volume height and desired resolution.
*   **$L$:** Base texture resolution (e.g., $256 \times 256$).
*   **Number of patterns:** At least 8 patterns are required for aperiodic tiling [S1].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Grazing Angles:** At grazing angles, the distance between slices along the ray ($\delta_v$) increases significantly. Without opacity adjustment, the same number of slices are traversed, leading to incorrect (too low) total opacity and visible gaps (parallax error) [S1].
*   **3D Texture MIP-mapping:** The method avoids 3D texture MIP-mapping for regular texcells because isotropic filtering blurs volume data in the slicing direction at grazing angles, and 3D texture implementations historically had bugs [S1].
*   **Alpha Pre-multiplication:** Textures must be alpha-premultiplied to avoid interpolation artifacts during blending [S1].

## Subtopics

### Silhouette Texcell Slicing
A variant for landscape silhouettes where slices are nearly facing the viewer. It uses a tilted base triangle offset to generate slices on the GPU, avoiding CPU polygon generation. The base triangle is adjusted to face the camera by calculating offsets $k_i = -\frac{\overline{GP_i} \cdot \overline{V}}{\overline{z} \cdot \overline{V}}$, where $G$ is the triangle center and $V$ is the view direction. Slices are generated by offsetting the adjusted base triangle. It uses 3D textures where MIP-map levels are adapted to the slicing rate to mitigate blurring, though 3D texture MIP-mapping is noted to have bugs [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Pre-processing is $O(n \cdot L^2)$ for generating the finest level and $O(n \cdot L^2)$ for generating the pyramid (blending is linear in texture size). Rendering is $O(S)$ where $S$ is the number of slices drawn, with constant-time per-slice compositing.
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(n \cdot L^2)$ for the finest level, plus geometric series for MIP-maps. Total memory is roughly $1.33 \times$ the finest level size.
*   **Performance:** High performance for non-grazing angles due to efficient 2D texture hardware support and anisotropic filtering. Avoids CPU-GPU bus transfer for slice geometry generation [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Efficient LOD management via 2D texture MIP-maps; correct anti-aliasing in the slice plane; no CPU polygon generation for regular slices.
*   **Limitations:** Poor quality at grazing angles (silhouettes) due to parallax error and coarse sampling; requires opacity adjustment; 3D texture MIP-mapping is unreliable [S1].
*   **When to use:** Large-scale forest rendering with moderate to high viewing angles.
*   **When not to use:** Scenes with extreme grazing angles (use Silhouette Texcell Slicing instead) or when 3D texture support is unstable [S1].

## Background
The method was introduced in "Rendering Forest Scenes in Real-Time" (2004) by Magnenat et al. It addresses the limitations of classical volume rendering and 3D texture approaches for real-time forest visualization, specifically targeting the need for high performance with thousands of trees and rich attributes [S1]. The term "texcell" is derived from "texture cell" to distinguish it from "texel" (texture pixel) [S1].
