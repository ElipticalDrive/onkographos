# Frequency Domain Volume Rendering

Also called: Frequency Domain Volume Rendering (FVR) on GPU, GPU FVR, GPU-based Frequency Domain Volume Rendering, Hardware-Accelerated FVR

## Overview
Frequency Domain Volume Rendering (FVR) is a volume rendering technique that transforms scalar data from the spatial domain to the frequency domain, extracts a 2D projection slice, and inverse-transforms the result to the spatial domain, thereby avoiding the $O(N^3)$ computational complexity of spatial projection methods [S1, S3, unofficial]. It is distinguished from its siblings by its reliance on the Projection-Slice Theorem to decouple the rendering step from the dataset resolution, allowing the rendering performance to depend primarily on the output image resolution rather than the input volume size [S2, S3, unofficial].

## How it works
The method follows the three-stage pipeline of domain-transform methods: Global Transformation, Slice Extraction, and Inverse Transformation [S1, unofficial].

**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** A 3D scalar volume $f(x, y, z)$ of size $N_x \times N_y \times N_z$.
*   **Output:** A 2D projection image of size $M_x \times M_y$.

**Data Structures:**
1.  **Frequency Volume:** A 3D texture storing complex numbers. In GPU implementations, this is typically a two-channel 32-bit floating-point 3D texture (real and imaginary parts) [S1, unofficial]. If the input dimensions are not powers of two, zero-padding is applied in the frequency domain to meet hardware texture dimension constraints [S1, unofficial].
2.  **Kernel Texture:** A 1D floating-point RGBA texture storing the discretized filter kernel [S3, unofficial]. For a kernel of width four, each channel stores one tile of the kernel. A single texture fetch at position $X$ returns four weight values [S3, unofficial].
3.  **Scramble Lookup Texture:** A texture containing pre-computed bit-reversal indices for the FFT scrambling phase [S1, unofficial].
4.  **Roots of Unity Texture:** A texture storing the principal $N$-th roots of unity required for butterfly passes [S1, unofficial].
5.  **Ping-Pong Buffers:** Two 2D textures used as source and destination buffers for iterative passes (scrambling and butterfly) [S1, unofficial].

**Parameters:**
*   **Interpolation Scheme:** Nearest Neighbor (NN), Tri-linear (TL), Tri-cubic (TC), or Windowed Sinc [S2, unofficial].
*   **Window Function:** For Windowed Sinc, a Blackman window of width four is used [S2, unofficial].
*   **Kernel Resolution:** The discretized kernel has a resolution of 64 samples per filter tile [S2, unofficial].
*   **Samples per Tile:** For cubic B-spline, the number of samples per filter tile can be 1, 4, or 16, influencing image quality [S2, unofficial].
*   **Projection Slice Resolution:** Typically $256 \times 256$ or $512 \times 512$ [S2, unofficial].

**Steps:**

1.  **Pre-processing (CPU):**
    1.  Rearrange spatial data to move the origin from the corner $[0,0,0]$ to the center (wrap-around operation) [S1, unofficial].
    2.  Perform a 3D Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) on the rearranged data [S1, unofficial].
    3.  Rearrange the transformed data to center low frequencies [S1, unofficial].
    4.  Upload the complex data to the GPU as a 3D texture [S1, unofficial].
    *   *Optional:* Suppress vignetting by spatially premultiplying the original volume with the reciprocal of the interpolation filter's frequency response [S2, unofficial].

2.  **Slicing Setup (GPU):**
    1.  Map the 3D frequency texture onto a proxy geometry (a single quad) stretched over the rendering target [S1, unofficial].
    2.  Set texture coordinates to cover the entire 3D texture [S1, unofficial].
    3.  Rotate the texture around the frequency volume origin to define the viewing direction [S1, unofficial]. The slice is perpendicular to the viewing direction and intersects the origin of the frequency spectrum [S1, unofficial].

3.  **Slice Extraction (Resampling):**
    1.  For each fragment (pixel) in the rendering target, determine the corresponding 3D coordinate in the frequency texture based on the rotated texture coordinates [S1, unofficial].
    2.  Estimate blending factors by multiplying texture coordinates with the original texture resolution; the blending factors are the fractional parts of these new coordinates [S3, unofficial].
    3.  Fetch neighboring voxels from the 3D texture using the chosen interpolation scheme (NN, TL, TC, or Windowed Sinc) [S2, unofficial].
    4.  Compute the weighted sum of the fetched voxels to produce the 2D slice value [S2, unofficial].
    *   *Note:* Higher-order filters (TC, Windowed Sinc) require more surrounding voxels (e.g., 64 for a $5 \times 5 \times 5$ filter) compared to Tri-linear (8 voxels) [S4, unofficial].

4.  **Inverse Transformation (GPU):**
    1.  Perform a 2D Inverse Fast Fourier Transform (IFFT) on the extracted 2D slice [S1, unofficial].
    2.  Split the 2D IFFT into two passes: rows then columns [S1, unofficial].
    3.  **Scrambling Phase:** Swap samples at position $x$ and $y$, where $y$ is the bit-reversal of $x$. Use the scramble lookup texture for efficient swapping [S1, unofficial].
    4.  **Butterfly Passes:** Perform $\log_2(N)$ butterfly passes, where $N$ is the number of rows or columns [S1, unofficial].
        *   Use ping-pong buffer swapping between source and destination textures [S1, unofficial].
        *   Fetch addresses of two contributing samples ($a, b$) and the principal $N$-th root of unity ($w$) from lookup textures [S1, unofficial].
        *   Perform complex multiplication and two additions: $a' = a + w \cdot b$, $b' = a - w \cdot b$ [S1, unofficial].
    5.  Scale the final output by $N^{-1}$ (where $N$ is the total number of points in the 2D transform) [S1, unofficial].

**Pseudocode:**

```python
def frequency_domain_volume_rendering(volume_3d, viewing_direction, output_size):
    # Step 1: Pre-processing (CPU)
    volume_centered = rearrange_origin_to_center(volume_3d)
    freq_volume_3d = fft3d(volume_centered)
    freq_volume_centered = fftshift(freq_volume_3d)
    gpu_freq_texture = upload_to_gpu(freq_volume_centered)

    # Step 2 & 3: Slicing (GPU Shader)
    slice_2d = resample_slice(gpu_freq_texture, viewing_direction, output_size)

    # Step 4: Inverse 2D FFT (GPU Shader)
    # Transform rows
    slice_rows_transformed = ifft1d_rows(slice_2d)
    # Transform columns
    slice_cols_transformed = ifft1d_cols(slice_rows_transformed)

    # Scale by N^-1
    final_image = slice_cols_transformed / (output_size[0] * output_size[1])
    return final_image

def ifft1d_rows(data_2d):
    for i in range(data_2d.shape[0]):
        data_2d[i, :] = _ifft1d_pass(data_2d[i, :], data_2d.shape[1])
    return data_2d

def ifft1d_cols(data_2d):
    for i in range(data_2d.shape[1]):
        data_2d[:, i] = _ifft1d_pass(data_2d[:, i], data_2d.shape[0])
    return data_2d

def _ifft1d_pass(arr, N):
    # Scrambling phase
    scrambled = bit_reverse_scramble(arr)
    # Butterfly passes
    buffer_a = scrambled
    buffer_b = [0.0] * N
    num_passes = int(math.log2(N))
    for i in range(num_passes):
        w = get_root_of_unity(i, N)
        for j in range(N):
            idx_a, idx_b = get_butterfly_indices(j, i, N)
            val_a = buffer_a[idx_a]
            val_b = buffer_a[idx_b]
            prod = complex_multiply(w, val_b)
            buffer_b[j] = complex_add(val_a, prod)
            buffer_b[(j + N // 2) % N] = complex_sub(val_a, prod)
        buffer_a, buffer_b = buffer_b, buffer_a
    return buffer_a
```

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Aliasing:** The rendering target resolution must meet the Nyquist criterion. Low resampling quality introduces ghosts or non-existent frequencies [S1, S2, unofficial].
*   **Ghosting/Copy Artifacts:** Caused by the absence of zero-padding in the spatial domain, resulting in visible copies of the dataset [S2, unofficial].
*   **Vignetting Artifact:** Central values of the final image are overemphasized due to the frequency response of the interpolation filter. Mitigated by spatial premultiplication with the reciprocal of the filter's frequency response [S2, unofficial].
*   **Overlapping Artifacts:** Occur if the projection slice resolution is insufficient for the dataset resolution (e.g., a $256^3$ dataset requires at least a $512 \times 512$ slice) [S2, unofficial].
*   **Hardware Limitations:** NVIDIA GPUs do not natively support floating-point 3D textures, requiring storage in four-channel fixed-point textures and additional conversion before interpolation [S1, unofficial].

## Subtopics

### [Frequency Domain Shear-Warp Volume Rendering](955-frequency-domain-shear-warp-volume-rendering.md)
Frequency Domain Shear-Warp Volume Rendering is a technique that performs the shear transformation of the shear-warp factorization in the frequency domain using the shifting theorem, allowing for arbitrary, view-independent sampling distances along viewing rays via zero-padding interpolation. Unlike standard shear-warp algorithms where sampling distance is coarse and view-dependent, this method decouples sampling density from the viewing direction by resampling in the frequency domain, thereby avoiding the large spatial zero-padding required by spatial-domain shear methods.

### Hardware FVR Engine Architecture
A hardware realization of the FVR algorithm consisting of a memory block, a resampling block, and an Inverse FHT/FFT block [S4, unofficial]. For a $256^3$ dataset and 25 frames/sec frame rate, one interpolation step for a $512 \times 512$ plane must be done within approximately 150 ns [S4, unofficial]. This timing allows for a single interpolation unit and memory addressing logic without parallelization [S4, unofficial]. A $5 \times 5 \times 5$ filter (like tri-cubic spline or POCS) requires 64 surrounding voxels per sample point, which can be handled using a lookup table (e.g., EPROM) for precomputed function values [S4, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** The global transformation is $O(N^3 \log N)$ and performed once offline. The rendering step is $O(M^2 \log M)$, where $M$ is the projection slice resolution [S1, unofficial].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storage for the 3D frequency volume ($O(N^3)$) and intermediate buffers for the 2D IFFT [S1, unofficial].
*   **Performance:** Rendering performance depends on the resolution of the projection slice, not explicitly on the dataset resolution [S2, unofficial]. Dataset resolution affects texture cache efficiency; higher resolution leads to more cache misses, causing slight performance differences (± 2 fps for a $512 \times 512$ slice) [S2, unofficial].
*   **Measured Performance:** On an ATI Radeon 9800 XT, at $256 \times 256$ resolution: NN=400 fps, TL=1050 fps, TC=180 fps, IFFT 2D=153 fps. At $512 \times 512$ resolution: NN=500 fps, TL=350 fps, TC=45 fps, IFFT 2D=35 fps [S2, unofficial]. The GPU implementation achieves a speed-up factor of approximately 17 compared to a CPU-based approach (17 fps on AMD Athlon XP 2200+) [S2, unofficial].
*   **Strengths:** High performance for static views; decouples rendering speed from dataset size.
*   **Limitations:** Limited interactivity for display parameters other than viewing direction; requires significant preprocessing; sensitive to interpolation quality and resolution [S1, S2, unofficial].
*   **When to use:** When rendering large static volumes with high performance requirements and fixed viewing parameters [S1, unofficial].
*   **When not to use:** When interactive modification of opacity, lighting, or other transfer function parameters is required [S1, unofficial].

## Background
Frequency Domain Volume Rendering was introduced by Ivan Viola, Armin Kanitsar, and Eduard Gröller in 2004 [S3, unofficial]. The method leverages the Projection-Slice Theorem to accelerate volume rendering by moving the computationally expensive projection step into the frequency domain, where it becomes a simple slice extraction [S1, unofficial]. The GPU-based implementation was recognized with the second-best paper award at SCCG 2004 [S3, unofficial].
