# Anisotropic Noise

Also called: Goldberg et al. noise, Oriented Band Filtering, Oriented subband noise

## Overview
Anisotropic noise is a procedural noise method that supports high-quality anisotropic filtering by tiling the frequency domain into oriented subbands [S1]. It solves the problem of aliasing artifacts and loss of detail inherent in isotropic filtering on tilted or distorted surfaces [S1]. Unlike isotropic methods that apply a uniform blur, Anisotropic Noise adapts noise content to non-uniform perspective distortions by weighting oriented subbands according to the local filter geometry [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs:**
- A set of pre-computed oriented noise subband textures.
- The local surface geometry (e.g., texture derivatives or Jacobian) to determine the anisotropic filter shape and orientation [S1].

**Outputs:**
- A scalar noise value for each sample point.

**Data Structures:**
- **Subband Textures:** Each oriented subband is stored as a 32-bit RGBA image [S1].
- **Channel Packing:** Four orientations are packed into one texture (e.g., Red, Green, Blue, Alpha channels) [S1].
- **Scale:** Subbands are precomputed at a single scale only [S1].

**Steps:**

1.  **Preprocessing (Offline):**
    1.  Partition the frequency domain using steerable filters to create oriented subbands that are tightly localized in scale and orientation [S1].
    2.  Synthesize noise tiles for each subband [S1].
    3.  Pack each oriented subband image into one channel of a 32-bit RGBA image, yielding four orientations per texture [S1].
    4.  Repeat for additional bands if necessary (typically 4 or 8 bands total, using 1 or 2 textures) [S1].

2.  **Runtime (Per-Pixel/Sample):**
    1.  Determine the local anisotropic filter parameters (orientation and scale) based on the surface geometry [S1].
    2.  Sample the pre-computed subband textures at the current location. Since subbands are precomputed at a single scale, scale variations are handled by simply scaling the pre-computed textures (linear interpolation) [S1].
    3.  Compute the appropriate weighted combinations of the oriented subbands. The weights are adapted with respect to the anisotropic filter to approximate the desired frequency spectrum on the surface [S1].
    4.  The final noise value is the weighted sum of the noise subbands at each pixel [S1].

**Formulas:**
Let $N_i(\mathbf{x})$ be the value of the $i$-th oriented subband at position $\mathbf{x}$.
Let $w_i$ be the weight for the $i$-th subband, determined by the anisotropic filter's orientation and scale [S1].
The final noise value $N(\mathbf{x})$ is:
$$ N(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{i} w_i N_i(\mathbf{x}) $$
The weights $w_i$ are computed to approximate the desired frequency spectrum, leveraging the steerable property of the filters which allows linear interpolation of filter profiles to generate intermediate orientations [S1].

**Parameters:**
- **Number of Bands:** Typically 4 or 8 bands (1 or 2 textures) [S1].
- **Scale:** Precomputed at a single scale; other scales are generated on the fly by scaling the textures [S1].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
- **Precision:** The method relies on linear interpolation of filter profiles to avoid interpolation artifacts [S1].
- **Boundaries:** The method assumes the ability to sample the pre-computed textures at arbitrary scales via scaling [S1].

## Subtopics

### [Fourier Spectral Synthesis](16451-fourier-spectral-synthesis.md)
Fourier Spectral Synthesis generates noise with a specific power spectrum by filtering white noise in the frequency domain. It is distinguished from spatial-domain noise methods by its direct control over the spectral density, making it ideal for generating random fractals and simulating natural phenomena with precise statistical properties.

### [Gabor Noise Surface Filtering](16556-gabor-noise-surface-filtering.md)
Gabor Noise Surface Filtering is an analytical method for applying spatial filters to Gabor noise by modifying the parameters of its constituent frequency-domain kernels. Unlike methods that discretize the spectrum into distinct bands and approximate filtering through weight adjustments, this approach exploits the mathematical closure of Gaussians under multiplication to compute the exact filtered kernel parameters directly. It sets itself apart from [Fourier Spectral Synthesis](16451-fourier-spectral-synthesis.md) by operating on a sum of localized Gabor kernels rather than a global spectral distribution, allowing for efficient, analytical anisotropic filtering without spectral aliasing or discretization artifacts.

## Operating characteristics
- **Time Complexity:** Runtime is efficient as it involves a weighted sum of texture samples [S1].
- **Memory Complexity:** Storage is determined by the number of subbands and textures. Typically 4 or 8 bands (1 or 2 textures) provide a good trade-off [S1].
- **Performance:** High-quality anisotropic filtering with reduced aliasing on tilted surfaces compared to isotropic methods [S1].
- **Strengths:** Supports high-quality anisotropic filtering; adapts to non-uniform perspective distortions; avoids interpolation artifacts through steerable filters [S1].
- **Limitations:** Requires pre-computation of subbands; limited to the orientations defined by the steerable filter set [S1].
- **When to use:** When high-quality anisotropic filtering is required on surfaces with significant perspective distortion or parametric distortion [S1].
- **When not to use:** When isotropic filtering is sufficient or when memory bandwidth is extremely constrained and pre-computed textures are prohibitive [S1].

## Background
Anisotropic noise was introduced by Goldberg et al. in 2008 [S1]. It was developed to address the limitations of existing noise functions which only supported isotropic filtering, leading to a tradeoff between aliasing artifacts and loss of detail [S1]. The method is based on steerable filters [SF95, PS00] that partition the frequency domain into oriented subbands [S1]. It has been used for surface noise via 2D texture mapping and for anisotropic analytic filtering [S1].
