# Absorbing Particle Rendering

Also called: Direct Volume Rendering (Absorption Only)

## Overview
Absorbing Particle Rendering simulates a participating medium composed of perfectly black particles that absorb all intercepted light without scattering or emitting [S3]. It solves for light intensity attenuation through a medium, resembling a cloud of black smoke or traditional X-ray imagery [S1, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs:**
- Initial light intensity $I_0$ at the entry point of the volume [S3].
- Extinction coefficient $k$ of the medium [S3].
- Distance $x$ traveled through the medium [S3].

**Outputs:**
- Transmitted light intensity $I(x)$ at the exit point [S3].

**Data Structures:**
The method relies on scalar parameters derived from the medium's physical properties:
- $A$: Area of each particle [S3].
- $N$: Number of particles per unit volume (number density) [S3].
- $k$: Extinction coefficient [S3].
- $\alpha$: Opacity, where transparency is $1 - \alpha$ [S3].

**Formulas:**
The change in light intensity $I$ over a differential distance $dx$ is governed by the differential equation:
$$ \frac{dI}{dx} = -k I $$
where $k$ is the extinction coefficient [S3].

The solution for intensity after traveling distance $x$ is:
$$ I(x) = I_0 e^{-k x} $$
where $I_0$ is the initial light intensity [S3].

The extinction coefficient $k$ is derived from the particle properties. For a unit volume approximated as a small cylindrical slab with base area $A$ and thickness $dx$, the intensity absorbed is determined by the total projected area of particles within that volume. The extinction coefficient is given by:
$$ k = N A $$
where $N$ is the number of particles per unit volume and $A$ is the area of each particle [S3].

**Parameters:**
- $r$: Radius of the particles. Typical values depend on the scene scale; for example, discrete media simulations may use geometric mean radii $\bar{r}_g$ around 800 units in specific benchmark contexts [S2].
- $\eta_i$: The imaginary part of the complex refractive index. Increasing $\eta_i$ reduces the intensity of scattering, but in the pure absorption-only model, particles are assumed perfectly black [S2][S3].

**Steps:**
1. Determine the area $A$ of each particle [S3].
2. Determine the number density $N$ (particles per unit volume) [S3].
3. Compute the extinction coefficient: $k = N \cdot A$ [S3].
4. For a viewing ray traversing a segment of length $x$ through the medium, compute the transmitted intensity: $I(x) = I_0 e^{-k x}$ [S3].
5. If implementing discretely, accumulate opacity $\alpha = 1 - e^{-k x}$ for compositing [S3].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
- **Precision:** For large optical depths ($k x \gg 1$), $I(x)$ approaches 0. Standard floating-point precision is sufficient, but underflow to zero may occur for very dense media [G].
- **Boundary Conditions:** The model assumes a homogeneous medium with constant $k$. If $N$ or $A$ varies spatially, $k$ must be integrated numerically along the ray [G].
- **Degenerate Input:** If $N=0$ or $A=0$, $k=0$ and $I(x) = I_0$ (no attenuation) [G].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
import math

def absorbing_particle_rendering(I0, A, N, x):
    """
    Calculate transmitted light intensity through an absorbing particle medium.
    
    Args:
        I0 (float): Initial light intensity at the start of the volume.
        A (float): Area of each particle.
        N (float): Number of particles per unit volume.
        x (float): Distance traveled through the medium.
        
    Returns:
        float: Transmitted light intensity I(x).
    """
    # Step 3: Calculate extinction coefficient
    k = N * A
    
    # Step 4: Calculate transmitted intensity using Beer-Lambert law
    # I(x) = I0 * exp(-k * x)
    I_x = I0 * math.exp(-k * x)
    
    return I_x
```

## Subtopics

### X-ray simulation via ray integration
Simulates attenuation using similar integration principles but accounts for energy-dependent absorption coefficients rather than discrete particle geometry [G]. Choose when modeling monochromatic or energy-specific X-ray projections. [X-ray simulation via ray integration](1445-x-ray-simulation-via-ray-integration.md)

### Prism Model
Approximates the medium as a continuous distribution of light-absorbing prisms rather than discrete particles, offering different computational trade-offs [G]. Choose when particle boundaries are less critical than continuous volume approximation. [Prism Model](3153-prism-model.md)

### Absorption Attenuation
A general term for the physical phenomenon of light reduction; this method is a specific implementation using discrete particle assumptions [G]. Choose when explicitly modeling particulate media rather than continuous attenuation fields. [Absorption Attenuation](7104-absorption-attenuation.md)

## Operating characteristics
- **Time Complexity:** $O(1)$ per ray segment if $k$ is constant. If $k$ varies, it requires numerical integration along the ray, typically $O(N_{steps})$ where $N_{steps}$ is the number of integration steps [G].
- **Memory Complexity:** $O(1)$ if parameters are precomputed. $O(N)$ if storing explicit particle positions for sparse sampling [G].
- **Performance:** Rendering time is minimal as it involves only exponential calculations. Benchmarks for discrete media show lowest rendering times at resolutions where there is roughly one particle per voxel (e.g., $60 \times 60 \times 60$ for $2 \times 10^5$ particles) [S2].
- **Strengths:** Computationally efficient; physically intuitive for dense, non-scattering media like smoke or X-ray attenuation [G].
- **Limitations:** Cannot represent scattering effects (glory, halos); assumes spherical particles; ignores emission [G].
- **When to use:** Simulating black smoke, fog, or X-ray projections where scattering is negligible [G].
- **When not to use:** When realistic lighting with shadows, caustics, or color bleeding from scattering is required [G].

## Background
The absorption-only model is a foundational concept in Direct Volume Rendering, historically used to simulate media that only block light [S1, unofficial]. It is derived from the Beer-Lambert law applied to discrete particulate media. The model assumes perfectly black particles that absorb light perfectly [S3].
