# Simulated Fluorescence Process (SFP) Renderer
Also called: SFP Renderer, SFP volume rendering algorithm

## Overview
The Simulated Fluorescence Process (SFP) Renderer is a volume rendering algorithm that interprets image intensity as a distribution of fluorescent dye, modeling physical light-matter interaction to render scenes as they would appear under real fluorescence microscopy conditions [S1]. Unlike standard isosurface rendering or methods requiring surface detection and segmentation, SFP operates directly on 3D data by simulating two distinct light transport stages: excitation by a distant light source and subsequent emission propagation to the viewer [S1]. This approach allows for the visualization of internal structures with realistic shadowing and absorption effects without preprocessing [S1].

## How it works
The SFP algorithm simulates the physical process of fluorescence in two sequential stages [S1]. It treats the input volume data as a distribution of fluorescent dye where intensity values determine absorption and emission properties [S1].

**Inputs and Outputs**
*   **Input:** A 3D volume dataset (typically multi-channel microscopy data) where intensity represents dye concentration [S1].
*   **Input:** A light source located at infinity (directional) [S1].
*   **Input:** A virtual table plane below the object [S1].
*   **Input:** Penetration Depth parameter (scalar) [S1].
*   **Output:** A 2D image representing the scene as viewed by an observer [S1].

**Data Structures**
The algorithm processes the volume as a grid of voxels [S1]. Each voxel $v$ at position $\mathbf{p}$ contains intensity values for one or more channels [S1]. Each channel is interpreted as a distinct dye with specific properties: transparency, color, and brightness [S1].

**Parameters**
*   **Penetration Depth ($d_p$):** A scalar parameter that sets the absorption and emission transparencies for all channels simultaneously [S1].
    *   Low values: Objects appear solid with hard shadows; excitation light is absorbed immediately [S1].
    *   High values: Light penetrates deeper, revealing internal structures with softer shadows [S1].

**Algorithm Steps**
1.  **Excitation Stage (Lighting):** The fluorescent distribution is illuminated by a light source located at infinity [S1]. For each voxel in the volume, the excitation intensity is calculated based on its orientation relative to the light source [S1]. Areas facing the light source are more excited because the dye absorbs the excitation light [S1]. Each voxel in the light beam is affected by shadowing from its predecessors along the light ray [S1]. Let $L_{exc}(\mathbf{p})$ be the excitation intensity at position $\mathbf{p}$, determined by integrating the extinction of the excitation light through the volume from the light source to $\mathbf{p}$ [S1]. The transmittance is computed as $T_{exc}(\mathbf{p}) = e^{-\tau_{exc}}$, where $\tau_{exc} = \int_0^{d} \sigma_a \, dt$ is the optical depth along the light ray [G].
2.  **Emission Stage (Rendering):** Light emitted by the object and the table below it propagates towards the viewer [S1]. Each voxel emits light proportional to its excitation intensity $L_{exc}(\mathbf{p})$ and its channel-specific brightness [S1]. The emitted light travels along the view ray towards the camera [S1]. As the light passes through the dye, it gets absorbed [S1]. Objects can be obscured from view by other objects due to shadowing from predecessors along the view ray [S1]. The final pixel color is the accumulated emission from all voxels along the view ray, attenuated by the absorption of the dye [S1]. The transmittance along the view ray is $T_{view}(t) = e^{-\tau_{view}}$, where $\tau_{view} = \int_0^t \sigma_a \, dt'$ [G].

**Pseudocode**
The following pseudocode outlines the core logic. Note that specific integration formulas for optical depth are not provided in the source; standard Beer-Lambert integration is implied by the description of shadowing and absorption [S1].

```python
import math

def render_sfp(volume, light_dir, view_dir, penetration_depth, table_plane):
    """
    Renders a volume using the Simulated Fluorescence Process.
    
    Args:
        volume: 3D list of lists of lists, shape [D, H, W, C]. Each element is a dict with 'intensity', 'brightness', 'channel_id'.
        light_dir: Tuple (x, y, z) unit vector for the light source at infinity.
        view_dir: Tuple (x, y, z) unit vector for the camera view.
        penetration_depth: Scalar controlling absorption/emission transparency.
        table_plane: Dict with 'normal' (x,y,z) and 'distance' (float) defining the plane equation.
        
    Returns:
        2D list of lists, shape [H, W, 3] representing the RGB image.
    """
    D, H, W, C = len(volume), len(volume[0]), len(volume[0][0]), len(volume[0][0][0])
    
    # [G] Map penetration depth to absorption coefficient sigma_a
    # Lower penetration depth -> higher absorption
    sigma_a = 1.0 / penetration_depth
    
    # Initialize excitation map
    excitation_map = [[[0.0 for _ in range(W)] for _ in range(H)] for _ in range(D)]
    
    # Step 1: Excitation Stage
    # Ray march along light_dir for each voxel to determine shadowing
    for z in range(D):
        for y in range(H):
            for x in range(W):
                optical_depth = 0.0
                t = 0.0
                step_size = 1.0
                # March backwards from voxel to light source
                while t < max(D, H, W) * step_size:
                    pos_x = x - t * light_dir[0]
                    pos_y = y - t * light_dir[1]
                    pos_z = z - t * light_dir[2]
                    if not (0 <= pos_z < D and 0 <= pos_y < H and 0 <= pos_x < W):
                        break
                    voxel = volume[int(pos_z)][int(pos_y)][int(pos_x)]
                    optical_depth += sigma_a * voxel['intensity'] * step_size
                    t += step_size
                transmittance = math.exp(-optical_depth)
                excitation_map[z][y][x] = transmittance * volume[z][y][x]['intensity']

    # Step 2: Emission Stage
    image = [[[0.0, 0.0, 0.0] for _ in range(W)] for _ in range(H)]
    
    for v in range(H):
        for u in range(W):
            accumulated_radiance = 0.0
            current_transmittance = 1.0
            t = 0.0
            step_size = 1.0
            # March from near to far along view ray
            while t < max(D, H, W) * step_size:
                pos_x = u + t * view_dir[0]
                pos_y = v + t * view_dir[1]
                pos_z = 0 + t * view_dir[2] # Assuming camera at z=0 looking along z
                if not (0 <= pos_z < D and 0 <= pos_y < H and 0 <= pos_x < W):
                    break
                
                voxel = volume[int(pos_z)][int(pos_y)][int(pos_x)]
                
                # Emitted light from this voxel
                emitted_light = excitation_map[int(pos_z)][int(pos_y)][int(pos_x)] * voxel['brightness']
                
                # Add contribution to pixel, attenuated by remaining path
                accumulated_radiance += emitted_light * current_transmittance
                
                # Update transmittance for subsequent voxels (absorption of emission light)
                current_transmittance *= math.exp(-sigma_a * voxel['intensity'] * step_size)
                
                # Check for table intersection to add table shadow/emission if applicable
                # [S1] "Light emitted by the object and a table below it propagates towards the viewer"
                if intersects_table(pos_x, pos_y, pos_z, table_plane):
                    table_emission = compute_table_emission(pos_x, pos_y, pos_z, table_plane)
                    accumulated_radiance += table_emission * current_transmittance
                    
                t += step_size
            image[v][u] = [accumulated_radiance, accumulated_radiance, accumulated_radiance] # Simplified color mapping
            
    return image

def intersects_table(x, y, z, plane):
    # [G] Standard plane intersection test
    normal = plane['normal']
    dist = plane['distance']
    return abs(x * normal[0] + y * normal[1] + z * normal[2] - dist) < 0.1

def compute_table_emission(x, y, z, plane):
    # [G] Table emits constant light if visible
    return 1.0
```

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls**
*   **No Surface Detection Required:** The algorithm does not require surface detection, sharp gradients, or segmentation, making it suitable for direct visualization of 3D microscopy data [S1].
*   **Multi-Channel Interpretation:** Each channel of a multi-channel image is interpreted as a dye with different properties (transparencies, color, brightness) [S1]. Incorrect channel mapping may lead to unrealistic colors [S1].
*   **Penetration Depth Sensitivity:** Low penetration depth results in solid objects with hard shadows, potentially hiding internal details [S1]. High penetration depth reveals internal structures but may reduce contrast [S1].

## Subtopics

### SFP Isosurface Integration
An isosurface added to the SFP scene that acts as a completely impenetrable barrier for both excitation and emission light [S1]. The isosurface casts hard shadows on the SFP material beneath it and on the table [S1]. It is partially obscured by SFP material located in front of it [S1]. The isosurface can also cast shadows on itself, allowing for complex lighting interactions even without fluorescent material [S1]. This feature creates a greater sense of space and depth compared to standard surface rendering [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Performance:** The performance of the SFP Renderer greatly benefits from GPU acceleration [S1].
*   **Strengths:**
    *   Does not require surface detection, sharp gradients, or segmentation [S1].
    *   Suitable for direct visualization of 3D microscopy data [S1].
    *   Provides realistic lighting and shadowing effects (excitation and emission) [S1].
    *   Supports multi-channel data interpretation [S1].
    *   Supports cutting planes (up to 6) for internal visualization [S1].
*   **Limitations:**
    *   Relies on the assumption that image intensity maps to fluorescent dye distribution [S1].
    *   Specific performance metrics (FPS, memory usage) are not provided in the sources [S1].
*   **When to use:** Direct visualization of 3D microscopy data where realistic fluorescence appearance is desired without preprocessing [S1].

## Background
The SFP Renderer is part of the Huygens software suite [S1]. It was introduced in Huygens 19.10, which added features like cutting planes [S1]. The algorithm is designed for use with data acquired from microscopes, such as the Leica Digital Light Sheet microscope, and is often used in conjunction with deconvolution algorithms like CMLE [S1]. It is used in biological research, as evidenced by testimonials from institutions like Maastruth University and EMBL Heidelberg [S1].
