# Bidirectional Reflectance Distribution Function (BRDF)
Also called: Reflectance equation
## Overview
The Bidirectional Reflectance Distribution Function (BRDF) is a 4D function that describes how light is reflected at an opaque surface, serving as the core component of the surface rendering equation. It defines the ratio of reflected radiance in a specific outgoing direction to the incident irradiance from a specific incoming direction, thereby modeling the angular distribution of reflected light. It is distinguished from its sibling [Bidirectional Transmittance Distribution Function (BTDF)](52-btdf.md) by modeling reflection rather than transmission, and from [Bidirectional Scattering Surface Reflectance Distribution Function (BSSRDF)](52-bssrdf.md) by assuming light does not penetrate the surface volume.
## Shared principles
The BRDF, denoted as $f_r(\vec{\omega}_i \rightarrow \vec{\omega}_r)$, is defined as the ratio of the differential reflected radiance $dL_r(\vec{\omega}_r)$ to the differential incident irradiance $dE(\vec{\omega}_i)$ [S1, unofficial].

$$f_r(\vec{\omega}_i \rightarrow \vec{\omega}_r) = \frac{dL_r(\vec{\omega}_r)}{dE(\vec{\omega}_i)}$$

The incident irradiance $dE(\vec{\omega}_i)$ from a direction $\vec{\omega}_i$ is related to the incident radiance $L_i(\vec{\omega}_i)$ by the projected solid angle:

$$dE(\vec{\omega}_i) = L_i(\vec{\omega}_i) \cos \theta_i d\omega_i$$

where $\theta_i$ is the angle between the incident direction $\vec{\omega}_i$ and the surface normal, and $d\omega_i$ is the differential solid angle [S1, unofficial]. Substituting this into the definition yields:

$$f_r(\vec{\omega}_i \rightarrow \vec{\omega}_r) = \frac{L_r(\vec{\omega}_r)}{L_i(\vec{\omega}_i) \cos \theta_i d\omega_i}$$

The total reflected radiance $L_r(\vec{\omega}_r)$ in a specific outgoing direction $\vec{\omega}_r$ is obtained by integrating the product of the BRDF, the incident radiance, and the cosine term over the hemisphere of incident directions $\Omega_i$ [S1, unofficial]. This integral is known as the reflectance equation:

$$L_r(\vec{\omega}_r) = \int_{\Omega_i} f_r(\vec{\omega}_i \rightarrow \vec{\omega}_r) L_i(\vec{\omega}_i) \cos \theta_i d\omega_i$$

The BRDF is a non-negative function. For physically plausible materials, it must satisfy the Helmholtz reciprocity principle, meaning $f_r(\vec{\omega}_i \rightarrow \vec{\omega}_r) = f_r(\vec{\omega}_r \rightarrow \vec{\omega}_i)$, and the energy conservation principle, where the total reflected energy does not exceed the incident energy [G].
## Subtopics

### Microfacet BRDF
Models surfaces as a collection of microscopic facets with specific normal distributions. It separates the surface geometry (Normal Distribution Function) from the microfacet orientation and Fresnel reflection. Suitable for glossy and metallic surfaces. See [Microfacet BRDF](52-microfacet-brdf.md).

### Lobe-based BRDF
Approximates the BRDF as a sum of analytical lobes (e.g., diffuse, specular) with parameters for color and roughness. Efficient for real-time rendering but less physically accurate than microfacet models for complex materials. See [Lobe-based BRDF](52-lobe-brdf.md).

### Measured BRDF
Uses empirical data captured from real materials to define the BRDF. Provides high fidelity for specific objects but lacks generalization and requires large data storage. See [Measured BRDF](52-measured-brdf.md).

## Background
The concept of the BRDF was formalized in the context of computer graphics to provide a rigorous mathematical framework for surface shading. It builds upon the [Surface Rendering Equation](14578-surface-rendering-equation.md), which defines the boundary condition for light transport. The term "reflectance equation" is often used interchangeably with the integral form of the BRDF [S1, unofficial]. The model assumes opaque surfaces where light interaction is confined to the surface interface, distinguishing it from volume-based scattering models.
