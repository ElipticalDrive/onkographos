# Acoustic Volume Rendering


## Overview
Acoustic Volume Rendering (AVR) adapts volume rendering techniques to model acoustic impulse responses (IRs), which are time-series signals, by using frequency-domain rendering and spherical integration to fit IR measurements [S1]. It represents a scene as a field where a network outputs acoustic volume density and a time-varying signal for any point and direction, solving the problem of modeling complex sound interactions (absorption, reflection, scattering) by accumulating signals along a ray [S1]. Unlike light volume rendering, AVR handles time delays as phase shifts in the frequency domain and integrates over a sphere to account for omnidirectional microphone capture, distinguishing it from [Neural Impulse Response Fields](17205-acoustic-volume-rendering-for-neural-impulse-response-fields.md) by explicitly modeling the acoustic wave propagation physics (time delay and energy decay) within the rendering integral [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** Emitter pose $(p_e \in \mathbb{R}^3, \omega_e \in \mathbb{R}^3)$, Listener pose $(p_l \in \mathbb{R}^3, \omega_l \in \mathbb{R}^3)$, and query direction $\omega$.
*   **Outputs:** The synthesized impulse response $h(t)$ (or its discrete version $h[n]$) at the listener's position.

**Data Structures:**
The scene is represented by a neural network $F_\Theta$.
*   **Network Input:** Concatenated embeddings of emitter location $p_e$, emitter orientation $\omega_e$, query point location $p$, and query direction $\omega$.
*   **Network Output:**
    *   Acoustic volume density $\sigma \in \mathbb{R}$.
    *   Discrete time signal $s[n] \in \mathbb{R}^T$, where $T$ is the number of time samples.

**Parameters:**
*   $v$: Velocity of sound. Used in time delay calculation. [S1]
*   $t_v$: Energy decay constant (scalar factor). The factor applied to all signals is $1/t_v$. The traveled distance is $t_v$. [S1]
*   $G(\omega)$: Listener gain pattern. [S1]
*   $N_\theta = 80, N_\phi = 40, N_r = 64$: Sampling numbers for spherical integration and ray marching. [S1]
*   Loss weights: $\lambda_{amp} = 0.5, \lambda_{phase} = 0.5, \lambda_{time} = 100, \lambda_{stft} = 1, \lambda_{energy} = 5$. [S1]

**Method Steps:**

1.  **Frequency Domain Conversion:** Convert the target impulse response $h(t)$ from the time domain to the frequency domain $H(f)$ using the Fourier Transform. This handles time delays as phase shifts and reduces spatial variation for easier network optimization [S1].
    $$ H(f) = \mathcal{F}\{h(t)\} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} h(t)e^{-j2\pi f t} dt $$
    For a direct path from emitter $p_e$ to listener $p_l$ with velocity $v$, the time delay is $\tau = \|p_l - p_e\| / v$, and the frequency domain representation is:
    $$ H(f) = \frac{1}{\|p_l - p_e\|^2} e^{-j2\pi f \tau} $$
    [S1]

2.  **Scene Representation:** Define the neural field $F_\Theta$. Given emitter pose $(p_e, \omega_e)$ and a query point $p$ with direction $\omega$, the network outputs density $\sigma$ and signal $s(t)$ (or $s[n]$) [S1].
    $$ F_\Theta: (p, \omega, p_e, \omega_e) \mapsto (\sigma, s(t)) $$
    [S1]

3.  **Ray Casting and Acoustic Volume Rendering:** For a listener at position $p$ looking in direction $\omega$, cast a ray $p(u) = p + u \cdot \omega$ with near bound $u_n$ and far bound $u_f$. Accumulate signals along the ray using the acoustic volume rendering equation [S1]:
    $$ h_\omega(t) = \frac{1}{t_v} \int_{u_n}^{u_f} L(u) \sigma(p(u)) s\left(t - \frac{u}{v}; p(u), \omega\right) du $$
    where $L(x)$ is the transmittance:
    $$ L(x) = \exp\left( -\int_{u_n}^{x} \sigma(p(x')) dx' \right) $$
    [S1]
    *   **Time Delay:** The signal $s(t)$ is delayed by $u/v$ to account for sound propagation time. [S1]
    *   **Energy Decay:** A factor of $1/t_v$ is applied to all signals along the ray to account for energy decay, assuming a constant traveled distance $t_v$ for signals originating from the impulse at $t=0$. [S1]

4.  **Spherical Integration:** Since microphones capture signals from all directions, integrate the directional signals $h_\omega(t)$ over the sphere $\Omega$ using the listener's gain pattern $G(\omega)$:
    $$ h(t) = \int_{\Omega} G(\omega) h_\omega(t) d\omega $$
    [S1]

5.  **Discretization:** The continuous signal $h(t)$ is sampled at interval $T$ to produce discrete impulse response $h[n] = h(nT)$ for network training and output. [S1]

**Pseudocode:**

```python
import numpy as np

def acoustic_volume_rendering(p_l, omega_l, p_e, omega_e, v, t_v, F_theta, N_theta=80, N_phi=40, N_r=64, T=256):
    """
    Synthesize impulse response h(t) at listener position p_l using Acoustic Volume Rendering.
    
    Args:
        p_l: Listener position (3,)
        omega_l: Listener orientation (3,)
        p_e: Emitter position (3,)
        omega_e: Emitter orientation (3,)
        v: Speed of sound
        t_v: Energy decay constant
        F_theta: Neural network function returning (sigma, s)
        N_theta: Number of theta samples for spherical integration
        N_phi: Number of phi samples for spherical integration
        N_r: Number of samples along the ray
        T: Number of time samples for output signal
        
    Returns:
        h: Discrete impulse response (T,)
    """
    
    # 1. Generate uniform samples on the sphere for spherical integration
    # Using simple spherical coordinates for demonstration
    thetas = np.linspace(0, np.pi, N_theta)
    phis = np.linspace(0, 2 * np.pi, N_phi)
    theta_grid, phi_grid = np.meshgrid(thetas, phis)
    
    # Convert spherical to Cartesian directions
    # Note: Standard physics convention. Adjust if coordinate system differs.
    directions = np.stack([
        np.sin(theta_grid) * np.cos(phi_grid),
        np.sin(theta_grid) * np.sin(phi_grid),
        np.cos(theta_grid)
    ], axis=-1)
    directions = directions.reshape(-1, 3)
    
    # 2. Initialize accumulator for h(t)
    h_accum = np.zeros(T, dtype=np.complex128)
    
    # 3. Spherical Integration
    for i, omega in enumerate(directions):
        # Ray bounds (example: fixed near/far, or scene-dependent)
        u_n = 0.0
        u_f = 10.0 # Not given in sources, placeholder for scene extent
        
        # 4. Ray Marching for Acoustic Volume Rendering
        u_samples = np.linspace(u_n, u_f, N_r)
        p_samples = p_l + u_samples[:, np.newaxis] * omega[np.newaxis, :]
        
        # Query network for density and signal
        # F_theta takes (p, omega, p_e, omega_e)
        # Returns sigma (N_r,) and s (N_r, T)
        sigma, s = F_theta(p_samples, omega, p_e, omega_e)
        
        # Calculate transmittance L(u)
        # L(x) = exp(- integral_{u_n}^{x} sigma(p(x')) dx')
        # Approximate integral using trapezoidal rule or cumulative sum
        # d_u = u_samples[1] - u_samples[0]
        # sigma_cum = np.cumsum(sigma) * d_u
        # L = np.exp(-sigma_cum)
        # Add L[0] = 1.0
        # [G] Standard volume rendering transmittance calculation
        d_u = u_samples[1] - u_samples[0]
        sigma_cum = np.cumsum(sigma) * d_u
        L = np.exp(-sigma_cum)
        L = np.concatenate([[1.0], L]) # L[0] is 1
        
        # Calculate time delays
        delays = u_samples / v
        
        # Apply time shift to signal s(t)
        # s(t - u/v)
        # In discrete domain, this requires interpolation or phase shift in frequency domain
        # The paper uses frequency domain rendering.
        # For simplicity in time-domain pseudocode, we assume s is shifted.
        # [G] Precise time shifting in discrete domain is complex; 
        # the paper advocates for frequency domain rendering to handle this.
        # Here we simulate the effect by shifting indices if possible, 
        # but strictly, the rendering happens in Frequency Domain.
        
        # Frequency Domain Rendering Approach (as per paper Sec 3.3)
        # Convert s[n] to frequency domain S[k]
        S_k = np.fft.fft(s, axis=1) # Shape (N_r, T)
        
        # Apply phase shift for time delay tau = u/v
        # H(f) = ... * e^(-j2pi f tau)
        # Frequency bins
        f = np.fft.fftfreq(T, d=1.0) # Assuming sampling rate 1 for simplicity, or use actual rate
        # Phase shift matrix
        phase_shift = np.exp(-1j * 2 * np.pi * f[np.newaxis, :] * delays[:, np.newaxis])
        
        # Apply phase shift to each sample's spectrum
        S_shifted = S_k * phase_shift
        
        # Convert back to time domain
        s_shifted = np.fft.ifft(S_shifted).real
        
        # Apply energy decay factor 1/t_v
        s_shifted = s_shifted / t_v
        
        # Alpha composition
        # h_omega(t) = integral L(u) sigma(u) s(t - u/v) du
        # Discrete approximation
        # h_dir = sum_{i} L[i] * sigma[i] * s_shifted[i] * d_u
        # [G] Alpha composition in volume rendering is usually:
        # C = sum T_i * alpha_i * c_i
        # where alpha_i = 1 - exp(-sigma_i * d_u)
        # T_i = product_{j<i} exp(-sigma_j * d_u)
        # The paper's formula (4) is a continuous integral approximation.
        # We approximate the integral sum.
        
        alpha = 1.0 - np.exp(-sigma * d_u)
        transmittance = np.exp(-np.cumsum(sigma * d_u))
        transmittance = np.concatenate([[1.0], transmittance[:-1]])
        
        # Contribution of each sample
        contribution = transmittance[:, np.newaxis] * alpha[:, np.newaxis] * s_shifted
        h_dir = np.sum(contribution * d_u, axis=0)
        
        # Accumulate with gain pattern G(omega)
        # [G] G(omega) is not defined in detail, assume omnidirectional G=1 for now
        G_omega = 1.0 
        h_accum += G_omega * h_dir
        
    # Normalize by number of samples for integration approximation
    h = h_accum / (N_theta * N_phi)
    
    return h
```

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Discrete Sampling Limits:** Real-world impulse responses are discrete, limiting time domain sampling. AVR addresses this by applying phase shifts in the frequency domain to account for time delays, bypassing finite time domain sampling limits [S1].
*   **Omnidirectional Capture:** Microphones capture combined signals from all directions, unlike cameras. AVR handles this by casting rays uniformly across a sphere and using spherical integration [S1].
*   **Energy Decay Model:** The model applies a constant factor $1/t_v$ to all signals, assuming a constant traveled distance $t_v$. This is a simplification of complex acoustic decay [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Depends on the number of spherical samples ($N_\theta \times N_\phi$) and ray samples ($N_r$). The paper uses $N_\theta=80, N_\phi=40, N_r=64$ [S1].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Depends on the neural network size (hash grid + 6-layer MLP) and the storage of frequency-domain signals.
*   **Performance:** AVR achieves state-of-the-art performance in synthesizing impulse responses for novel poses, surpassing current leading methods by a substantial margin in both simulated and real-world datasets [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Handles complex sound interactions (absorption, reflection, scattering); supports zero-shot rendering of binaural audio; integrates individual HRTFs at inference time; uses frequency-domain rendering to handle time delays and reduce spatial variation [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Relies on accurate acoustic simulation data (e.g., from AcoustiX) for training; simplifies energy decay with a constant factor; requires spherical integration which can be computationally expensive [S1].
*   **When to Use:** Virtual and augmented reality applications requiring realistic audio synthesis; scenarios where capturing complex acoustic phenomena is essential [S1].
*   **When Not to Use:** Real-time applications with strict latency constraints where spherical integration is too slow; scenes where the constant energy decay model is insufficiently accurate.

## Background
Acoustic Volume Rendering (AVR) was introduced by Zitong Lan, Chenhao Zheng, Zhiwei Zheng, and Mingmin Zhao in the paper "Acoustic Volume Rendering for Neural Impulse Response Fields" (arXiv:2411.06307v1, 2024) [S1]. The work was developed in parallel with AcoustiX, an acoustic simulation platform that generates more physically accurate impulse responses than existing simulators like SoundSpaces 2.0 [S1]. The method addresses challenges in impulse response synthesis, such as time-series signal nature, high spatial variation, and omnidirectional microphone capture, by adapting volume rendering techniques to the frequency domain [S1].
