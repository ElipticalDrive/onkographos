# Analytic Transmittance Integration
Also called: Analytic segment integration, Exact segment integration, Iteration B
## Overview
Analytic Transmittance Integration computes the radiance along a ray with floating-point accuracy by integrating the radiative transfer equation analytically over segments where optical properties (extinction coefficient $\tau$ and emission coefficient $c$) are constant [S1, book]. It sets itself apart from discrete summation methods (like [Discrete Volume Transmittance Integration](3905-discrete-volume-transmittance-integration.md)) by avoiding Riemann sum approximations, which introduce significant error when the optical depth $\tau \Delta s$ is large and transmittance varies significantly between samples [S1, book].
## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
- Input: A sequence of $n$ segments along a ray. For each segment $i \in [0, n-1]$, the inputs are the extinction coefficient $\tau_i$, the emission coefficient $c_i$, and the geometric length $\Delta s_i$. The boundary radiance $B$ at the end of the ray is also required.
- Output: The accumulated radiance $E$ at the camera.

**Data Structures:**
The method assumes access to arrays or variables for $\tau$, $c$, and $\Delta s$ indexed by segment $i$. No complex data structures are required beyond simple scalar accumulators.

**Steps:**
1. Initialize the accumulated radiance $E = 0$.
2. Initialize the accumulated transmittance $T = 1$.
3. Iterate through each segment $i$ from $0$ to $n-1$:
   a. Compute the segment transmittance factor $F = \exp(-\tau_i \Delta s_i)$.
   b. Update the accumulated radiance: $E = E + T \cdot c_i \cdot (1 - F)$.
   c. Update the accumulated transmittance: $T = T \cdot F$.
4. After the loop, add the contribution of the boundary radiance: $E = E + T \cdot B$.

**Derivation:**
The method solves the integral of the single-scattering term over a segment $[s_i, s_{i+1}]$ where $\tau(u) = \tau_i$ and $c(u) = c_i$ are constant. The contribution to radiance is:
$$
\int_{s_i}^{s_{+1}} T(s_i) \tau_i c_i \exp\left(-\int_{s_i}^s \tau_i du\right) ds = T(s_i) c_i (1 - \exp(-\tau_i \Delta s_i))
$$
where $T(s_i)$ is the transmittance accumulated up to the start of segment $i$ [S1, book].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
def analytic_transmittance_integration(tau, c, delta_s, B):
    """
    Compute radiance using analytic segment integration.
    
    Args:
        tau: List of extinction coefficients for each segment.
        c: List of emission coefficients for each segment.
        delta_s: List of geometric lengths for each segment.
        B: Boundary radiance at the end of the ray.
        
    Returns:
        E: Accumulated radiance.
    """
    E = 0.0
    T = 1.0
    n = len(tau)
    
    for i in range(n):
        # Compute transmittance over the current segment
        F = math.exp(-tau[i] * delta_s[i])
        
        # Add emitted and attenuated light from this segment
        E = E + T * c[i] * (1.0 - F)
        
        # Update accumulated transmittance
        T = T * F
        
    # Add boundary radiance attenuated by the full path
    E = E + T * B
    
    return E
```

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
- If $\tau_i = 0$, $F = 1$, and the term $c_i(1-F)$ becomes 0, which is correct (no extinction, but emission is handled by the limit $\lim_{\tau \to 0} c(1-e^{-\tau \Delta s}) = c \Delta s$ if emission is defined per unit length, though the formula assumes $c$ is the source term in the RTE). Note: The provided formula assumes $c$ is the emission coefficient such that the source term is $\tau c$ or similar; standard RTE is $dL/ds = -\tau L + \epsilon$. If $\epsilon$ is constant, the integral is $\epsilon/\tau (1-e^{-\tau \Delta s})$. The source uses $c_i$ directly in $c_i(1-F)$, implying the source term is normalized or $c$ represents $\epsilon/\tau$ or the specific formulation in [S1, book]. Implementers must ensure $c$ matches the source's definition.
- Precision: For very large $\tau \Delta s$, $F \to 0$, and $1-F \to 1$. Standard floating point handles this well. For very small $\tau \Delta s$, $1-F \approx \tau \Delta s$, which is stable.
- Boundary: The boundary radiance $B$ is only attenuated by the total transmittance $T$ after all segments.

## Subtopics

### [Trapezoid-based Radiance Integration in Box Coordinates](9001-trapezoid-based-radiance-integration-in-box-coordinates.md)
Exactly integrates unoccluded single-scattering radiance in rectilinear box-shaped media with homogeneous density by decomposing intersections into trapezoid segments. Choose when rendering homogeneous boxes with light constraints.

### [Trapezoid Integration for Transmittance](9006-trapezoid-integration-for-transmittance.md)
Analytically integrates transmittance over linear segments in skewed coordinate systems where geometric path length varies non-linearly. Choose when handling skewed box intersections with constant optical properties.

## Operating characteristics
- **Accuracy:** Higher than discrete Riemann sum methods (Iteration A) when $\tau \Delta s$ is large, as it avoids approximation errors in the transmittance integral [S1, book].
- **Complexity:** $O(n)$ time and $O(1)$ extra space, where $n$ is the number of segments.
- **Strengths:** Floating-point accuracy for constant-property segments; no need for multiple samples per segment.
- **Limitations:** Requires segments to have constant optical properties. If properties vary within a segment, it must be subdivided.
- **When to use:** When rendering participating media with piecewise-constant optical properties and high accuracy is required.
- **When not to use:** When optical properties vary continuously within segments and subdivision is too expensive, or when using heterogeneous medium methods that handle variation analytically.

## Background
The method is described in the context of the volume rendering integral, specifically as "Iteration B" to distinguish it from the Riemann sum-based "Iteration A" [S1, book]. It is cited in discussions of local and global illumination in volume rendering. The approach builds on the analytical solution of the radiative transfer equation for constant coefficients. Related hardware-accelerated approaches include Shirley and Tuchman's software tetrahedron projection and Wylie et al.'s hardware-based steps [S1, book].
