# Refractive Radiative Transfer Equation

Also called: RRTE, Refractive RTE, RTE with spatially varying index of refraction

## Overview

The Refractive Radiative Transfer Equation (RRTE) is a generalization of the standard Radiative Transfer Equation (RTE) for participating media that possess a spatially varying index of refraction $n(\mathbf{x})$ [S1]. It solves the problem of modeling light transport in heterogeneous media where light rays follow curved trajectories due to continuous refraction, ensuring strict conservation of energy [S2, unofficial]. Unlike the [Standard volume rendering equation](1165-standard-volume-rendering-equation.md) which assumes straight-line propagation, the RRTE accounts for the bending of light rays and the scaling of radiance by $1/n^2$ along these curved paths [S1]. It extends the [Radiative Transfer Equation Formulation](1194-radiative-transfer-equation-formulation.md) by incorporating Hamiltonian optics principles to handle the coupled position-direction dynamics in inhomogeneous refractive media [S1].

## How it works

The RRTE describes the transport of **basic radiance** $\tilde{L}$, defined as standard radiance $L$ scaled by the square of the local index of refraction:
$$ \tilde{L} = \frac{L}{n^2} $$
This quantity is conserved along ray paths in lossless refractive media, unlike standard radiance which changes due to the changing cross-sectional area of the ray tube [S1].

### Inputs and Outputs
*   **Inputs**:
    *   Spatially varying index of refraction field $n(\mathbf{x})$ [S1].
    *   Extinction coefficient $\sigma_t(\mathbf{x})$, absorption coefficient $\sigma_a(\mathbf{x})$, and scattering coefficient $\sigma_s(\mathbf{x})$ [S1].
    *   Phase function $P(\boldsymbol{\omega}', \boldsymbol{\omega})$ [S1].
    *   Emissive basic radiance $\tilde{L}_e(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\omega})$ [S1].
    *   Initial conditions: position $\mathbf{x}_0$, direction $\boldsymbol{\omega}_0$, and time $t_0$ (for transient case) [S1].
*   **Outputs**:
    *   Basic radiance $\tilde{L}(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\omega}, t)$ at any point along the curved ray path [S1].

### Data Structures
The method operates on continuous fields [S1]. For numerical implementation (e.g., photon mapping or ray marching), the following structures are required:
1.  **Index of Refraction Field**: A scalar field $n(\mathbf{x})$ accessible via interpolation (e.g., trilinear on a grid or analytical function) [S1].
2.  **Optical Properties**: Fields for $\sigma_t(\mathbf{x})$, $\sigma_a(\mathbf{x})$, $\sigma_s(\mathbf{x})$, and $P(\cdot)$ [S1].
3.  **Ray State**: A tuple $(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\omega}, s, t)$ representing position, direction, arc length, and time [S1].
4.  **Velocity Vector**: An intermediate vector $\mathbf{v}$ used to integrate the ray equation [S1].

### Derivation Steps

1.  **Start with Phase Space Continuity**: Begin with the continuity equation in phase space $(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\omega})$:
    $$ \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \cdot \left( \frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} f \right) + \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \cdot \left( \frac{d\boldsymbol{\omega}}{dt} f \right) = Q(f) $$
    where $f$ is the photon density and $Q$ is the source term [S1].
2.  **Substitute Ray Dynamics**: Use the ray equation of geometric optics to define the trajectory derivatives. The position derivative is the direction vector scaled by group velocity, and the direction derivative is governed by the gradient of the refractive index:
    $$ \frac{d\mathbf{x}}{ds} = \boldsymbol{\omega} $$
    $$ \frac{d\boldsymbol{\omega}}{ds} = \frac{1}{n} \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} n - \boldsymbol{\omega} \left( \boldsymbol{\omega} \cdot \frac{1}{n} \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} n \right) $$
    This is derived from the Hamiltonian ray equation:
    $$ \frac{d}{ds} \left( n \frac{d\mathbf{x}}{ds} \right) = \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} n $$ [S1].
3.  **Expand Divergence Terms**: Expand the streaming terms $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \cdot (\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{ds} L)$ and $\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \cdot (\frac{d\boldsymbol{\omega}}{ds} L)$ using the product rule and chain rule [S1].
    *   Spatial term: $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \cdot (\boldsymbol{\omega} L) = \boldsymbol{\omega} \cdot \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} L$ [S1].
    *   Angular term: Involves $\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} L \cdot \frac{d\boldsymbol{\omega}}{ds}$ [S1].
4.  **Group Terms for Basic Radiance**: Combine the spatial and angular derivatives to form the gradient of $L/n^2$. The key identity is:
    $$ \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \cdot \left( \frac{d\mathbf{x}}{ds} L \right) + \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \cdot \left( \frac{d\boldsymbol{\omega}}{ds} L \right) = n^2 \boldsymbol{\omega} \cdot \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \left( \frac{L}{n^2} \right) + n^2 \frac{d\boldsymbol{\omega}}{ds} \cdot \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \left( \frac{L}{n^2} \right) $$
    This step exploits the fact that $n$ is independent of direction ($\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} n = 0$) [S1].
5.  **Normalize the Equation**: Divide the entire radiative transfer equation by $n^2$. Substitute $\tilde{L} = L/n^2$ and $\tilde{L}_e = L_e/n^2$. The time derivative term also requires handling the group velocity $v_g = ds/dt$. The expanded RRTE in terms of basic radiance is:
    $$ \frac{1}{v_g} \frac{\partial \tilde{L}}{\partial t} + \boldsymbol{\omega} \cdot \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \tilde{L} + \frac{d\boldsymbol{\omega}}{ds} \cdot \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \tilde{L} = \sigma_a \tilde{L}_e - \sigma_t \tilde{L} + \frac{\sigma_s}{4\pi} \int_{\Omega} P(\boldsymbol{\omega}', \boldsymbol{\omega}) \tilde{L} \, d\boldsymbol{\omega}' $$ [S1].
6.  **Formulate Total Derivative**: Recognize that the left-hand side is the total derivative of $\tilde{L}$ with respect to arc length $s$:
    $$ \frac{d\tilde{L}}{ds} = \frac{dt}{ds} \frac{\partial \tilde{L}}{\partial t} + \frac{d\mathbf{x}}{ds} \cdot \frac{\partial \tilde{L}}{\partial \mathbf{x}} + \frac{d\boldsymbol{\omega}}{ds} \cdot \frac{\partial \tilde{L}}{\partial \boldsymbol{\omega}} $$
    Thus, the steady-state RRTE is:
    $$ \frac{d\tilde{L}}{ds} = \sigma_a \tilde{L}_e - \sigma_t \tilde{L} + \frac{\sigma_s}{4\pi} \int_{\Omega} P(\boldsymbol{\omega}', \boldsymbol{\omega}) \tilde{L} \, d\boldsymbol{\omega}' $$ [S1].

### Ray Integration (Path Calculation)

To evaluate the equation, the curved ray path $\mathbf{x}(s)$ must be computed. This is done by solving the second-order ODE for the ray trajectory [S1].

1.  **Define Intermediate Vector**: Let $\mathbf{v}(s) = n(s) \frac{d\mathbf{x}}{ds}$. The ray equation becomes:
    $$ \frac{d\mathbf{v}}{ds} = \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} n $$ [S1].
2.  **Integrate Velocity**:
    $$ \mathbf{v}(s) = \mathbf{v}_0 + \int_{s_0}^{s} \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} n(\mathbf{x}(s')) \, ds' $$
    where $\mathbf{v}_0 = n(\mathbf{x}_0) \boldsymbol{\omega}_0$ [S1].
3.  **Integrate Position**:
    $$ \mathbf{x}(s) = \mathbf{x}_0 + \int_{s_0}^{s} \frac{\mathbf{v}(s')}{n(\mathbf{x}(s'))} \, ds' $$
    Note that $\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{ds} = \frac{\mathbf{v}}{n}$, and since $\mathbf{v} = n \boldsymbol{\omega}$, we have $\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{ds} = \boldsymbol{\omega}$, ensuring $\|\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{ds}\| = 1$ [S1].

### Transmittance Calculation

The transmittance $\tau$ along the curved path from $\mathbf{x}_0$ to $\mathbf{x}$ is:
$$ \tau(\mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{x}) = \exp \left( - \int_{s_0}^{s} \sigma_t(\mathbf{x}(s')) \, ds' \right) $$
Since $\|\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{ds'}\| = 1$, the arc length element $ds'$ is sufficient; no additional norm factor is needed [S1].

### Formal Solution (Steady-State)

The integral solution for basic radiance at point $\mathbf{x}$ along a curved ray originating from $\mathbf{x}_0$ is:
$$ \tilde{L}(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\omega}) = \tau(\mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{x}) \tilde{L}(\mathbf{x}_0, \boldsymbol{\omega}'(\mathbf{x}_0)) + \int_{\mathbf{x}_0}^{\mathbf{x}} \tau(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{x}) \sigma_t(\mathbf{u}) \tilde{J}(\mathbf{u}, \boldsymbol{\omega}'(\mathbf{u})) \, d\mathbf{u} $$
where $\tilde{J}$ is the basic source term:
$$ \tilde{J}(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\omega}) = (1 - \Lambda) \tilde{L}_e(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\omega}) + \frac{\Lambda}{4\pi} \int_{\Omega} \tilde{L}(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\omega}_i) P(\boldsymbol{\omega}_i, \boldsymbol{\omega}) \, d\boldsymbol{\omega}_i $$
and $\Lambda = \sigma_s / \sigma_t$ is the single-scattering albedo [S1].

### Pseudocode

```python
def compute_rrte_steady_state(x0, omega0, n_field, sigma_t_field, sigma_a_field, sigma_s_field, phase_func, L_e_func, max_steps, step_size):
    """
    Computes basic radiance along a curved ray in a refractive participating medium.
    Uses a simple Euler integration for the ray path and transmittance.
    """
    x = list(x0)
    omega = list(omega0)
    n = n_field(x)
    v = [n * o for o in omega]
    
    transmittance = 1.0
    in_scatter_contribution = 0.0
    L_boundary = L_e_func(x0, omega0) / (n_field(x0)**2)
    
    for i in range(max_steps):
        # 1. Compute gradient of n at current position (finite difference)
        eps = 1e-6
        grad_n = [0.0, 0.0, 0.0]
        for d in range(3):
            x_plus = list(x)
            x_plus[d] += eps
            x_minus = list(x)
            x_minus[d] -= eps
            grad_n[d] = (n_field(x_plus) - n_field(x_minus)) / (2 * eps)
            
        # 2. Update velocity vector v: dv/ds = grad_n
        v = [v[d] + grad_n[d] * step_size for d in range(3)]
        
        # 3. Update position: dx/ds = v / n
        n_current = n_field(x)
        x = [x[d] + (v[d] / n_current) * step_size for d in range(3)]
        
        # 4. Update direction omega: omega = v / n
        n_new = n_field(x)
        omega = [v[d] / n_new for d in range(3)]
        
        # 5. Update transmittance
        sigma_t = sigma_t_field(x)
        transmittance = transmittance * exp(-sigma_t * step_size)
        
        # 6. Compute source term J at new position
        L_e_val = L_e_func(x, omega)
        L_e_basic = L_e_val / (n_new**2)
        sigma_s = sigma_s_field(x)
        Lambda = sigma_s / sigma_t if sigma_t > 0 else 0.0
        
        if Lambda < 1.0:
            J_basic = (1 - Lambda) * L_e_basic
        else:
            J_basic = 0.0
            
        # 7. Accumulate in-scatter contribution
        in_scatter_contribution += transmittance * sigma_t * J_basic * step_size
        
        # Check for termination (e.g., exit medium or max distance)
        if distance(x, x0) > max_distance:
            break
            
    L_basic_final = transmittance * L_boundary + in_scatter_contribution
    return L_basic_final

def distance(a, b):
    return sum((a[i] - b[i])**2 for i in range(len(a)))**0.5
```

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls

1.  **Discontinuous Refraction**: The RRTE assumes a continuous gradient $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} n$. It cannot model sharp interfaces (e.g., air-glass boundary) directly because $\nabla n$ is undefined (infinite). At such boundaries, Snell's law must be applied explicitly to update $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ and $n$, and the RRTE is used only within homogeneous or continuously varying regions [S1].
2.  **Curved Ray Norm**: For curved rays, $\|\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{ds}\|$ is not trivially 1 in arbitrary parameterizations, but by defining $s$ as arc length and using the eikonal relationship $\boldsymbol{\omega} = \frac{\nabla S}{|\nabla S|}$, it is guaranteed that $\|\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{ds}\| = 1$ [S1].
3.  **Dispersion**: The derivation assumes a quasi-stationary medium. If dispersion is significant, the group velocity $v_g$ depends on frequency $\nu$. The steady-state equation assumes $\partial \nu / \partial t = 0$ [S1].
4.  **Energy Conservation**: Previous methods using nonsymmetric BTDFs for discrete refraction fail to conserve energy in continuously refracting media. The RRTE ensures conservation of energy via an extended definition of radiance [S2, unofficial].

## Subtopics

### Transient RRTE Formal Solution
The transient RRTE accounts for time-dependent light transport, such as in time-of-flight imaging or light echoes. It introduces the group velocity $v_g = ds/dt$ into the Lagrangian derivative. The formal solution is:
$$ \tilde{L}(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\omega}, t) = \tau(\mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{x}) \tilde{L}(\mathbf{x}_0, \boldsymbol{\omega}'(\mathbf{x}_0), t_0) + \int_{\mathbf{x}_0}^{\mathbf{x}} \tau(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{x}) \sigma_t(\mathbf{u}) v_g(\mathbf{u}) \tilde{J}(\mathbf{u}, \boldsymbol{\omega}'(\mathbf{u}), t'(\mathbf{u})) \, d\mathbf{u} $$
where the transmittance is integrated over time:
$$ \tau(\mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{x}_1) = \exp \left( - \int_{t_0}^{t_1} \sigma_t(\mathbf{x}(s(t'))) v_g \, dt' \right) $$ [S1].

## Operating characteristics

*   **Complexity**: The computational cost is dominated by the numerical integration of the ray path $\mathbf{x}(s)$, which requires evaluating $\nabla n$ and $n$ at each step. This is $O(N)$ per ray, where $N$ is the number of integration steps. The scattering term requires integration over the solid angle, making full rendering $O(N \cdot M)$ where $M$ is the number of angular samples [S1].
*   **Memory**: Requires storage of the refractive index field and optical properties. For photon mapping, a bounding box hierarchy over photon discs is needed [S1].
*   **Strengths**: Correctly models caustics and subsurface scattering in media with spatially varying refractive indices (e.g., sugar solutions, biological tissue with varying density). Ensures energy conservation where standard methods fail [S1][S2, unofficial].
*   **Limitations**: Cannot handle sharp discontinuities without hybrid handling (Snell's law). Computationally expensive due to curved ray tracing. Requires smooth $n(\mathbf{x})$ fields [S1].
*   **When to use**: Rendering of caustics through inhomogeneous transparent media, subsurface scattering in tissues with varying optical properties, or time-of-flight simulations in complex media [S1][S2, unofficial].

## Background

The RRTE was introduced in a paper published in ACM Transactions on Graphics and presented at ACM SIGGRAPH 2014. It generalizes the standard RTE to include spatially varying index of refraction. The concept of basic radiance ($L/n^2$) was previously used by Hanrahan and Krueger [1993] for subsurface scattering and by Veach [1996] for caustics with discrete refraction. The RRTE unifies these approaches for continuous refraction. The derivation relies on non-linear Hamiltonian optics and the conservation of energy in phase space [S1][S2, unofficial].
