# Surface Shading Effects
## Overview
Surface shading effects apply local illumination models to volumetric data to create the appearance of solid surfaces, shadows, and ambient occlusion within participating media [G]. This family solves the problem of volumes appearing as featureless fog by introducing directional lighting, self-shadowing, and indirect light approximation [G]. It differs from sibling methods like [Lighting Models](2056-lighting-models.md) by focusing specifically on surface-like appearance cues (normals, shadows) rather than general scattering phase functions, and from [Two-Pass Shadow Casting](2196-two-pass-shadow-casting-kajiya-and-von-herzen.md) by typically evaluating shading per-sample during ray marching rather than via separate geometry passes [G].

## Shared principles
All methods in this family share the core mechanism of evaluating visibility or illumination at a specific point $\mathbf{p}$ within or on the boundary of a volume, based on a light source direction $\mathbf{l}$ or environment [G].

1.  **Visibility/Transmittance Evaluation:** The fundamental operation is determining how much light reaches $\mathbf{p}$ from a source [G]. This involves integrating the extinction coefficient $\sigma_t(\mathbf{x})$ along a path from the source to $\mathbf{p}$ (or vice versa) [G]. The transmittance $T$ is calculated as:
    $$ T = \exp\left(-\int_{\mathbf{p}_{source}}^{\mathbf{p}} \sigma_t(\mathbf{x}) \, d\mathbf{x}\right) $$
    In discrete implementations, this integral is approximated by summing $\sigma_t \cdot \Delta s$ over samples along the ray [G].

2.  **Local Shading Models:** For surface-like appearance in volumes, normals are approximated from the volume gradient $\mathbf{g} = \nabla V$ [G]. Usually, a simple absorption and emission model is used because it is relatively cheap to evaluate and gives good enough results [G].

3.  **Shadowing Approximation:** Shadows are computed by testing occlusion along the direction to the light source [G].

4.  **Ambient Occlusion:** Indirect illumination is approximated by integrating over the hemisphere or using simplified models (e.g., upward ray for sky light) [G]. The ambient contribution $L_{ambient}$ is often modulated by a shadowing factor based on local density:
    $$ L_{ambient} = L_{sky} \cdot \exp(-D_{up}) \cdot AO_{factor} $$
    where $D_{up}$ is the integrated density along an upward ray [G].

## Subtopics

### [Powder Sugar Effect](1713-powder-sugar-effect.md)
The Powder Sugar Effect is a local illumination approximation used in volumetric rendering to simulate the physical reality that less light is available for scattering at the fringes of a cloud or participating medium [1713-powder-sugar-effect.md]. It solves the artifact where low-density regions appear unnaturally bright or "glowing" by modulating the scattered light contribution based on local density [1713-powder-sugar-effect.md]. It sets itself apart from sibling methods like [Shadow Density Scale](11398-shadow-density-scale.md) by specifically targeting the *source* of scattered light (reducing it where density is low) rather than the *view* attenuation (brightening dense regions to simulate multiple scattering) [1713-powder-sugar-effect.md].

**Inputs and Outputs:** Input: local density $d$, step size $\Delta s$, and decay parameter $\alpha$ (`powder_decay`). Output: scalar modulation factor $P \in [0, 1]$ applied to the scattered light increment [1713-powder-sugar-effect.md].

**Method:** Calculate optical thickness $t = d \cdot \Delta s$. Evaluate $P(t) = 1 - e^{-\alpha t}$. Multiply the scattered light increment $\Delta L_{scat}$ by $P(t)$ [1713-powder-sugar-effect.md].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
import math

def powder_sugar_effect(density: float, step_size: float, powder_decay: float) -> float:
    """
    Calculates the modulation factor for scattered light at cloud fringes.
    
    Args:
        density: Local volume density at the current sample point.
        step_size: The distance step taken during volume integration.
        powder_decay: The decay constant controlling fringe darkness.
        
    Returns:
        A float between 0.0 and 1.0 to multiply the scattered light increment.
    """
    optical_thickness = density * step_size
    modulation_factor = 1.0 - math.exp(-powder_decay * optical_thickness)
    return modulation_factor
```

**Edge Cases:** Zero density yields $P=0$; high density yields $P \to 1$; parameter sensitivity affects fringe darkness; sparse sampling may exacerbate blue noise [1713-powder-sugar-effect.md].

### Backlighting Glow
A shading technique for thin, translucent objects like wings that adds a term brightening the surface as it turns away from the light source while the camera looks toward it, simulating light transmission through the membrane [S2, unofficial]. It is chosen for semi-transparent biological or delicate structures where subsurface scattering is approximated by geometric orientation [S2, unofficial]. Parameter: `SUN` direction vector is `vec3(-0.42, 0.60, -0.68)` [S2, unofficial].

## Background
Surface shading techniques for participating media originated to give volumetric clouds and fog a tangible appearance rather than flat fog [G]. The Powder Sugar Effect was introduced to address the lack of detail at cloud fringes by attenuating scattered light in low-density areas [1713-powder-sugar-effect.md]. The integration of complex lighting cycles, soft shadows, and ambient occlusion into voxel-based volumetric rendering was demonstrated in `voxelG`, which combined terrain and cloud shading [S1, unofficial]. Specialized techniques like backlighting glow have been applied to procedural animations of translucent objects, such as butterflies, to enhance realism [S2, unofficial].
