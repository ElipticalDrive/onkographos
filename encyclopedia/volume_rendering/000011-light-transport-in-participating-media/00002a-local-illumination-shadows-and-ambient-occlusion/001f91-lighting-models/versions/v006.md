# Lighting Models


## Overview
Shading or illumination aims to enhance the appearance of a rendered object by modeling effects like shadows, light scattering, and absorption, depending on the chosen optical model [S3, unofficial]. Local lighting shades each sample independently, ignoring the rest of the scene, so it cannot produce shadows or scattering but is cheap to compute [S3, unofficial]. It sets itself apart from global illumination techniques by using simplified local approximations rather than stochastic integration of the rendering equation [S3, unofficial].

## Shared principles
All methods in this family share the core mechanism of evaluating visibility or illumination at a specific point $\mathbf{p}$ within or on the boundary of a volume, based on a light source direction $\mathbf{l}$ or environment [G].

1.  **Visibility/Transmittance Evaluation:** The fundamental operation is determining how much light reaches $\mathbf{p}$ from a source [G]. This involves integrating the extinction coefficient $\sigma_t(\mathbf{x})$ along a path from the source to $\mathbf{p}$ (or vice versa) [G]. The transmittance $T$ is calculated as:
    $$ T = \exp\left(-\int_{\mathbf{p}_{source}}^{\mathbf{p}} \sigma_t(\mathbf{x}) \, d\mathbf{x}\right) $$
    In discrete implementations, this integral is approximated by summing $\sigma_t \cdot \Delta s$ over samples along the ray [G].

2.  **Local Shading Models:** For surface-like appearance in volumes, normals are approximated from the volume gradient $\mathbf{g} = \nabla V$ [G]. Usually, a simple absorption and emission model is used because it is relatively cheap to evaluate and gives good enough results [S1, unofficial][S3, unofficial].

3.  **Shadowing Approximation:** Shadows are computed by testing occlusion along the direction to the light source [G].

4.  **Ambient Occlusion:** Indirect illumination is approximated by integrating over the hemisphere or using simplified models (e.g., upward ray for sky light) [G]. The ambient contribution $L_{ambient}$ is often modulated by a shadowing factor based on local density:
    $$ L_{ambient} = L_{sky} \cdot \exp(-D_{up}) \cdot AO_{factor} $$
    where $D_{up}$ is the integrated density along an upward ray [G].

## Subtopics

### [Shading models](837-shading-models.md)
Applies local illumination calculations to enhance the perception of 3D shape and surface orientation within volumetric data. It sets itself apart from [Gradient-based Shading with Phase Functions](1020-gradient-based-shading-with-phase-functions.md) by using simplified local reflection rather than complex phase functions, and from [Frequency Domain Directional Shading](852-frequency-domain-directional-shading.md) by operating in the spatial domain.

### [Shading for Semi-Transparent Volumes](2144-shading-for-semi-transparent-volumes.md)
Enables local diffuse illumination for semi-transparent volume rendering by utilizing multi-stage rasterization hardware to compute lighting in a single pass. It differs from isosurface shading by using alpha blending instead of alpha testing, and from methods requiring 3D textures by sacrificing real trilinear interpolation to fit gradient and color data into separate 2D textures.

### [Cook-Torrance BRDF](2509-cook-torrance-brdf.md)
A physically based rendering lighting model that uses the microfacet surface model to accurately represent real-world reflectance, distinguishing between metals and dielectrics. It sets itself apart from siblings by explicitly separating diffuse and specular lobes with energy-conserving weights and using the Fresnel effect to vary reflectivity based on viewing angle.

### [Whitted-Style Shading with Diffuse/Glossy Materials](4896-whitted-style-shading-with-diffuse-glossy-materials.md)
Evaluates illumination at a surface point by combining ambient lighting with direct illumination from light sources, using shadow rays to determine visibility. It differs from its siblings by explicitly separating the computation of diffuse and glossy reflection components.

### [3D Lookup Table Illumination Model](6930-3d-lookup-table-illumination-model.md)
Approximates light transport in participating media by indexing a precomputed table with three angular parameters derived from the surface normal, light direction, and view direction. It differs from sibling methods by replacing real-time integration of the scattering phase function with a fast trilinear texture lookup.

### [Spot Light with Cone Falloff](8712-spot-light-with-cone-falloff.md)
Computes the attenuation factor for a point light source that emits light within a specific cone direction, applying smooth falloff based on the angle between the light direction and the vector to the shading point. It is distinguished from area lights by its zero-size emission model and from uniform point lights by its angular dependency.

### Shading
Aims to enhance appearance by modeling effects like shadows, light scattering, and absorption, depending on the chosen optical model [S3, unofficial]. A simple absorption and emission model is usually used because it is relatively cheap to evaluate and gives good enough results [S3, unofficial]. Local illumination shades each sample independently, ignoring the rest of the scene, so it cannot produce shadows or scattering but is cheap to compute [S3, unofficial].

### Two-sided Lighting
Under this model, each directional light shines in two directions: both the specified direction and the opposite direction [S4]. It is configured by setting the light color and direction, enabling the light, and calling `vpEnable` with the `VP_LIGHT_BOTH_SIDES` option [S4].

### Atmospheric Lighting Model
A lighting model that attenuates light through the volume, providing simple but robust lighting without relying on a surface normal [S5, unofficial]. It is used for multi-field gradients because the gradient orientation may not correspond to the surface of the classified region and can flip direction [S5, unofficial].

### Uniform Update for Lighting and Camera
Computes and sets shader uniforms for up to 4 lights, eye position, and eye look direction in object space [S6, unofficial]. It involves computing the inverse of the actor's world matrix, converting light positions to object space, and transforming the eye look direction accordingly [S6, unofficial].

### Day Lighting Model
Applies a simple day lighting model with sun, sky, and ambient components to a base color [S7, unofficial]. The sky factor $S$ is $0$ if $n_z < -0.5$, $1$ if $n_z > 0.5$, else $0.5$. Direct lighting is $D = \max(\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{s}, 0) \cdot V$. The result is $C = B \cdot (A + S \cdot C_{sky} + D \cdot C_{sun})$ [S7, unofficial]. Typical parameters include a sun direction of `(-0.45, 0.35, 0.82)`, ambient color `vec3(0.17, 0.20, 0.27)`, sky color `vec3(0.23, 0.24, 0.21)`, and direct sun color `vec3(0.88, 0.76, 0.61)` [S7, unofficial].

## Background
Early volume rendering methods focused on simple absorption and emission due to computational constraints [S3, unofficial]. The VolPack library introduced standardized API calls for setting light properties and enabling two-sided lighting [S4]. Atmospheric lighting models were developed to handle multi-field gradients where surface normals are unreliable [S5, unofficial]. Modern implementations often compute and update lighting uniforms in object space for efficient GPU rendering [S6, unofficial]. Specialized models like the day lighting model apply sun, sky, and ambient components to base colors for terrain or voxel data [S7, unofficial].
