# Cook-Torrance BRDF

Also called: Microfacet BRDF, GGX Cook-Torrance, Walter et al. BRDF

## Overview
The Cook-Torrance BRDF is a physically based rendering model that represents the appearance of real-world opaque materials by combining diffuse and specular components based on microfacet theory [S1, S3, S4]. It distinguishes itself from simpler models like Phong by strictly conserving energy and using a statistical approximation of surface roughness to accurately simulate specular highlights, self-shadowing, and Fresnel effects [S4][S5, unofficial]. Unlike [Shading models](837-shading-models.md) which may use empirical approximations, this method decomposes reflectance into physically meaningful terms: Normal Distribution, Geometry, and Fresnel [S3].

## How it works
**Inputs:**
*   Surface normal $\mathbf{n}$ (unit vector) [S2, S4].
*   View direction $\mathbf{v}$ (unit vector, pointing from surface to camera) [S2, S4].
*   Light direction $\mathbf{l}$ (unit vector, pointing from surface to light source) [S2, S4].
*   Diffuse albedo $\mathbf{c}$ (RGB color) [S2, S4].
*   Roughness parameter $\gamma$ (scalar) [S2].
*   Base reflectivity $F_0$ (RGB color, typically $0.04$ for non-metals, or equal to $\mathbf{c}$ for metals) [S2, S4].
*   Light radiance $L_i$ (RGB color) [S2, S4].
*   Emitted light $L_e$ (RGB color, optional) [S2, S4].

**Outputs:**
*   Outgoing radiance $L_o$ (RGB color) [S2, S4].

**Data Structures:**
Not given in the sources.

**Steps:**

1.  **Calculate Halfway Vector:**
    Compute the halfway vector $\mathbf{h}$ between the view direction $\mathbf{v}$ and the light direction $\mathbf{l}$:
    $$ \mathbf{h} = \frac{\mathbf{l} + \mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{l} + \mathbf{v}\|} $$
    [S2, S4]

2.  **Compute Normal Distribution Function (D):**
    Map roughness $\gamma$ to $\alpha$:
    $$ \alpha = \gamma^2 $$
    [S1, S2]
    Calculate $D$:
    $$ D(\mathbf{n}, \mathbf{h}, \alpha) = \frac{\alpha^2}{\pi \left( (\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{h})^2 (\alpha^2 - 1) + 1 \right)^2} $$
    [S1, S2][S6, unofficial]
    *Note: [S4] omits the square in the denominator's inner term, but the squared form is standard in [S1, S2][S6, unofficial].*

3.  **Compute Geometry Function (G):**
    Calculate the roughness-dependent coefficient $k$:
    $$ k = \frac{(\alpha + 1)^2}{8} $$
    [S2, S4][S6, unofficial]
    Compute the Schlick geometry term for a vector $\mathbf{i}$ (where $\mathbf{i}$ is either $\mathbf{v}$ or $\mathbf{l}$):
    $$ G_{\text{Schlick}}(\mathbf{n}, \mathbf{i}, k) = \frac{\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{i}}{(\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{i})(1 - k) + k} $$
    [S2, S4][S6, unofficial]
    The total geometry function is the product of the terms for view and light:
    $$ G(\mathbf{n}, \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{l}, \alpha) = G_{\text{Schlick}}(\mathbf{n}, \mathbf{v}, k) \cdot G_{\text{Schlick}}(\mathbf{n}, \mathbf{l}, k) $$
    [S2, S4][S6, unofficial]

4.  **Compute Fresnel Function (F):**
    Use the Fresnel-Schlick approximation:
    $$ F(\mathbf{h}, \mathbf{v}, F_0) = F_0 + (1 - F_0)(1 - \mathbf{h} \cdot \mathbf{v})^5 $$
    [S2, S4][S6, unofficial]
    *Note: [S1] uses a different exponential form $F(\boldsymbol{\omega}_o, \mathbf{h}) = F_0 + (1 - F_0)2^{-[5.55473(\boldsymbol{\omega}_o \cdot \mathbf{h}) + 6.8316](\boldsymbol{\omega}_o \cdot \mathbf{h})}$, but the Schlick approximation is standard for real-time PBR [S2, S4][S6, unofficial].*

5.  **Compute Diffuse and Specular Weights:**
    Ensure energy conservation. The specular weight $k_s$ equals the Fresnel term, and the diffuse weight $k_d$ is the complement:
    $$ k_s = F(\mathbf{h}, \mathbf{v}, F_0) $$
    $$ k_d = 1 - k_s $$
    [S2, S4][S6, unofficial]

6.  **Compute BRDF Components:**
    *   **Lambertian Diffuse:**
        $$ f_{\text{Lambert}} = \frac{\mathbf{c}}{\pi} $$
        [S2, S4][S6, unofficial]
    *   **Cook-Torrance Specular:**
        $$ f_{\text{CookTorrance}} = \frac{D(\mathbf{n}, \mathbf{h}, \alpha) F(\mathbf{h}, \mathbf{v}, F_0) G(\mathbf{n}, \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{l}, \alpha)}{4 (\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{v}) (\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{l})} $$
        [S2, S4][S6, unofficial]

7.  **Combine and Integrate:**
    The total outgoing radiance $L_o$ is the sum of emitted light and the integral of reflected light over the hemisphere $\Omega$:
    $$ L_o = L_e + \int_{\Omega} \left( k_d f_{\text{Lambert}} + k_s f_{\text{CookTorrance}} \right) L_i (\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{l}) \, d\omega_i $$
    [S2, S4][S6, unofficial]
    For a single directional light source, this simplifies to:
    $$ L_o = L_e + \left( (1 - F) \frac{\mathbf{c}}{\pi} + \frac{D F G}{4 (\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{v}) (\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{l})} \right) L_i (\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{l}) $$
    [S2][S6, unofficial]

**Pseudocode:**

```python
import math

def cook_torrance_brdf(n, v, l, c, gamma, F0, Li, Le):
    # 1. Halfway vector
    h = [l[0]+v[0], l[1]+v[1], l[2]+v[2]]
    h_len = math.sqrt(h[0]**2 + h[1]**2 + h[2]**2)
    h = [x / h_len for x in h]

    # 2. Normal Distribution (GGX)
    alpha = gamma * gamma
    nh = n[0]*h[0] + n[1]*h[1] + n[2]*h[2]
    denom = (nh * nh) * (alpha * alpha - 1) + 1
    D = (alpha * alpha) / (math.pi * denom * denom)

    # 3. Geometry (Schlick-GGX)
    k = ((gamma + 1) ** 2) / 8
    nv = n[0]*v[0] + n[1]*v[1] + n[2]*v[2]
    nl = n[0]*l[0] + n[1]*l[1] + n[2]*l[2]

    # 4. Fresnel (Schlick)
    hv = h[0]*v[0] + h[1]*v[1] + h[2]*v[2]
    F = F0 + (1 - F0) * ((1 - hv) ** 5)

    # 5. Diffuse and Specular Weights
    ks = F
    kd = 1 - ks

    # 6. Components
    f_diffuse = kd * (c[0]/math.pi, c[1]/math.pi, c[2]/math.pi)
    G_v = nv / (nv * (1 - k) + k)
    G_l = nl / (nl * (1 - k) + k)
    G = G_v * G_l
    f_specular = (D * F * G) / (4 * nv * nl)

    # 7. Total Radiance
    Lo = [Le[i] + (f_diffuse[i] + f_specular) * Li[i] * nl for i in range(3)]
    return Lo
```

**Parameters:**
*   $\gamma$ (Roughness): Scalar roughness parameter [S2].
*   $F_0$ (Base Reflectivity): $0.04$ for non-metals (dielectrics), equal to albedo $\mathbf{c}$ for metals [S2, S4][S6, unofficial].
*   $\mathbf{c}$ (Albedo): RGB color [S2, S4].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Grazing Angles:** When $\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{v}$ or $\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{l}$ approaches zero, the denominator in the specular term approaches zero. The geometry and Fresnel terms stabilize the result, but numerical precision issues may arise [G].
*   **Energy Conservation:** Failing to multiply the diffuse term by $(1 - F)$ results in energy creation, leading to overly bright surfaces [S2, S4].
*   **Roughness Mapping:** Ensure $\alpha$ is derived correctly from roughness. The GGX formula requires $\alpha^2$ in the numerator and $\alpha^4$ effectively in the denominator structure [S1, S2].

## Subtopics

### Cook-Torrance GGX BRDF
A variant that specifically employs the anisotropic GGX Normal Distribution Function, Smith height-correlated geometry term, and Schlick Fresnel approximation [S7, unofficial]. It distinguishes itself by supporting anisotropic roughness (different roughness in tangent and bitangent directions) and using a height-correlated geometry model for more accurate shadowing on rough surfaces [S7, unofficial]. Choose this when rendering materials with directional scratches or brushed metals [S7, unofficial]. It is a more advanced version of the standard isotropic Cook-Torrance model [S7, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Not given in the sources.
*   **Memory Complexity:** Not given in the sources.
*   **Performance:** Not given in the sources.
*   **Strengths:** Physically accurate, energy-conserving, handles a wide range of materials (metals, dielectrics, rough, smooth) with few parameters [S4][S5, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** Does not account for subsurface scattering or complex inter-reflections (requires global illumination techniques like [3D Lookup Table Illumination Model](6930-3d-lookup-table-illumination-model.md) or path tracing) [S3]. The Schlick approximation for Fresnel is an approximation and may deviate from physical reality at extreme angles or for specific materials [S2].
*   **When to use:** Real-time rendering, PBR pipelines, when physical accuracy is required for surface appearance [S3, S4].
*   **When not to use:** When subsurface scattering is dominant (use [Shading for Semi-Transparent Volumes](2144-shading-for-semi-transparent-volumes.md)), or when performance is critically constrained and artistic control is preferred over physical accuracy.

## Background
The Cook-Torrance BRDF was introduced by Richard L. Cook and Kenneth E. Torrance [S2, S4]. It was one of the first models to apply microfacet theory to computer graphics, providing a physically based explanation for specular highlights. The model was later popularized in real-time rendering through the work of Walter et al. (2007) who introduced the GGX distribution, and Karis (2013) who simplified the Fresnel and geometry terms for real-time engines [S1, S4]. It is used in libraries like Jrender to support advanced shading features [S3].
