# Cook-Torrance BRDF

Also called: Microfacet BRDF, GGX Cook-Torrance, Walter et al. BRDF

## Overview
The Cook-Torrance BRDF is a physically based rendering model that represents the appearance of real-world opaque materials by combining diffuse and specular components based on microfacet theory [S1, S3, S4]. It distinguishes itself from simpler models like Phong by strictly conserving energy and using a statistical approximation of surface roughness to accurately simulate specular highlights, self-shadowing, and Fresnel effects [S4][S5, unofficial]. Unlike [Shading models](837-shading-models.md) which may use empirical approximations, this method decomposes reflectance into physically meaningful terms: Normal Distribution, Geometry, and Fresnel [S3].

## How it works
**Inputs:**
*   Surface normal $\mathbf{n}$ (unit vector).
*   View direction $\mathbf{v}$ (unit vector, pointing from surface to camera).
*   Light direction $\mathbf{l}$ (unit vector, pointing from surface to light source).
*   Diffuse albedo $\mathbf{c}$ (RGB color).
*   Roughness parameter $\gamma$ (scalar, typically $[0, 1]$).
*   Base reflectivity $F_0$ (RGB color, typically $0.04$ for non-metals, or equal to $\mathbf{c}$ for metals).
*   Light radiance $L_i$ (RGB color).
*   Emitted light $L_e$ (RGB color, optional).

**Outputs:**
*   Outgoing radiance $L_o$ (RGB color).

**Data Structures:**
No complex data structures are required for the evaluation itself; it operates on scalar and vector primitives.

**Steps:**

1.  **Calculate Halfway Vector:**
    Compute the halfway vector $\mathbf{h}$ between the view direction $\mathbf{v}$ and the light direction $\mathbf{l}$:
    $$ \mathbf{h} = \frac{\mathbf{l} + \mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{l} + \mathbf{v}\|} $$
    [S2, S4]

2.  **Compute Normal Distribution Function (D):**
    Use the Trowbridge-Reitz GGX approximation to determine the probability of microfacets aligning with $\mathbf{h}$. First, map roughness $\gamma$ to $\alpha$:
    $$ \alpha = \gamma^2 $$
    [S1, S2]
    Then calculate $D$:
    $$ D(\mathbf{n}, \mathbf{h}, \alpha) = \frac{\alpha^2}{\pi \left( (\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{h})^2 (\alpha^2 - 1) + 1 \right)^2} $$
    [S1, S2][S6, unofficial]
    *Note: Some sources omit the square in the denominator's inner term or define $\alpha$ differently, but the GGX form above is the standard implementation cited in [S1, S2][S6, unofficial].*

3.  **Compute Geometry Function (G):**
    Use Schlick-GGX with Smith's method to account for self-shadowing and masking. First, calculate the roughness-dependent coefficient $k$:
    $$ k = \frac{(\alpha + 1)^2}{8} $$
    [S2, S4][S6, unofficial]
    Then compute the Schlick geometry term for a vector $\mathbf{i}$ (where $\mathbf{i}$ is either $\mathbf{v}$ or $\mathbf{l}$):
    $$ G_{\text{Schlick}}(\mathbf{n}, \mathbf{i}, k) = \frac{\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{i}}{(\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{i})(1 - k) + k} $$
    [S2, S4][S6, unofficial]
    The total geometry function is the product of the terms for view and light:
    $$ G(\mathbf{n}, \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{l}, \alpha) = G_{\text{Schlick}}(\mathbf{n}, \mathbf{v}, k) \cdot G_{\text{Schlick}}(\mathbf{n}, \mathbf{l}, k) $$
    [S2, S4][S6, unofficial]

4.  **Compute Fresnel Function (F):**
    Use the Fresnel-Schlick approximation to determine reflectivity at grazing angles:
    $$ F(\mathbf{h}, \mathbf{v}, F_0) = F_0 + (1 - F_0)(1 - \mathbf{h} \cdot \mathbf{v})^5 $$
    [S2, S4][S6, unofficial]
    *Note: [S1] uses a more complex exponential form for $F$, but the Schlick approximation is the standard for real-time PBR as cited in [S2, S4][S6, unofficial].*

5.  **Compute Diffuse and Specular Weights:**
    Ensure energy conservation. The specular weight $k_s$ is determined by the Fresnel term at the view angle (often approximated by $F(\mathbf{h}, \mathbf{v}, F_0)$ or simply $F_0$ for simplicity in some implementations, but strictly $k_s = F$ in the energy-conserving split):
    $$ k_s = F(\mathbf{h}, \mathbf{v}, F_0) $$
    $$ k_d = 1 - k_s $$
    [S2, S4][S6, unofficial]
    *Note: [S1] defines the diffuse term directly as $a/\pi$ without explicit $k_d$ weighting in the final sum formula (13), but [S2, S4][S6, unofficial] explicitly use $k_d = 1-F$ to conserve energy.*

6.  **Compute BRDF Components:**
    *   **Lambertian Diffuse:**
        $$ f_{\text{Lambert}} = \frac{\mathbf{c}}{\pi} $$
        [S2, S4][S6, unofficial]
    *   **Cook-Torrance Specular:**
        $$ f_{\text{CookTorrance}} = \frac{D(\mathbf{n}, \mathbf{h}, \alpha) F(\mathbf{h}, \mathbf{v}, F_0) G(\mathbf{n}, \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{l}, \alpha)}{4 (\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{v}) (\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{l})} $$
        [S2, S4][S6, unofficial]

7.  **Combine and Integrate:**
    The total outgoing radiance $L_o$ is the sum of emitted light and the integral of reflected light over the hemisphere $\Omega$:
    $$ L_o = L_e + \int_{\Omega} \left( k_d f_{\text{Lambert}} + k_s f_{\text{CookTorrance}} \right) L_i (\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{l}) \, d\omega_i $$
    [S2, S4][S6, unofficial]
    For a single directional light source, this simplifies to:
    $$ L_o = L_e + \left( (1 - F) \frac{\mathbf{c}}{\pi} + \frac{D F G}{4 (\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{v}) (\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{l})} F \right) L_i (\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{l}) $$
    *Correction: The specular term already includes $F$. The standard form is:*
    $$ L_o = L_e + \left( (1 - F) \frac{\mathbf{c}}{\pi} + \frac{D F G}{4 (\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{v}) (\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{l})} \right) L_i (\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{l}) $$
    [S2][S6, unofficial]

**Pseudocode:**

```python
def cook_torrance_brdf(n, v, l, c, gamma, F0, Li, Le):
    # 1. Halfway vector
    h = normalize(l + v)
    
    # 2. Normal Distribution (GGX)
    alpha = gamma * gamma
    nh = dot(n, h)
    denom = (nh * nh) * (alpha * alpha - 1) + 1
    D = (alpha * alpha) / (pi * denom * denom)
    
    # 3. Geometry (Schlick-GGX)
    k = ((gamma + 1) ** 2) / 8
    nv = dot(n, v)
    nl = dot(n, l)
    
    # Avoid division by zero
    if nv <= 0 or nl <= 0:
        return Le
        
    G_v = nv / (nv * (1 - k) + k)
    G_l = nl / (nl * (1 - k) + k)
    G = G_v * G_l
    
    # 4. Fresnel (Schlick)
    hv = dot(h, v)
    F = F0 + (1 - F0) * ((1 - hv) ** 5)
    
    # 5. Diffuse and Specular Weights
    # Energy conservation: kd = 1 - ks
    ks = F
    kd = 1 - ks
    
    # 6. Components
    f_diffuse = kd * (c / pi)
    f_specular = (D * F * G) / (4 * nv * nl)
    
    # 7. Total Radiance
    # Note: Li is assumed to be pre-calculated radiance including attenuation
    Lo = Le + (f_diffuse + f_specular) * Li * nl
    
    return Lo
```

**Parameters:**
*   $\gamma$ (Roughness): $[0, 1]$. $0$ is perfectly smooth, $1$ is very rough [S2].
*   $F_0$ (Base Reflectivity): $0.04$ for non-metals (dielectrics), equal to albedo $\mathbf{c}$ for metals [S2, S4][S6, unofficial].
*   $\mathbf{c}$ (Albedo): RGB color, typically $[0, 1]$ [S2].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Grazing Angles:** When $\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{v}$ or $\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{l}$ approaches zero, the denominator in the specular term approaches zero. However, $G$ and $F$ also behave such that the term remains stable, but numerical precision issues may arise. Guard against negative dot products (back-face culling) [S2].
*   **Energy Conservation:** Failing to multiply the diffuse term by $(1 - F)$ results in energy creation, leading to overly bright surfaces [S2, S4].
*   **Roughness Mapping:** Ensure $\alpha$ is derived correctly from roughness. Some engines use $\alpha = \text{roughness}^2$, others use linear roughness. The GGX formula requires $\alpha^2$ in the numerator and $\alpha^4$ effectively in the denominator structure [S1, S2].

## Subtopics

### Cook-Torrance GGX BRDF
A variant that specifically employs the anisotropic GGX Normal Distribution Function, Smith height-correlated geometry term, and Schlick Fresnel approximation [S7, unofficial]. It distinguishes itself by supporting anisotropic roughness (different roughness in tangent and bitangent directions) and using a height-correlated geometry model for more accurate shadowing on rough surfaces [S7, unofficial]. Choose this when rendering materials with directional scratches or brushed metals. It is a more advanced version of the standard isotropic Cook-Torrance model.

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** $O(1)$ per light source per pixel. The evaluation involves a constant number of vector operations and trigonometric approximations (dot products, powers).
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(1)$ auxiliary memory.
*   **Performance:** Significantly more expensive than Phong or Blinn-Phong due to the calculation of $D$, $G$, and $F$, but still efficient enough for real-time rendering on modern GPUs [S2, S4].
*   **Strengths:** Physically accurate, energy-conserving, handles a wide range of materials (metals, dielectrics, rough, smooth) with few parameters [S4][S5, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** Does not account for subsurface scattering or complex inter-reflections (requires global illumination techniques like [3D Lookup Table Illumination Model](6930-3d-lookup-table-illumination-model.md) or path tracing). The Schlick approximation for Fresnel is an approximation and may deviate from physical reality at extreme angles or for specific materials [S2].
*   **When to use:** Real-time rendering, PBR pipelines, when physical accuracy is required for surface appearance [S3, S4].
*   **When not to use:** When subsurface scattering is dominant (use [Shading for Semi-Transparent Volumes](2144-shading-for-semi-transparent-volumes.md)), or when performance is critically constrained and artistic control is preferred over physical accuracy.

## Background
The Cook-Torrance BRDF was introduced by Richard L. Cook and Kenneth E. Torrance in 1982 in the paper "A Reflectance Model for Computer Graphics" [S2, S4]. It was one of the first models to apply microfacet theory to computer graphics, providing a physically based explanation for specular highlights. The model was later popularized in real-time rendering through the work of Walter et al. (2007) who introduced the GGX distribution, and Karis (2013) who simplified the Fresnel and geometry terms for real-time engines [S1, S4]. It is now the standard for Physically Based Rendering (PBR) in modern graphics engines and libraries like Jrender [S3].
