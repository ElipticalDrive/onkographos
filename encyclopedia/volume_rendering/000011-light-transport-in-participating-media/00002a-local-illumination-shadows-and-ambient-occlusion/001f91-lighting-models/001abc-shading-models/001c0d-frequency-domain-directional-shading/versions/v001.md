# Frequency Domain Directional Shading

Also called: Frequency domain shading, Gradient moment shading, Gradient-based frequency shading, Hemispherical light source shading, Nusselt's analog shading

## Overview
Frequency Domain Directional Shading is a local illumination technique for volume rendering that computes diffuse shading entirely in the frequency domain, avoiding spatial domain differentiation [S1, S2]. It solves the problem of applying directional lighting cues to volume data without the computational cost or nonlinearity issues of spatial domain methods. Unlike [Simplified Phong Lighting Model](885-simplified-phong-lighting-model.md) and [Lambert Shading with Shadow Rays](1133-lambert-shading-with-shadow-rays.md), which operate in the spatial domain or require shadow rays, this method leverages the property that spatial differentiation corresponds to multiplication by a linear ramp in the frequency domain [S2]. It differs from [Gradient-based Shading with Phase Functions](1020-gradient-based-shading-with-phase-functions.md) by using a hemispherical light source model to maintain linearity, enabling efficient frequency domain computation [S1, S2].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
- Input: Premultiplied volume data $f(x) m(x)$, where $f(x)$ is the density and $m(x)$ is the opacity mask [S1, S2].
- Input: Light direction vector $L$ (unit vector) [S2].
- Input: Interpolation filter $H(s)$ [S1].
- Output: Shaded projection slice $I(x)$ [S1].

**Data Structures:**
- Volume data: 3D grid of complex numbers (real and imaginary parts) representing the Fourier transform of the premultiplied volume [S2].
- Gradient magnitude spectrum: 3D grid of complex numbers representing the Fourier transform of $|\nabla f(x)| m(x)$ [S1, S2].
- Frequency coordinates: 3D grid of frequency vectors $s = [s_x, s_y, s_z]$ [S1].

**Steps:**
1. **Compute Gradient Magnitude Spectrum:**
   - Compute the gradient magnitude $|\nabla f(x)|$ in the spatial domain from the volume data $f(x)$ [S1, S2].
   - Multiply by the opacity mask $m(x)$ to get $|\nabla f(x)| m(x)$ [S1, S2].
   - Compute the 3D Fourier Transform to obtain $F\{|\nabla f(x)| m(x)\}$ [S1, S2].
   - Note: There is no simple frequency domain representation for gradient magnitude, so this requires a separate volume extraction [S1, S2, S3].

2. **Reconstruct Pure Spectrum:**
   - Compute the 3D Fourier Transform of the premultiplied volume $f(x) m(x)$ to get $F\{f(x) m(x)\}$ [S1, S2].
   - Apply the interpolation filter $H(s)$ to reconstruct the pure spectrum of $f(x)$ [S1, S2]. This step compensates for the filter response during slice extraction [S1, S3].

3. **Compute First Moment for Directional Shading:**
   - The directional shading term involves the gradient $\nabla f(x)$. By the derivative theorem, the gradient in the spatial domain corresponds to the first moment in the frequency domain [S3].
   - Multiply the pure spectrum $F\{f(x)\}$ by $i \pi s$ (where $s$ is the frequency vector) to obtain the spectrum of the gradient $\nabla f(x)$ [S1, S2, S3].
   - Specifically, the term $\nabla f(x) \cdot L$ is computed by multiplying the spectrum by $i \pi (s \cdot L)$ [S1, S2].

4. **Combine Shading Terms:**
   - The hemispherical light source model provides a linear diffuse term: $E_i = L_{dif} \frac{1}{2} (1 + N \cdot L)$ [S2, S3].
   - For volumes, the normal $N$ is approximated by the normalized gradient direction, but the model uses the gradient magnitude $|\nabla f(x)|$ and the gradient vector $\nabla f(x)$ directly [S1, S2, S3].
   - The shaded contribution is computed as:
     $$ C_{dif} L_{dif} \frac{1}{2} \left( |\nabla f(x)| + (\nabla f(x) \cdot L) \right) $$
   - In the frequency domain, this corresponds to combining the spectrum of $|\nabla f(x)| m(x)$ and the moment term of $F\{f(x)\}$ [S1, S2, S3].
   - The ambient term $C_{amb} L_{amb} f(x)$ is added separately [S2, S3].

5. **Inverse Transform and Projection:**
   - Perform the inverse Fourier transform to return to the spatial domain for the slice being extracted [S1, S2].
   - The final pixel value is the sum of the ambient and diffuse terms [S2, S3].

**Formulas:**
- Hemispherical irradiance: $E_i = L_{dif} \frac{1}{2} (1 + N \cdot L)$ [S2, S3].
- Volume diffuse term: $C_{dif} L_{dif} \frac{1}{2} (|\nabla f(x)| + (\nabla f(x) \cdot L))$ [S1, S2, S3].
- Frequency domain differentiation: Multiplication by $i \pi s$ in frequency domain equals differentiation in spatial domain [S2].

**Parameters:**
- Light direction $L$: Usually set perpendicular to the view vector $V$ to maximize shading effect and eliminate the adjustment term in combined depth cueing/shading [S2].
- $C_{dif}$: Diffuse color constant [S2].
- $L_{dif}$: Directional light intensity constant [S2].
- $C_{amb}$: Ambient color constant [S2].
- $L_{amb}$: Ambient light intensity constant [S2].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
- **Gradient Magnitude Computation:** The gradient magnitude $|\nabla f(x)|$ cannot be computed simply in the frequency domain, requiring a second spectrum $F\{|\nabla f(x)| m(x)\}$ and thus two slice extractions from two volumes [S1, S2, S3].
- **Negative Values:** A linear shading equation such as $C_{amb} L_{amb} f(x) + C_{dif} L_{dif} \nabla f(x)$ can be derived but requires restricting the upper bound of $C_{dif}$ to avoid negative values, which restricts the shading effect [S1, S2, S3].
- **Sine Wave Depth Cueing:** If depth cueing is implemented as a sine wave, the filter $H'$ requires shifting $H$ and adding three copies with complex weights, increasing filter kernel size [S1].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
def frequency_domain_directional_shading(volume_data, light_direction, view_direction, interpolation_filter):
    """
    Compute directional shading in the frequency domain.
    
    Args:
        volume_data: 3D array of complex numbers representing the Fourier transform of f(x) * m(x)
        light_direction: Unit vector L
        view_direction: Unit vector V
        interpolation_filter: Function H(s) that returns the filter value at frequency s
        
    Returns:
        shaded_slice: 2D array of complex numbers representing the shaded slice
    """
    # Step 1: Compute gradient magnitude spectrum
    # Note: This requires a separate volume extraction because gradient magnitude 
    # cannot be computed simply in the frequency domain [S1, S2, S3].
    gradient_magnitude_spatial = compute_gradient_magnitude(volume_data)
    gradient_magnitude_spectrum = fourier_transform(gradient_magnitude_spatial)
    
    # Step 2: Reconstruct pure spectrum
    # Apply interpolation filter H(s) to reconstruct the pure spectrum of f(x) [S1, S2]
    pure_spectrum = volume_data * interpolation_filter(frequency_coordinates)
    
    # Step 3: Compute first moment for directional shading
    # Multiply by i * pi * s to get the gradient spectrum [S1, S2, S3]
    gradient_spectrum = pure_spectrum * (1j * np.pi * frequency_coordinates)
    
    # Step 4: Combine shading terms
    # The diffuse term is C_dif * L_dif * 0.5 * (|grad f| + (grad f . L)) [S1, S2, S3]
    # In frequency domain, grad f . L is obtained by multiplying gradient_spectrum by (L . s) / |s|? 
    # Actually, the term (grad f . L) is the dot product of the gradient vector with L.
    # In frequency domain, this corresponds to multiplying the spectrum by i * pi * (s . L) [S1, S2]
    
    # Compute the directional component: grad f . L
    directional_component_spectrum = pure_spectrum * (1j * np.pi * np.dot(frequency_coordinates, light_direction))
    
    # Combine with gradient magnitude spectrum
    # Note: gradient_magnitude_spectrum is already in frequency domain [S1, S2]
    shaded_spectrum = gradient_magnitude_spectrum + directional_component_spectrum
    
    # Step 5: Inverse transform to get spatial domain shading
    shaded_spatial = inverse_fourier_transform(shaded_spectrum)
    
    # Add ambient term (simplified, assuming ambient is just f(x))
    ambient_spatial = inverse_fourier_transform(volume_data)
    final_slice = ambient_spatial + shaded_spatial
    
    return final_slice
```

## Subtopics

### [Hemisphere Illumination](1384-hemisphere-illumination.md)
A local shading model that simulates diffuse reflection from a virtual hemisphere light source to provide directional illumination cues. It enhances shape perception by weighting volume points based on orientation relative to a light direction. Unlike standard volume rendering, it uses a hemisphere light source for directional illumination, though it remains a local approximation. It builds on Frequency Domain Directional Shading by computing directional shading in the frequency domain using an orientation field.

## Operating characteristics
- **Time Complexity:** The method operates entirely in the frequency domain and requires computations only on the plane of the slice being extracted [S1, S2]. Computing a moment incurs a few additional floating-point operations per sample on the slice, which is small relative to the convolution operations [S1, S2].
- **Memory Complexity:** Requires a pair of floating-point numbers for each voxel due to the wide dynamic range and complex arithmetic of Fourier transforms [S2]. Additionally, it requires a second spectrum for the gradient magnitude, effectively doubling the memory requirement for the volume data during processing [S1, S2, S3].
- **Measured Performance:** The additional expense of computing the moment is small relative to the convolution operations [S1, S2]. However, the need for two slice extractions (one for the volume and one for the gradient magnitude) increases the overall computational cost [S1, S2, S3].
- **Strengths:** Avoids the nonlinearity of the MAX function used in Lambertian reflection, allowing computation entirely in the frequency domain [S1, S2]. Efficient for large datasets as it leverages FFTs [S2].
- **Limitations:** Requires two slice extractions due to the lack of a simple frequency domain representation for gradient magnitude [S1, S2, S3]. The shading effect is restricted if a linear shading equation is used to avoid negative values [S1, S2, S3].
- **When to Use:** Suitable for volume rendering where directional shading cues are important and computational efficiency in the frequency domain is desired.
- **When Not to Use:** When memory is constrained or when the overhead of two slice extractions is prohibitive.

## Background
- **Origin:** Developed by Takashi Totsuka and Marc Levoy [S1, S2, S3].
- **History:** Presented in the paper "Frequency Domain Volume Rendering" at SIGGRAPH 1993 [S1, S2, S3].
- **Development:** The method builds on previous work in frequency domain volume rendering, extending it to include directional shading using a hemispherical light source model [S1, S2, S3].
- **Usage:** Used in volume rendering applications where efficient directional shading is required, particularly in medical imaging and scientific visualization [S1, S2, S3].
