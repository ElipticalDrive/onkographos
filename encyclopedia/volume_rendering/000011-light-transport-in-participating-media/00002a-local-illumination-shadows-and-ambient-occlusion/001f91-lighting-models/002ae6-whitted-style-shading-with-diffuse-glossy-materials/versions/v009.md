# Whitted-Style Shading with Diffuse/Glossy Materials

Also called: Diffuse Reflection, Lambertian Shading with Shadow Rays, Lambertian reflection, Volume Shading with Shadow Rays, Whitted-Style Shading

## Overview
Whitted-Style Shading with Diffuse/Glossy Materials evaluates illumination at a surface point by combining ambient lighting with direct illumination from light sources, using shadow rays to determine visibility [S2, unofficial]. It explicitly separates the computation of diffuse (Lambertian) and glossy (specular) reflection components, whereas [Shading models](837-shading-models.md) provides a broader taxonomy, [Shading for Semi-Transparent Volumes](2144-shading-for-semi-transparent-volumes.md) handles volume attenuation, and [Cook-Torrance BRDF](2509-cook-torrance-brdf.md) uses a more complex microfacet model for speculars [S1, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs:**
- Ray origin $\mathbf{o}$ and direction $\mathbf{d}$ [G].
- Scene geometry (objects with materials) and light sources [G].
- Material properties: diffuse coefficient $k_d$, glossy coefficient $k_s$, albedo $\mathbf{C}_{albedo}$, shininess exponent $\alpha$ [G].

**Outputs:**
- Final color $\mathbf{C}_{final}$ for the ray [G].

**Data Structures:**
- `Ray`: Contains origin $\mathbf{o}$, direction $\mathbf{d}$, min/max $t$ bounds, and depth [S2, unofficial].
- Intersection data: Contains intersection point $\mathbf{p}$, surface normal $\mathbf{n}$, material reference, and distance $t$ [G].

**Steps:**
1. **Intersection Test:** Find the closest intersection between the input ray and the scene geometry. If no intersection is found, return the background color [S2, S3, unofficial]. If the hit is invalid, return a default background color, e.g., $\{0.2, 0.6, 1.0\}$ [S3, unofficial].
2. **Transform to World Space:** If the intersection is in object space, transform the normal $\mathbf{n}_{obj}$ and position $\mathbf{p}_{obj}$ to world space using the object-to-world matrix $\mathbf{M}_{obj}$ [S3, unofficial]:
   $$ \mathbf{n} = \text{normalize}(\mathbf{M}_{obj} \mathbf{n}_{obj}) $$
   $$ \mathbf{p} = \mathbf{M}_{obj} \mathbf{p}_{obj} $$
3. **Ambient Contribution:** Calculate the ambient term. Implementations vary: one approach multiplies a constant factor by the diffuse coefficient, e.g., $\{0.25, 0.25, 0.25\} \cdot k_d$ [S2, unofficial]. Another adds a small constant multiplied by the albedo, e.g., $0.003 \cdot \mathbf{C}_{albedo}$ [S3, unofficial].
4. **Direct Illumination Loop:** For each light source in the scene:
   a. **Light Direction:** Compute the vector $\mathbf{L}$ from the hit point $\mathbf{p}$ to the light position $\mathbf{p}_L$:
      $$ \mathbf{L} = \mathbf{p}_L - \mathbf{p} $$
      Compute the inverse squared distance:
      $$ d^{-2} = \frac{1}{\|\mathbf{L}\|^2} $$
      Normalize the direction:
      $$ \mathbf{l} = \frac{\mathbf{L}}{\|\mathbf{L}\|} $$
   b. **Shadow Ray:** Cast a shadow ray from $\mathbf{p}$ towards the light. To prevent self-intersection (shadow acne), offset the origin slightly along the normal:
      $$ \mathbf{o}_{shadow} = \mathbf{p} + \epsilon \mathbf{n}, \quad \epsilon \approx 10^{-4} $$
      The shadow ray direction is $\mathbf{l}$ [S3, unofficial]. The maximum $t$ is set to the distance to the light $\|\mathbf{L}\|$ [S2, unofficial], though some implementations use an unbounded maximum distance [S3, unofficial].
   c. **Visibility Check:** Trace the shadow ray. If it hits an object before reaching the light, the point is in shadow ($\mathbf{C}_{direct} = \mathbf{0}$) [S3, unofficial].
   d. **Diffuse Calculation:** If not occluded, compute the Lambertian diffuse reflection. The BRDF $f_r$ is constant for diffuse surfaces, related to reflectance $\rho$ by $\rho = \pi f_r$ [S1, unofficial]. The reflected radiance is:
      $$ \mathbf{C}_{diffuse} = k_d \cdot \mathbf{C}_{albedo} \cdot \max(\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{l}, 0) \cdot I_L \cdot d^{-2} $$
      where $I_L$ is the light emission intensity [S3, unofficial].
   e. **Glossy Calculation:** If the material has a glossy component, compute the specular highlight. First, compute the perfect reflection vector $\mathbf{R}$:
      $$ \mathbf{R} = 2(\mathbf{n} \cdot (-\mathbf{v}))\mathbf{n} - (-\mathbf{v}) $$
      where $\mathbf{v}$ is the view direction (from $\mathbf{p}$ to camera) [G]. The glossy term is:
      $$ \mathbf{C}_{glossy} = k_s \cdot I_L \cdot d^{-2} \cdot \max(\mathbf{R} \cdot \mathbf{v}, 0)^\alpha $$
      where $\alpha$ is the shininess exponent [S1, unofficial].
5. **Accumulate:** Sum the ambient, diffuse, and glossy contributions:
   $$ \mathbf{C}_{final} = \mathbf{C}_{ambient} + \sum_{lights} (\mathbf{C}_{diffuse} + \mathbf{C}_{glossy}) $$

**Parameters:**
- $\alpha$ (shininess): Controls the sharpness of glossy highlights. Higher values yield narrower highlights [S1, unofficial].
- $\epsilon$ (shadow offset): Prevents self-intersection artifacts. Typical value $10^{-4}$ [S3, unofficial].
- Light position: Typical value $\{-3.0, 2.0, 5.0\}$ [S3, unofficial].
- Light emission: Typical value $20.0$ [S3, unofficial].
- Albedo: Typical value $\{1.0, 1.0, 1.0\}$ [S3, unofficial].

**Edge Cases:**
- **Self-Intersection:** Without the offset $\epsilon$, shadow rays may immediately hit the surface they originated from, causing incorrect shadows [S3, unofficial].
- **Zero Distance:** If the light is at the hit point, $d^{-2}$ becomes infinite. Clamp or handle light positions carefully [G].
- **Back-facing Normals:** Ensure $\max(\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{l}, 0)$ handles cases where the light is behind the surface [G].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
def whitted_shading(ray: Ray, scene: Scene, lights: List[Light], material: Material) -> vec3:
    # 1. Intersection Test
    hit = scene.trace(ray)
    if not hit.valid:
        return vec3(0.2, 0.6, 1.0)  # Background color [S3, unofficial]
    
    # 2. Transform to World Space [S3, unofficial]
    p = scene.object_to_world @ hit.p
    n = normalize(scene.object_to_world[:3, :3] @ hit.ng)
    
    color = vec3(0.0)
    
    # 3. Ambient Contribution [S2, S3, unofficial]
    color += vec3(0.25) * material.diffuse_coefficient
    color += 0.003 * material.albedo
    
    # 4. Direct Illumination Loop
    for light in lights:
        L = light.position - p
        inv_dd = 1.0 / dot(L, L)
        l = normalize(L)
        
        # 4b. Shadow Ray [S2, S3, unofficial]
        shadow_origin = p + 1e-4 * n
        shadow_ray = Ray(shadow_origin, l, 0.0, sqrt(dot(L, L)))  # Distance to light [S2, unofficial]
        occluded = scene.trace_any(shadow_ray)
        
        if not occluded:
            # 4d. Diffuse Calculation [S1, S3, unofficial]
            diff = max(dot(n, l), 0.0) * light.emission * inv_dd * material.albedo
            color += diff
            
            # 4e. Glossy Calculation [S1, unofficial]
            if material.has_glossy:
                v = -ray.direction
                R = 2 * dot(n, -v) * n - (-v)
                spec = max(dot(R, v), 0.0) ** material.shininess
                color += spec * light.emission * inv_dd * material.specular_coefficient
                
    return color
```

## Subtopics

### [Color Ramp Modulation](14791-color-ramp-modulation.md)
Color Ramp Modulation is a shading technique that alters the diffuse and specular color components of a material by sampling a 1D color ramp texture, using light intensity values as texture coordinates. It differs from standard Whitted-style shading by introducing non-linear color mapping based on illumination intensity, allowing for stylized or physically-inspired color shifts (e.g., darkening shadows or brightening highlights) without changing the underlying light transport model. It builds on [Whitted-Style Shading with Diffuse/Glossy Materials](4896-whitted-style-shading-with-diffuse-glossy-materials.md).

### [Inter-reflection Modeling](15367-inter-reflection-modeling.md)
Inter-reflection Modeling is a technique for approximating indirect illumination in inverse rendering by tracing a single ray from a surface point in its reflective direction to a secondary intersection point, computing the radiance at that point, and blending it into the primary point's output. Unlike methods such as IRON and WildLight that assume collocated camera and light sources, this approach accommodates variations in both incident and outgoing light directions, making it suitable for non-collocated capture setups.

### Direct Illumination with Shadow Rays
A simple shading model that returns either emissive radiance or diffuse reflectance based on the material properties of the intersected object, using shadow rays to determine visibility [S2, unofficial].

### Glossy Reflection
Models reflections on rough surfaces by distributing outgoing rays around the perfect mirror reflection direction, controlled by a shininess exponent $\alpha$ [S1, unofficial]. The outgoing radiance follows $L_r(\vec{\omega}_r) = L_r d\omega_r(\max(\vec{R} \cdot \vec{\omega}_r), 0)^\alpha$, where $\vec{R}$ is the perfect mirror reflection vector and $\vec{\omega}_r$ is the view direction [S1, unofficial].

### Ray Reflection Tracing
Traces reflected rays to simulate mirror-like reflections, limited to a maximum recursion depth (e.g., 1 bounce) [S4, unofficial]. The main shade function calculates the reflection direction `rd = -normalize(reflect(p, n))` and adds reflected color weighted by a specular coefficient [S4, unofficial].

### Water Reflection
Water reflects the sky it is under rather than a static authored color, making low sun angles optimal for lake visuals [S5, unofficial]. Swimming underwater limits sight distance to 34 meters and removes red light from the scene [S5, unofficial].

### Pre-computed Normal Shading
An alternative lighting mode using quantized, pre-computed normals stored in a second Mixture Graph, as opposed to on-the-fly computed opacity gradients using discrete differences [S6].

### Phong Shading for Hybrid Surfaces
Computes per-pixel normals for a hybrid surface consisting of an initial isosurface and a newly generated cut surface, using a Phong fragment shader to ensure illumination quality [S7, unofficial]. Normals are interpolated at vertices for the initial isosurface, approximated from volume gradients for cut triangles in tetrahedra containing the isosurface, and handled separately for other cut triangles [S7, unofficial].

### Monte Carlo Shading
Approximates the rendering equation via stochastic estimation, targeting mirror-like objects and higher frequency integrands at higher computational cost [S8, unofficial]. MC estimators can exhibit high variance and produce noisy pixel gradients at low sample counts, requiring $N > 1000$ samples for rougher surfaces [S8, unofficial]. MC is preferred for metallic surfaces ($\beta \to 0$) because reflected rays are concentrated, while spherical Gaussian shading is preferred for rough surfaces ($\beta \to 1$) [S8, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
- **Time Complexity:** Not given in the sources.
- **Memory Complexity:** Not given in the sources.
- **Strengths:** Simple to implement, fast for scenes with few lights [G].
- **Limitations:** No indirect illumination [G], noisy for rough surfaces if using MC variants [S8, unofficial], limited recursion depth for reflections [S4, unofficial].
- **When to use:** Real-time rendering with static lighting, simple materials, or as a base for more complex models [G].
- **When not to use:** Scenes requiring soft shadows, global illumination, or highly complex material interactions [G].

## Background
Originated from Whitted's 1980 paper "An Improved Illumination Model for Shaded Display" [G]. It is a foundational technique in ray tracing, extending the basic ray-object intersection to include reflection and refraction [G]. In volume rendering, it is adapted to handle local illumination on volume boundaries or implicit surfaces [S3, unofficial].
