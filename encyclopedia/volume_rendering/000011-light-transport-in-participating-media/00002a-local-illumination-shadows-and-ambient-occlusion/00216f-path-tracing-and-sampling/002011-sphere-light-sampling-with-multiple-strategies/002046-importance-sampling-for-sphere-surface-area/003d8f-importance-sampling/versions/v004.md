# Importance Sampling
## Overview
Importance sampling is a Monte Carlo integration technique that accelerates convergence by sampling from a probability density function (PDF) $p(x)$ that approximates the shape of the integrand $f(x)$ [S1, unofficial]. It concentrates computational effort on regions that contribute significantly to the integral, reducing variance compared to uniform sampling [S1, unofficial].
## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
- Input: An integrand function $f(x)$ over a domain $\Omega$, and a target PDF $p(x)$ [G].
- Output: An unbiased estimator of $\int_{\Omega} f(x) dx$ [G].

**Data Structures:**
- No specific complex data structures are required; the method relies on a random number generator capable of sampling from $p(x)$ [G].

**Steps:**
1. **Select PDF:** Choose $p(x)$ such that $p(x) \approx c \cdot f(x)$ for some constant $c$, with $p(x) > 0$ whenever $f(x) > 0$ [G].
2. **Sample:** Draw $N$ independent samples $X_1, \dots, X_N$ from $p(x)$ [G].
3. **Estimate:** Compute the Monte Carlo estimator $F_N$ [S1, unofficial]:
   $$ F_N = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{f(X_i)}{p(X_i)} $$

**Formulas:**
- The estimator is $F_N = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{f(X_i)}{p(X_i)}$ [S1, unofficial].
- In the ideal case where $p(x) = c f(x)$, the ratio $\frac{f(X_i)}{p(X_i)}$ becomes constant $\frac{1}{c}$, resulting in zero variance [S1, unofficial].

**Parameters:**
- $N$: The number of samples. The convergence rate is $O(1/\sqrt{N})$ [S1, unofficial].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
- If $p(x) = 0$ where $f(x) > 0$, the estimator is undefined or biased [G].
- If $p(x)$ poorly approximates $f(x)$, variance reduction is minimal compared to uniform sampling [G].
- Precision issues may arise if $p(X_i)$ is extremely small, leading to large weights [G].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
def importance_sampling_estimator(f, p_pdf, p_sample, N):
    total = 0.0
    for _ in range(N):
        x = p_sample()
        f_val = f(x)
        p_val = p_pdf(x)
        if p_val == 0:
            continue
        total += f_val / p_val
    return total / N
```
[G] The pseudocode assumes `p_sample` returns a valid sample and `p_pdf` returns the density. Implementers typically generate `p_sample` using inverse transform sampling or rejection sampling based on the analytical form of $p(x)$.

## Operating characteristics
- **Time Complexity:** $O(N)$, where $N$ is the number of samples [G].
- **Memory Complexity:** $O(1)$ auxiliary space per sample if processed sequentially [G].
- **Performance:** Convergence rate is $O(1/\sqrt{N})$, independent of dimensionality [S1, unofficial].
- **Strengths:** Accelerates computation for integrals with sharp peaks or localized contributions [G].
- **Limitations:** Requires finding a suitable $p(x)$ that is both easy to sample from and close to $f(x)$ [G].
- **When to use:** When the integrand has known high-contribution regions (e.g., light directions in rendering) [S1, unofficial].
- **When not to use:** When no good approximation of $f(x)$ is available, or when $f(x)$ is smooth and uniform sampling is sufficient [G].

## Background
The technique is used in computer graphics to accelerate light transport simulation by sampling directions that contribute significantly to the final image [S1, unofficial]. It leverages the fact that sampling from a distribution similar to the integrand reduces the variance of the estimator, leading to faster convergence [S1, unofficial].
