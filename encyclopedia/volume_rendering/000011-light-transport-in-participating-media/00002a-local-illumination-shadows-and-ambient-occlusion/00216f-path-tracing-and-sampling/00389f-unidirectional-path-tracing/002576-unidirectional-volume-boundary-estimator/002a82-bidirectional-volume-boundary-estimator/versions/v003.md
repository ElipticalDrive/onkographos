# Bidirectional Volume Boundary Estimator

Also called: Bidirectional estimator, Multi-directional volume boundary sampling

## Overview
A Monte Carlo estimator that samples volume boundary paths by first drawing the volume boundary vertex, then constructing two subpaths to connect it to the light source and the detector. It sets itself apart from its parent, the [Unidirectional Volume Boundary Estimator](3538-unidirectional-volume-boundary-estimator.md), by reversing the construction order: instead of building a main path from the detector and sampling side paths to the boundary, it samples the boundary vertex first and connects it bidirectionally to both light and camera. This approach is conceptually similar to the multi-directional technique by Zhang et al. [ZMY* 20] for sampling ordinary boundary paths, but applied specifically to volume boundary paths [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs:**
- Scene geometry and participating media properties.
- Light source distribution.
- Camera position and lens parameters.

**Outputs:**
- Radiance estimate for a pixel.

**Data Structures:**
- Volume boundary vertex $p^B$: A point on the interface between the volume and the background (or another medium).
- Subpaths: Two separate path segments originating from $p^B$.

**Steps:**
1. **Sample Boundary Vertex:** Draw a volume boundary vertex $p^B$ from the volume boundary path space. The sampling distribution must account for the geometry and visibility of the boundary [S1].
2. **Construct Light Subpath:** From $p^B$, construct a subpath to the light source using unidirectional sampling with next-event estimation (NEE). This involves sampling directions towards the light and evaluating the transmittance and scattering contributions along the segment [S1].
3. **Construct Camera Subpath:** From $p^B$, construct a subpath to the detector (camera) using unidirectional sampling with next-event estimation (NEE). This involves sampling directions towards the camera lens and evaluating the transmittance and scattering contributions [S1].
4. **Combine:** Combine the contributions from both subpaths to form the final radiance estimate for the boundary path [S1].

**Formulas:**
The estimator computes the contribution of a boundary path $\tilde{\mathbf{p}}$ as:
$$ L = \frac{f(\tilde{\mathbf{p}}) v_{\perp}(\mathbf{p}_K)}{\text{pdf}(\tilde{\mathbf{p}})} $$
where $f(\tilde{\mathbf{p}})$ is the path throughput, $v_{\perp}(\mathbf{p}_K)$ is the projected area factor, and $\text{pdf}(\tilde{\mathbf{p}})$ is the probability density of sampling the boundary vertex and the subsequent subpaths [S1].

**Parameters:**
- Sampling distribution for $p^B$: Not specified in the sources. [G] Typically involves uniform sampling over the boundary surface area weighted by visibility.
- Next-event estimation: Standard NEE parameters apply.

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
- Visibility: Ensuring that the subpaths from $p^B$ to the light and camera are valid (i.e., not occluded by other geometry) is critical. [G] Standard ray tracing techniques should be used to check visibility.
- Degenerate boundaries: If the boundary vertex lies on a concave corner or edge, sampling may become unstable. [G] Careful handling of boundary geometry is required.

**Pseudocode:**
```python
def bidirectional_volume_boundary_estimator(scene, light, camera):
    # Step 1: Sample a volume boundary vertex pB
    pB = sample_volume_boundary(scene)
    
    # Step 2: Construct subpath from pB to light
    # Using unidirectional sampling with next-event estimation
    light_subpath = unidirectional_subpath_to_light(pB, scene, light)
    
    # Step 3: Construct subpath from pB to detector
    # Using unidirectional sampling with next-event estimation
    camera_subpath = unidirectional_subpath_to_camera(pB, scene, camera)
    
    # Step 4: Combine contributions
    # The exact combination formula depends on the specific MIS strategy used
    # For a single estimator, it is simply the sum of the two subpath contributions
    radiance = light_subpath.contribution + camera_subpath.contribution
    
    return radiance
```

## Subtopics

### [Multi-Directional Sampling of Boundary Paths](6393-multi-directional-sampling-of-boundary-paths.md)
Multi-Directional Sampling of Boundary Paths is a Monte Carlo technique for numerically estimating the boundary integral in differentiable rendering by sampling material boundary segments directly, thereby avoiding the prohibitively expensive explicit silhouette detection required by standard edge sampling methods. It sets itself apart from its parent, the Bidirectional Volume Boundary Estimator, by reparameterizing the boundary segment definition from two independent endpoints to an interior point and a direction, guaranteeing that the resulting endpoints lie on each other's visibility boundary by construction.

### [Multiple Importance Sampling for Boundary Paths](6397-multiple-importance-sampling-for-boundary-paths.md)
This method combines the unidirectional and bidirectional volume boundary estimators using path-level Multiple Importance Sampling (MIS) with the balanced heuristic. It addresses the variance reduction problem inherent in using a single sampling strategy for boundary paths, particularly when dealing with homogeneous translucent objects. Unlike the unidirectional estimator, which builds paths from the detector, or the bidirectional estimator, which samples the boundary vertex first, MIS allows both strategies to contribute to the final estimate, weighting their contributions by their respective sampling probabilities.

## Operating characteristics
- **Time Complexity:** The bidirectional estimator requires sampling a boundary vertex and then constructing two subpaths. The complexity is generally higher than the unidirectional estimator due to the additional subpath construction, but it can provide better coverage of the boundary path space [S1].
- **Memory Complexity:** The memory usage is proportional to the number of vertices in the subpaths. Since two subpaths are constructed, the memory usage is roughly double that of the unidirectional estimator for the same number of samples [S1].
- **Measured Performance:** Not given in the sources.
- **Scaling:** The method scales with the complexity of the scene geometry and the number of participating media. More complex boundaries and media will increase the computational cost [S1].
- **Strengths:**
    - Provides a more balanced sampling of the boundary path space compared to the unidirectional estimator [S1].
    - Conceptually similar to established multi-directional techniques for ordinary boundary paths, suggesting robustness [S1].
- **Limitations:**
    - Higher computational cost due to the construction of two subpaths [S1].
    - Requires careful handling of visibility checks for both subpaths [G].
- **When to use:** Use when the unidirectional estimator suffers from high variance due to poor coverage of the boundary path space, or when the boundary geometry is complex and requires bidirectional sampling for accurate estimation [S1].
- **When not to use:** Use the unidirectional estimator when computational efficiency is paramount and the boundary geometry is simple, or when the scene is dominated by direct lighting from a single direction [S1].

## Background
The bidirectional volume boundary estimator was introduced in the context of path-space differentiable volume rendering. It builds on the unidirectional volume boundary estimator and aims to improve the sampling of volume boundary paths by considering both light and camera directions from the boundary vertex [S1]. The method is conceptually similar to the multi-directional technique by Zhang et al. [ZMY* 20] for sampling ordinary boundary paths, indicating a broader trend in differentiable rendering towards more sophisticated sampling strategies for boundaries [S1].
