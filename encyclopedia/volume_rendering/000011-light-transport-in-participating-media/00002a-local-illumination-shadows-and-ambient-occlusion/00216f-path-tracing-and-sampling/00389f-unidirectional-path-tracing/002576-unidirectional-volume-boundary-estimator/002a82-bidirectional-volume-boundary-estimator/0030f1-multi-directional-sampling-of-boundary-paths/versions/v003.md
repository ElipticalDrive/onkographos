# Multi-Directional Sampling of Boundary Paths

Also called: Interior-to-Boundary Reparameterization for Boundary Estimator, Zhang et al. 2020 boundary sampling

## Overview
Multi-Directional Sampling of Boundary Paths is a Monte Carlo technique for estimating the boundary integral in volume rendering that avoids explicit silhouette detection by decoupling the boundary segment from the source and detector subpaths [S1, S2, unofficial]. It samples a point on the evolving surface and a direction to reconstruct the boundary segment endpoints via ray tracing, guaranteeing that the endpoints lie on each other's visibility boundary by construction [S1, S2, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs**
*   **Input:** Scene geometry $M(\pi)$ defined by a reference map $P$ (or motion $X$) transforming material points to spatial positions; a parameter $\pi$ defining the current state of the geometry [S2, unofficial].
*   **Output:** A boundary path consisting of a boundary segment $\mathbf{p}_{0S}\mathbf{p}_{0D}$, a source subpath $\bar{\mathbf{p}}_S$ connecting $\mathbf{p}_{0S}$ to the light source, and a detector subpath $\bar{\mathbf{p}}_D$ connecting $\mathbf{p}_{0D}$ to the detector [S2, unofficial].

**Data Structures**
*   **Boundary Segment:** Defined by two material points $\mathbf{p}_{0S}, \mathbf{p}_{0D}$ and their corresponding spatial positions $\mathbf{x}_{0S}, \mathbf{x}_{0D}$ [S2, unofficial].
*   **Sampling Variables:** A spatial point $\mathbf{x}_B \in M(\pi)$ and a direction $\boldsymbol{\omega}_B \in S^2$ [S2, unofficial]. The point $\mathbf{x}_B$ is not a vertex of the final path but lies in the interior of the spatial segment $\mathbf{x}_{0S}\mathbf{x}_{0D}$ [S1, S2, unofficial].

**Numbered Steps**
1.  **Sample Boundary Segment Parameters:** Draw a point $\mathbf{x}_B$ on the evolving surface $M(\pi)$ and a direction $\boldsymbol{\omega}_B$ on the unit sphere $S^2$ [S2, unofficial].
2.  **Compute Spatial Endpoints:** Perform ray tracing from $\mathbf{x}_B$ in directions $-\boldsymbol{\omega}_B$ and $\boldsymbol{\omega}_B$ to find the spatial endpoints of the boundary segment:
    $$ \mathbf{x}_{0S} = \text{rayIntersect}(\mathbf{x}_B, -\boldsymbol{\omega}_B) $$
    $$ \mathbf{x}_{0D} = \text{rayIntersect}(\mathbf{x}_B, \boldsymbol{\omega}_B) $$
    By construction, $\mathbf{x}_{0S}$ and $\mathbf{x}_{0D}$ are guaranteed to be on the visibility boundary of each other, eliminating the need for explicit silhouette detection [S1, S2, unofficial].
3.  **Map to Material Space:** Convert the spatial endpoints $\mathbf{x}_{0S}$ and $\mathbf{x}_{0D}$ back to material points $\mathbf{p}_{0S}$ and $\mathbf{p}_{0D}$ using the inverse of the reference map $P$ (or motion $X$) [S2, unofficial].
4.  **Construct Source Subpath:** Using standard path sampling methods (e.g., unidirectional or bidirectional path tracing), construct the source subpath $\bar{\mathbf{p}}_S$ starting from $\mathbf{p}_{0S}$ towards the light source [S1, S2, unofficial].
5.  **Construct Detector Subpath:** Using standard path sampling methods, construct the detector subpath $\bar{\mathbf{p}}_D$ starting from $\mathbf{p}_{0D}$ towards the detector [S1, S2, unofficial].

**Formulas**
The method relies on a change of variables to rewrite the boundary integral. The original integral over material boundary points is transformed into an integral over $\mathbf{x}_B$ and $\boldsymbol{\omega}_B$:

$$ \iiint \hat{f}_S d\mu(\bar{\mathbf{p}}_S) \hat{f}_B J_B(\mathbf{x}_B, \boldsymbol{\omega}_B) \hat{f}_D d\mu(\bar{\mathbf{p}}_D) d\boldsymbol{\omega}_B d\mathbf{x}_B $$

where $\hat{f}_S$ and $\hat{f}_D$ are the source and detector subpath densities, and $\hat{f}_B$ is the boundary term density [S2, unofficial].

The Jacobian determinant $J_B(\mathbf{x}_B, \boldsymbol{\omega}_B)$ is the product of two determinants:
1.  The change of variables from spatial endpoints $(\mathbf{x}_{0S}, \mathbf{x}_{0D})$ to $(\mathbf{x}_B, \boldsymbol{\omega}_B)$.
2.  The change of variables from material points $(\mathbf{p}_{0S}, \mathbf{p}_{0D})$ to spatial ones $(\mathbf{x}_{0S}, \mathbf{x}_{0D})$ [S2, unofficial].

**Parameters**
*   $P$: The reference map of the motion $X$, transforming a material point to its spatial representation [S2, unofficial].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls**
*   **Sampling Difficulty:** Efficiently sampling $\mathbf{x}_B$ and $\boldsymbol{\omega}_B$ remains highly nontrivial [S1, unofficial].
*   **Interior Constraint:** The method assumes that $\mathbf{x}_B$ lies strictly in the interior of the segment $\mathbf{x}_{0S}\mathbf{x}_{0D}$ [S2, unofficial].
*   **Specular Surfaces:** Connecting the resulting boundary segments to lights and cameras is difficult when highly specular surfaces (mirror, glass) exist between the segment's endpoints and the target, resembling the Specular-Diffuse-Specular light path sampling problem [S3].

## Subtopics

### Path Segment Edge Sampling
An alternative method by Zhang et al. that samples a path segment directly from an edge and connects it to cameras and light sources, avoiding per-shading-point edge selection [S3]. It randomly selects a point and direction on an edge to form a segment, then selects directions such that all points on the direction recognize the edge as a silhouette [S3]. It uses a hash grid where cell importance is estimated using photon mapping to select edges efficiently [S3]. A key pitfall is the difficulty of connecting edge segments to lights/cameras when highly specular surfaces exist between the segment's endpoints and the target, resembling the Specular-Diffuse-Specular light path sampling problem [S3].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Avoids the prohibitively expensive explicit silhouette detection required by methods like Li et al. (2018), which must detect silhouettes at each vertex of a light path [S1, unofficial]. However, efficient sampling of $\mathbf{x}_B$ and $\boldsymbol{\omega}_B$ remains highly nontrivial [S1, unofficial].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Not given in the sources.
*   **Measured Performance:** Not given in the sources.
*   **Strengths:** Guarantees that endpoints are on each other's visibility boundary by construction, simplifying the boundary segment sampling process [S1, S2, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** The method does not solve the problem of connecting boundary segments to lights/cameras in the presence of highly specular surfaces [S3].
*   **When to use:** When explicit silhouette detection is too expensive for complex geometry [S1, unofficial].
*   **When not to use:** When the scene contains many highly specular surfaces between the boundary segment and the light/camera, as connection becomes difficult [S3].

## Background
The method was proposed by Zhang et al. in 2020 as part of path-space differentiable rendering [S1, S2, unofficial][S3]. It addresses the challenge of estimating the boundary integral in differentiable rendering without the computational cost of Monte Carlo edge sampling, which requires detecting object silhouettes at each vertex of a light path [S1, unofficial]. The technique is a key component of the Multi-Directional Sampling of Boundary Paths approach, enabling efficient sampling of boundary segments by decoupling them from the source and detector subpaths [S2, unofficial].
