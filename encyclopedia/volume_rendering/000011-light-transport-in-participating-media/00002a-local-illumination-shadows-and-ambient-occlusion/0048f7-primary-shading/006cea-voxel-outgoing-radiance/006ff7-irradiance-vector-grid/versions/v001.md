# Irradiance Vector Grid

Also called: Irradiance Vertex Grid, Volumetric Vector-Based Representation

## Overview
The Irradiance Vector Grid (IVG) is a volumetric data structure for caching low-frequency indirect illumination in participating media or diffuse scenes, designed for efficient storage and GPU rendering [S1]. It stores a fixed set of irradiance vectors in a 3D grid to capture geometric and photometric robustness against local variations of geometry and reflectance [S1]. Unlike scalar irradiance caching or Christensen’s cache, which suffer from Voronoi-like artifacts or geometric dependency, IVG uses directional interpolation on a fixed axis-aligned grid to provide smooth, normal-aware indirect lighting [S1].

## How it works

### Inputs and Outputs
**Inputs:**
*   Scene geometry (for photon tracing intersection).
*   Grid dimensions: $N_i \times N_j \times N_k$ (number of voxels in each axis).
*   Number of photons to shoot.
*   Surface diffuse BRDF $\rho_D(p)$.

**Outputs:**
*   Irradiance Vector Grid: A 3D texture array storing irradiance vectors at grid vertices.
*   Rendered image with indirect illumination computed via fragment shader lookup and interpolation.

### Data Structures

**1. Irradiance Vector Grid (Preprocessing/Software)**
*   **Layout:** Axis-aligned uniform rectangular 3D grid.
*   **Vertices:** $v_{ijk}$ where $i \in [0, N_i], j \in [0, N_j], k \in [0, N_k]$.
*   **Storage per Vertex:** Six irradiance vectors, one for each main direction $\delta \in \{+x, -x, +y, -y, +z, -z\}$.
*   **Vector Content:** For each direction $\delta$, an irradiance vector $I_{\delta}^{ijk}$ is stored. Since irradiance is spectral, one vector is stored per color channel (forming an irradiance matrix). Total 54 floating-point numbers per vertex ($3 \text{ channels} \times 6 \text{ directions} \times 3 \text{ components}$).

**2. Irradiance Vector Grid (GPU Texture Storage)**
To reduce texture fetches, the data is compressed into two 16-bit 3D textures per direction (12 textures total for all 6 directions) [S1]:
*   **Color Vector Texture:** Stores the magnitudes of the irradiance vectors for each color channel.
    *   $r = \|I_R\|$
    *   $g = \|I_G\|$
    *   $b = \|I_B\|$
*   **Direction Vector Texture:** Stores the average direction of the combined irradiance.
    *   $d = \frac{I_R + I_G + I_B}{\|I_R + I_G + I_B\|}$

### Definitions and Formulas

**Irradiance Vector:**
For a point $p$ with normal $n$, the irradiance vector $I_n(p)$ is defined as [S1]:
$$ I_n(p) = \int_{\Omega_n} L(p \leftarrow \omega_i) \omega_i d\omega_i $$
where $L(p \leftarrow \omega_i)$ is incident radiance from direction $\omega_i$, $d\omega_i$ is the differential solid angle, and $\Omega_n$ is the hemisphere centered at $p$ oriented toward $n$.

**Reflected Radiance:**
The diffusely reflected radiance $L_r(p \rightarrow \omega_o)$ is calculated from the irradiance vector as [S1]:
$$ L_r(p \rightarrow \omega_o) = \frac{\rho_D(p)}{\pi} \langle I_n(p), n \rangle $$
where $\rho_D$ is the diffuse BRDF and $\langle , \rangle$ denotes a dot product.

**Cell Area ($A_{\delta}$):**
For a uniform rectangular 3D grid, $A_{\delta}$ is the area of the voxel face oriented in direction $\delta$.
*   For interior vertices, $A_{\delta}$ is the full voxel face area.
*   For grid boundary vertices, $A_{\delta}$ is divided by two.
*   For grid corner vertices, $A_{\delta}$ is divided by four [S1].

### Preprocessing: Building the Grid

The grid is built using Photon Tracing. Any global illumination software can be used, but the specific propagation steps are [S1]:

1.  **Photon Emission:** Shoot photons from light sources.
2.  **Scene Intersection:** Use a ray tracing acceleration structure to find the closest intersection with the scene geometry.
3.  **Grid Propagation:** Propagate the photon into the Irradiance Vector Grid without performing intersection tests against the grid itself.
4.  **Face Traversal:** When a photon traverses a voxel face, add its contribution to the irradiance vector $I_{\delta}^{ijk}$ associated with the nearest vertex $v_{ijk}$ and the direction $\delta$ provided by the face normal.
5.  **Surface Reflection:** When a photon hits scene geometry, apply classical stochastic reflection according to the local BRDF.
6.  **Normalization:** After all photons are processed, normalize the vectors. For a vertex $v_{ijk}$ and direction $\delta$, let $N_{\delta}^{ijk}$ be the number of contributing photons. The irradiance vector is computed as [S1]:
    $$ I_{\delta}^{ijk} = \frac{1}{A_{\delta}} \sum_{n=1}^{N_{\delta}^{ijk}} \phi_n \omega_n $$
    where $\phi_n$ is the flux of the $n$-th photon and $\omega_n$ is its direction.

### Rendering: Interpolation and Shading

To compute the indirect illumination for a point $p$ with normal $n$, two interpolation steps are performed [S1]:

**Step 1: Spatial Interpolation**
Obtain the irradiance vector $I_{\delta}(p)$ for specific directions by spatially interpolating the vectors $I_{\delta}^{ijk}$ stored at the grid vertices surrounding $p$.
*   Only three out of six possible directions ($\pm x, \pm y, \pm z$) are interpolated.
*   The choice between $+x$ and $-x$ (resp. $+y/-y$, $+z/-z$) is determined by the sign of the normal component $n_x$ (resp. $n_y, n_z$).
    *   If $n_x > 0$, interpolate $I_{+x}(p)$. Else, interpolate $I_{-x}(p)$.
    *   If $n_y > 0$, interpolate $I_{+y}(p)$. Else, interpolate $I_{-y}(p)$.
    *   If $n_z > 0$, interpolate $I_{+z}(p)$. Else, interpolate $I_{-z}(p)$.
*   Use trilinear or tricubic interpolation for the spatial approximation.

**Step 2: Directional Interpolation**
Remap the three spatially interpolated irradiance vectors ($I_x(p), I_y(p), I_z(p)$) according to the normal direction $n$ to obtain the final irradiance vector $I_n(p)$ [S1]:
$$ I_n(p) = I_x(p) n_x^2 + I_y(p) n_y^2 + I_z(p) n_z^2 $$

**Final Shading:**
Compute the reflected radiance using the formula:
$$ L_r(p \rightarrow \omega_o) = \frac{\rho_D(p)}{\pi} \langle I_n(p), n \rangle $$
This value is added to the direct illumination in the fragment shader.

```python
def normalize_grid(grid):
    for each vertex v_ijk in grid:
        for each direction delta in [+x, -x, +y, -y, +z, -z]:
            A_delta = get_cell_area(v_ijk, delta) # Divided by 2 or 4 at boundaries
            N_delta = grid.count_photons(v_ijk, delta)
            if N_delta > 0:
                sum_flux_direction = grid.sum_flux_direction(v_ijk, delta)
                grid.I[v_ijk][delta] = sum_flux_direction / A_delta

# Rendering: Interpolation
def get_irradiance_vector(p, n, grid):
    # Step 1: Spatial Interpolation
    # Select directions based on normal signs
    if n.x >= 0:
        I_x = grid.interpolate_trilinear(p, direction='+x')
    else:
        I_x = grid.interpolate_trilinear(p, direction='-x')
        
    if n.y >= 0:
        I_y = grid.interpolate_trilinear(p, direction='+y')
    else:
        I_y = grid.interpolate_trilinear(p, direction='-y')
        
    if n.z >= 0:
        I_z = grid.interpolate_trilinear(p, direction='+z')
    else:
        I_z = grid.interpolate_trilinear(p, direction='-z')
        
    # Step 2: Directional Interpolation
    I_n = I_x * (n.x ** 2) + I_y * (n.y ** 2) + I_z * (n.z ** 2)
    
    return I_n

def shade_point(p, n, rho_D, grid):
    I_n = get_irradiance_vector(p, n, grid)
    # Reflected radiance
    L_r = (rho_D / pi) * dot(I_n, n)
    return L_r
```

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **High-Frequency Effects:** The technique is intended only for low-frequency indirect illumination. High-frequency effects (sharp shadows, specular reflections, caustics) cause memory requirements to explode and are not captured correctly [S1]. These must be handled by other techniques (e.g., soft shadow maps, specular environment maps) [S1].
*   **Direct Access Artifacts:** Direct access to cached values (without interpolation) produces objectionable illumination patterns (Voronoi-like zones). Spatial and directional interpolation is required to avoid this [S1].
*   **Noise:** Noise due to undersampling appears mostly on larger planes and higher frequency details such as shadows. Quality scales with the number of photon shots [S1].
*   **Energy Leaks:** Unbiased estimation can lead to energy leaks or under-estimation of irradiance values depending on the relative geometric configuration with the grid. These artifacts are reduced by increasing the grid resolution [S1].
*   **Boundary Conditions:** The cell area $A_{\delta}$ must be correctly adjusted (divided by 2 or 4) for boundary and corner vertices to ensure correct density estimation [S1].

## Operating characteristics

**Time and Memory Complexity:**
*   **Preprocessing:** Grows mainly linearly with the number of photons, with a small overhead for increasing grid size [S1].
*   **Rendering (GPU):** Access time is constant with respect to the number of cached samples, unlike Christensen's kd-tree which is logarithmic [S1]. Complexity is pixel-dependent as interpolation occurs in the fragment stage [S1].
*   **Memory:** Low memory requirements. A 16x16x16 compressed grid requires only 288 KB [S1].

**Measured Performance:**
*   **GPU (Nvidia 7950 GTX):** A 16x16x16 compressed grid (288 KB) with a dragon model (400K triangles) achieves 91 fps with trilinear interpolation and 87 fps with tricubic interpolation at 600x600 resolution [S1].
*   **Software (Direct Illumination Scene):** With 5M photons and a 40x50x40 grid, indirect illumination computation took 51s for IVG vs 5527s for Christensen's cache. Total rendering time was 1341s vs 6618s [S1].
*   **Software (Indirect Illumination Scene):** With 80M photons and a 12x8x20 grid, IVG took 150s precomputation and 2376s reconstruction, vs 466s precomputation and 10050s reconstruction for Christensen's method [S1].
*   **Geometric Robustness:** Using the same grid (20x22x20) and photon count (8M), precomputation on low-detail geometry (24K triangles) took 58s, while high-detail geometry (2M triangles) took 180s. The resulting illuminations had less than 1% maximum pixel difference [S1].

**Strengths and Limitations:**
*   **Strengths:** Geometric robustness against local variations of normal vectors and diffuse reflectance; smoothness everywhere in the 3D scene; low memory consumption; efficient GPU implementation; constant access time [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Cannot capture high-frequency effects; noise from undersampling; potential energy leaks/under-estimation in complex geometries; requires preprocessing [S1].

**When to Use:**
*   Use for low-frequency indirect illumination in real-time rendering or software global illumination where geometric robustness and smoothness are prioritized over high-frequency detail.
*   Use when scene geometry changes moderately, as the fixed grid remains robust.

**When Not to Use:**
*   Do not use for scenes requiring sharp shadows, specular highlights, or caustics.
*   Do not use if direct access to cache is preferred (leads to artifacts).

## Background

**Origin:**
The Irradiance Vector Grid was introduced by Pacanowski, Granier, Poulin, and Schlick in 2009 [S1]. It extends the work of Pacanowski et al. [PRG+ 08, PRL+ 08] and is based on the irradiance vector concept introduced by Arvo [Arv94]. The work was published as INRIA Research Report n° 6983 [S1].

**History and Development:**
The method was developed to address the limitations of irradiance caching (geometric dependency) and radiance caching (geometry-dependent sample placement) by using a fixed 3D grid. It was designed to be easily adaptable to GPU hardware and to provide robustness against local variations of diffuse reflectance and normal vectors [S1].

**Usage:**
It is used as an efficient and high-quality caching data structure for bidirectional rendering techniques and for real-time GPU rendering engines to include global illumination effects [S1].
