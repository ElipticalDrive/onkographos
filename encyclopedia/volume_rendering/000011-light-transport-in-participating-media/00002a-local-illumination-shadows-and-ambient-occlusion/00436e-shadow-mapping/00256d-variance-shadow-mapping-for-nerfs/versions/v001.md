# Variance Shadow Mapping for NeRFs

Also called: NeRF shadow mapping, VSM for NeRFs

## Overview
Variance Shadow Mapping for NeRFs is a technique for rendering cast shadows in Neural Radiance Fields that adapts the variance shadow mapping algorithm from traditional rasterization to implicit neural representations. It solves the problem of computing soft shadows without performing expensive volumetric transmittance integration along shadow rays. Unlike naive shadow mapping which produces hard, aliased edges, this method transforms discontinuous depth information from the light's perspective into continuous visibility probabilities, allowing for smooth soft shadows. It builds on [Shadow Ray Casting](2796-shadow-ray-casting.md) by replacing the ray-casting transmittance integral with a depth-buffer comparison.

## How it works
The method approximates visibility by comparing the depth of the scene point from the camera's view against the depth distribution stored in a shadow map rendered from the light source's view.

**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:**
    *   Camera parameters (intrinsics and extrinsics) for the current view.
    *   Light source position and orientation (defining the virtual camera for the shadow map).
    *   A trained NeRF model (SGs or MLP) capable of querying depth and color.
    *   Shadow map resolution $N \times N$.
*   **Outputs:**
    *   Visibility factor $V(\mathbf{r}) \in [0, 1]$ for each pixel ray $\mathbf{r}$.
    *   Final radiance $I(\mathbf{r})$ incorporating the shadow.

**Data Structures:**
1.  **Shadow Map:** A 2D texture of size $N \times N$. It stores two channels per pixel:
    *   Mean depth $\mu(X)$: The filtered mean depth value.
    *   Mean squared depth $\mu(X^2)$: The filtered mean of the squared depth values.
2.  **Depth Buffer:** A temporary buffer used to render the raw depth $X$ and squared depth $X^2$ from the light view before filtering.

**Steps:**

1.  **Reconstruct 3D Point from Camera View:**
    For each pixel $\mathbf{r}$ in the camera view, retrieve the depth $z(\mathbf{r})$ from the NeRF rendering or depth query. Using the camera intrinsics $K$ and extrinsics $C$, reconstruct the 3D coordinate $\mathbf{p}$ of the surface point:
    $$ \mathbf{p} = C^{-1} \cdot K^{-1} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \cdot z(\mathbf{r}) $$
    where $(u, v)$ are the pixel coordinates. [G] Note: Standard unprojection assumes a pinhole camera model.

2.  **Project to Light View:**
    Transform the 3D point $\mathbf{p}$ into the coordinate system of the virtual camera located at the light source. Let $L$ be the light-to-world transformation matrix. The point in light view is $\mathbf{p}_l = L \cdot \mathbf{p}$. Project $\mathbf{p}_l$ to 2D screen coordinates $(u_l, v_l)$ in the light view using the light's intrinsics $K_l$.

3.  **Render Shadow Map (Light View):**
    Render the scene from the light's perspective to populate the shadow map. This involves two passes or a multi-channel render:
    *   **Pass 1 (Mean Depth):** Render the depth buffer $X$. For each pixel $\mathbf{r}_l$ in the light view, store the depth $z_l(\mathbf{r}_l)$ of the closest surface point.
    *   **Pass 2 (Squared Depth):** Render the squared depth buffer $X^2$. For each pixel $\mathbf{r}_l$, store $z_l(\mathbf{r}_l)^2$.

4.  **Box Filtering:**
    Apply a box filter (uniform average) over a kernel of size $K \times K$ (typically small, e.g., $3 \times 3$ or $5 \times 5$) to both the mean depth map and the squared depth map.
    *   Compute $\mu(X)(\mathbf{r}_l)$: The filtered mean depth at pixel $\mathbf{r}_l$.
    *   Compute $\mu(X^2)(\mathbf{r}_l)$: The filtered mean squared depth at pixel $\mathbf{r}_l$.

5.  **Calculate Variance:**
    Compute the variance $\sigma^2(\mathbf{r}_l)$ for each pixel in the light view using the identity $\text{Var}(X) = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2$:
    $$ \sigma^2(\mathbf{r}_l) = \mu(X^2)(\mathbf{r}_l) - \mu^2(X)(\mathbf{r}_l) $$
    If $\sigma^2(\mathbf{r}_l) < 0$ due to numerical precision, clamp to $0$. [G]

6.  **Compute Visibility:**
    For the original pixel $\mathbf{r}$ in the camera view, map it to the corresponding pixel $\mathbf{r}_l$ in the light view (as determined in Step 2). Retrieve $\mu(X)(\mathbf{r}_l)$ and $\sigma^2(\mathbf{r}_l)$. Compare the original depth $z(\mathbf{r})$ with the filtered mean depth from the light view:
    $$ V(\mathbf{r}) = \begin{cases} 1, & \text{if } z(\mathbf{r}) \leq \mu(X)(\mathbf{r}_l) \\ \frac{\sigma^2(\mathbf{r}_l)}{\sigma^2(\mathbf{r}_l) + (z(\mathbf{r}) - \mu(X)(\mathbf{r}_l))^2}, & \text{if } z(\mathbf{r}) > \mu(X)(\mathbf{r}_l) \end{cases} $$
    This formula is derived from Chebyshev's inequality, providing an upper bound on the probability that the point is occluded.

7.  **Final Rendering:**
    Combine the visibility with the direct illumination and specular components. The final radiance $I(\mathbf{r})$ is:
    $$ I(\mathbf{r}) = I_d(\mathbf{r}) + I_s(\mathbf{r}) + V(\mathbf{r}) \mathcal{R}(\mathbf{r}) S_{SG}(\mathbf{r}) $$
    where $I_d$ is diffuse direct light, $I_s$ is specular light, $\mathcal{R}$ is the reflectance, and $S_{SG}$ is the shadow map contribution or ambient term. [S1]

import numpy as np

def variance_shadow_mapping_for_nerf(nerf_model, camera_params, light_params, shadow_map_size=512, kernel_size=3):
    """
    Computes visibility using Variance Shadow Mapping for NeRFs.
    
    Args:
        nerf_model: Object with method query_depth(ray_o, ray_d) returning depth z.
        camera_params: Dict with 'K' (3x3), 'C' (4x4 extrinsic).
        light_params: Dict with 'K_l' (3x3), 'C_l' (4x4 extrinsic).
        shadow_map_size: Resolution N of the shadow map.
        kernel_size: Size of the box filter kernel.
        
    Returns:
        visibility_map: 2D array of shape (H, W) with values in [0, 1].
    """
    H, W = camera_params['image_height'], camera_params['image_width']
    K = camera_params['K']
    C = camera_params['C']
    K_l = light_params['K_l']
    C_l = light_params['C_l']
    
    # Step 1 & 2: Reconstruct 3D points and project to light view
    # Create grid of pixel coordinates
    u, v = np.meshgrid(np.arange(W), np.arange(H))
    u = u.astype(np.float32)
    v = v.astype(np.float32)
    
    # Homogeneous coordinates in camera space
    cam_coords = np.stack([u, v, np.ones_like(u)], axis=-1) # (H, W, 3)
    
    # Unproject to 3D world coordinates
    # p_cam = K^-1 * [u, v, 1]^T * z
    # p_world = C^-1 * [p_cam, 1]^T
    # We need depth z for each pixel. Query NeRF.
    # Assuming ray_o and ray_d can be derived from camera_params
    ray_o, ray_d = unproject_rays(camera_params)
    depths = np.zeros((H, W))
    for i in range(H):
        for j in range(W):
            # Query NeRF for depth along ray (i, j)
            # This is a simplification; in practice, batched queries are used.
            depths[i, j] = nerf_model.query_depth(ray_o[i, j], ray_d[i, j])
            
    # Convert pixel coords to 3D points
    # p_cam_hom = [u, v, 1]
    # p_cam = inv(K) @ p_cam_hom * z
    inv_K = np.linalg.inv(K)
    cam_coords_hom = np.stack([u, v, np.ones_like(u)], axis=-1) # (H, W, 3)
    cam_coords_hom = np.expand_dims(cam_coords_hom, axis=-1) # (H, W, 3, 1)
    
    # Matrix multiplication: (H, W, 3, 3) @ (H, W, 3, 1) -> (H, W, 3, 1)
    # Using einsum for clarity
    p_cam = np.einsum('ijk,ijlk->ijl', inv_K, cam_coords_hom) * np.expand_dims(depths, axis=-1) # (H, W, 3)
    
    # Convert to homogeneous world coordinates
    # p_world_hom = [x, y, z, 1]
    p_world_hom = np.zeros((H, W, 4))
    p_world_hom[:, :, :3] = p_cam
    p_world_hom[:, :, 3] = 1.0
    
    # Transform to light view
    # p_light_hom = C_l @ p_world_hom
    # Note: C_l is usually World-to-Light. If it's Light-to-World, invert it.
    # Assuming C_l is World-to-Light transformation matrix
    p_light_hom = np.einsum('ij,klj->kli', C_l, p_world_hom) # (H, W, 4)
    
    # Project to light screen coordinates
    # u_l = K_l @ p_light_hom[...,:3] / p_light_hom[...,3]
    p_light_xyz = p_light_hom[:, :, :3]
    p_light_w = p_light_hom[:, :, 3]
    
    # Avoid division by zero
    p_light_w = np.maximum(p_light_w, 1e-6)
    
    p_light_uv_hom = np.einsum('ijk,klj->kli', K_l, p_light_xyz) # (H, W, 3)
    u_l = p_light_uv_hom[:, :, 0] / p_light_w
    v_l = p_light_uv_hom[:, :, 1] / p_light_w
    
    # Step 3: Render Shadow Map (Light View)
    # We need to render depth from the light's perspective for the entire scene.
    # This typically involves rendering the NeRF from the light's view.
    # For simplicity, we assume we have a function render_light_view that returns depth and squared depth maps.
    # In a real implementation, this would involve sampling the NeRF from the light's camera.
    
    # Placeholder for actual NeRF rendering from light view
    # light_depth_map: (N, N)
    # light_depth_sq_map: (N, N)
    light_depth_map, light_depth_sq_map = render_light_view(nerf_model, light_params, shadow_map_size)
    
    # Step 4: Box Filtering
    kernel = np.ones((kernel_size, kernel_size)) / (kernel_size * kernel_size)
    
    mu_X = convolve_2d(light_depth_map, kernel)
    mu_X2 = convolve_2d(light_depth_sq_map, kernel)
    
    # Step 5: Calculate Variance
    variance = mu_X2 - mu_X**2
    variance = np.maximum(variance, 0) # Clamp negative variance due to precision
    
    # Step 6: Compute Visibility
    # Map u_l, v_l to shadow map coordinates
    # Assuming light view is centered and normalized, adjust for shadow map size
    # This mapping depends on the specific projection setup. 
    # Here we assume u_l, v_l are in shadow map pixel coordinates.
    
    # Interpolate mu_X and variance at (u_l, v_l)
    # Using bilinear interpolation
    mu_X_interp = interpolate_2d(mu_X, u_l, v_l)
    variance_interp = interpolate_2d(variance, u_l, v_l)
    
    # Compare depths
    # z(r) is the depth from camera view
    # mu(X)(r_l) is the filtered depth from light view
    
    occluded = depths > mu_X_interp
    
    visibility = np.ones_like(depths)
    
    # Apply VSM formula for occluded pixels
    denom = variance_interp + (depths - mu_X_interp)**2
    # Avoid division by zero
    denom = np.maximum(denom, 1e-6)
    
    visibility[occluded] = variance_interp[occluded] / denom[occluded]
    
    return visibility

**Parameters:**
*   **Shadow Map Resolution ($N$):** Typically $512 \times 512$ or $1024 \times 1024$. Higher resolution reduces aliasing but increases memory and rendering time for the shadow map.
*   **Kernel Size ($K$):** Typically $3 \times 3$ or $5 \times 5$. Larger kernels produce softer shadows but may blur fine details.
*   **Light Position:** Defines the virtual camera. The shadow quality depends on the angle and distance relative to the scene.

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Negative Variance:** Due to floating-point precision errors, $\mu(X^2) - \mu^2(X)$ can be slightly negative. It must be clamped to $0$. [G]
*   **Depth Precision:** NeRF depth queries can be noisy. Filtering helps, but extreme noise may lead to incorrect visibility.
*   **Self-Shadowing:** The method compares depths from different viewpoints. If the light is very close to the surface, small errors in depth reconstruction can cause self-shadowing artifacts.
*   **Occlusion Culling:** The method assumes the depth from the light view represents the closest occluder. If the NeRF rendering from the light view is incomplete or has holes, the shadow map may be incorrect.

## Subtopics

### Hard Shadow Mapping for NeRFs
Produces binary visibility (0 or 1) by comparing raw depth without filtering. Results in aliased, hard-edged shadows. Choose when computational resources are extremely limited and soft shadows are not required. [Link](2796-shadow-ray-casting.md)

### Volumetric Shadow Casting
Computes transmittance by integrating extinction along the shadow ray. Produces physically accurate volumetric shadows but is computationally expensive. Choose when accurate light scattering in participating media is critical. [Link](956-shadow-receiving-for-volumes.md)

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Dominated by the NeRF rendering from the light view ($O(N^2 \cdot S)$ where $S$ is samples per pixel) and the depth query for the camera view ($O(H \cdot W)$). The filtering and visibility calculation are $O(N^2)$ and $O(H \cdot W)$ respectively.
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(N^2)$ for the shadow map (two channels).
*   **Measured Performance:** Significantly faster than volumetric shadow casting as it avoids ray marching for shadows. The performance is comparable to traditional shadow mapping but depends on the speed of NeRF rendering from arbitrary viewpoints.
*   **Strengths:** Produces soft shadows efficiently; no need for complex volumetric integration; compatible with standard NeRF pipelines.
*   **Limitations:** Approximate; shadows may appear blurry or inaccurate if the NeRF depth is noisy; requires rendering the scene from the light's perspective, which may be computationally intensive for complex scenes.
*   **When to Use:** When soft shadows are desired and computational efficiency is important, and the NeRF can be rendered quickly from the light's view.
*   **When Not to Use:** When physically accurate volumetric shadows are required, or when the NeRF depth is highly unreliable.

## Background
Variance Shadow Mapping for NeRFs was introduced in the paper "Relighting Scenes With Object Insertions in Neural Radiance Fields" (2025) [S1]. It adapts the variance shadow mapping technique, originally developed for rasterization in traditional computer graphics, to the context of neural radiance fields. The key insight is to use the depth information from the NeRF to construct a shadow map from the light's perspective, and then use the variance of the depth distribution to estimate visibility probabilities. This approach avoids the need for explicit ray casting and transmittance integration, making it more efficient for NeRF-based rendering.
