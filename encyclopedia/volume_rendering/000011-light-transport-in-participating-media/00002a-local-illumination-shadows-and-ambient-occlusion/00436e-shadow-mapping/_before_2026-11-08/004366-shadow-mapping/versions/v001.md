# Shadow Mapping

Also called: Shadow Map, Depth Buffer Shadowing

## Overview
Shadow Mapping is a family of techniques that evaluate visibility by comparing a point's depth in light space against a pre-rendered depth texture (the shadow map) [S1, unofficial]. It solves the problem of determining which parts of a volume or scene are occluded from a light source without performing expensive ray-marching transmittance calculations for every sample [S1, unofficial]. Unlike [Shadow Ray Casting](2796-shadow-ray-casting.md), which integrates extinction along a ray, Shadow Mapping relies on discrete depth comparisons, often augmented with filtering or statistical bounds to approximate soft shadows or handle specific media like NeRFs.

## Shared principles
All methods in this family share the core mechanism of evaluating visibility or illumination at a specific point $\mathbf{p}$ within or on the boundary of a volume, based on a light source direction $\mathbf{l}$ or environment [G].

1.  **Visibility/Transmittance Evaluation:** The fundamental operation is determining how much light reaches $\mathbf{p}$ from a source [G]. This involves integrating the extinction coefficient $\sigma_t(\mathbf{x})$ along a path from the source to $\mathbf{p}$ (or vice versa) [G]. The transmittance $T$ is calculated as:
    $$ T = \exp\left(-\int_{\mathbf{p}_{source}}^{\mathbf{p}} \sigma_t(\mathbf{x}) \, d\mathbf{x}\right) $$
    In discrete implementations, this integral is approximated by summing $\sigma_t \cdot \Delta s$ over samples along the ray [G].

2.  **Local Shading Models:** For surface-like appearance in volumes, normals are approximated from the volume gradient $\mathbf{g} = \nabla V$ [G]. Usually, a simple absorption and emission model is used because it is relatively cheap to evaluate and gives good enough results [S1, unofficial].

3.  **Shadowing Approximation:** Shadows are computed by testing occlusion along the direction to the light source [G].

4.  **Ambient Occlusion:** Indirect illumination is approximated by integrating over the hemisphere or using simplified models (e.g., upward ray for sky light) [G]. The ambient contribution $L_{ambient}$ is often modulated by a shadowing factor based on local density:
    $$ L_{ambient} = L_{sky} \cdot \exp(-D_{up}) \cdot AO_{factor} $$
    where $D_{up}$ is the integrated density along an upward ray [G].

## Subtopics

### [Shadow Receiving for Volumes](956-shadow-receiving-for-volumes.md)
Shadow Receiving for Volumes is a technique that allows volumetric data to receive shadows cast by polygonal actors without requiring the volume to be rendered twice. It solves the performance bottleneck associated with traditional shadow pipelines, where rendering a volume as a shadow caster or receiver typically incurs significant overhead due to double traversal. Unlike sibling methods that compute self-shadowing or light-space transmittance buffers, this method integrates shadow occlusion checks directly into the view-space ray traversal against a pre-computed shadow map. It is distinct from [Lookup Table Shadow Rendering](3588-lookup-table-shadow-rendering.md) and [Ray Marching with Shadow Mapping](1415-ray-marching-with-shadow-mapping.md) by relying on standard shadow map textures rather than 3D transmittance textures or light-strength buffers.

### [Variance Shadow Mapping for NeRFs](3561-variance-shadow-mapping-for-nerfs.md)
Variance Shadow Mapping for NeRFs is a technique for rendering cast shadows in neural radiance fields that avoids the computational cost of ray-marching transmittance along shadow rays. Inspired by traditional variance shadow mapping, it computes shadow visibility by comparing the depth of a surface point (from the camera view) against a statistical distribution of depths rendered from a virtual light source. It produces soft, continuous shadows in the range $[0, 1]$, distinguishing it from naive shadow mapping which yields hard edges, and from direct shadow ray casting which requires expensive cumulative transmittance calculations.

### [PCF Shadow Lookup with Slope Scaling and Edge Fade](10994-pcf-shadow-lookup-with-slope-scaling-and-edge-fade.md)
Computes sun shadow occlusion at a world position using a comparison sampler and a dynamic Percentage-Closer Filtering (PCF) kernel, with slope-scaled depth bias and distance-based edge fading to prevent peter-panning and hard shadow edges. It differs from standard shadow mapping by offsetting the sample position along the surface normal rather than the light direction to maintain contact stability, and by fading occlusion at the periphery of the shadow map to avoid black artifacts at volume boundaries.

### Reflective Shadow Maps
- **Step**: Perform a direct lighting pass of the scene and store the result in textures. Render from the point of view of the light source, storing depth information and surface properties (albedo, normal, direct illumination). During final shading, treat texels of the reflective shadow map as point lights to provide single bounce indirect illumination. Optimize by reversing processing: create several lights based on the entire map and splat them in screen-space [S1, unofficial].
- **Pitfall**: Using all texels leads to excessive performance cost, requiring heuristics to choose regions of the map. Insufficient number of lights causes temporal stability issues and flickering when moving lights [S1, unofficial].

### Moment-Based Order-Independent Transparency
- **Definition**: A method for compact storage and fast reconstruction of the depth-dependent transmittance function per pixel, allowing compositing of transparent surfaces rendered in arbitrary order. It works with the logarithm of the transmittance such that the function may be accumulated additively rather than multiplicatively [S2, unofficial].
- **Step**: Perform an additive rendering pass for all transparent surfaces to yield moments. Use moment-based reconstruction algorithms to provide approximations to the original function, which are used for compositing in a second additive pass [S2, unofficial].
- **Parameter**: Variants utilize existing algorithms with four or six power moments, or new algorithms using eight power moments or up to four trigonometric moments [S2, unofficial].
- **Comparison**: Completely order-independent, unlike methods requiring sorting. Works well for participating media as well as transparent surfaces [S2, unofficial].

## Background
Shadow mapping was originally introduced by Lance Williams in 1978 [G]. It has since become the standard technique for real-time shadow rendering in computer graphics due to its efficiency and compatibility with hardware depth buffers. In the context of volume rendering, it was adapted to allow volumes to receive shadows from polygonal geometry without the performance penalty of double traversal, as seen in implementations like `vtkGPUVolumeRayCastMapper2` [S1, unofficial]. More recently, variants like Variance Shadow Mapping have been applied to Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs) to handle soft shadows in implicit representations [S1, unofficial]. Reflective Shadow Maps extend the concept to include indirect illumination by treating shadow map texels as virtual point lights [S1, unofficial].
