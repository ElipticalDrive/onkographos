# Variance Occlusion Maps

Also called: Variance Ambient Maps

## Overview
Variance Occlusion Maps (VOM) approximate the Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF) of voxel values within a spherical region using only the first two statistical moments (mean and variance) to determine the percentage of occluding voxels for ambient occlusion and soft shadows [S1]. Unlike naive shadow mapping or standard variance shadow mapping which operate on 2D depth buffers, VOM operates directly on 3D volume data, allowing for spatially varying soft shadows and ambient occlusion that respect the underlying geometry's density distribution [S1]. It sets itself apart from its sibling [Variance Shadow Mapping](5686-variance-shadow-mapping.md) by extending the moment-based approximation into volumetric space, enabling occlusion queries against isosurfaces rather than just depth planes.

## How it works

### Inputs and Outputs
*   **Input:** A scalar volume dataset (e.g., density field, distance field, or MRI data).
*   **Output:** A mip-mapped two-channel floating-point texture (the Variance Ambient Map) storing the first and second moments, and rendered images with ambient occlusion and/or soft shadows applied to the extracted isosurface.

### Data Structures
The core data structure is the **Variance Ambient Map**, stored as a mip-mapped two-channel floating-point texture [S1].
*   **Layout:** Each texel stores two values:
    *   Channel 1: $M_1$ (First moment, mean).
    *   Channel 2: $M_2$ (Second moment, mean of squared values).
*   **Mip-map Generation:** The map is generated by iteratively doubling the Gaussian kernel size and down-sampling the volume by a factor of two. This allows the representation of large filter regions at lower resolutions [S1].

### Formulas
The method relies on Chebyshev's inequality to bound the probability of occlusion.

1.  **First Moment ($M_1$):**
    $$ M_1 = E(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x p(x) dx $$
    Where $x$ is the voxel value and $p(x)$ is the probability density function [S1].

2.  **Second Moment ($M_2$):**
    $$ M_2 = E(x^2) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x^2 p(x) dx $$
    [S1]

3.  **Mean ($\mu$) and Variance ($\sigma^2$):**
    Derived from the stored moments:
    $$ \mu = M_1 $$
    $$ \sigma^2 = M_2 - M_1^2 $$
    [S1]

4.  **Chebyshev Bound for Occlusion Probability ($p_{max}$):**
    Given a threshold $t$ (the isovalue), the upper bound on the probability that a voxel value $x$ is less than $t$ (i.e., occluding) is:
    $$ p_{max}(t) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } t \le \mu \\ \frac{\sigma^2}{\sigma^2 + (t-\mu)^2} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} $$
    [S1]

### Parameters
*   **Blur Kernel:** A $5 \times 5 \times 5$ separable Gaussian kernel is used during preprocessing [S1].
*   **Down-sampling Factor:** 2 (kernel size doubles, resolution halves at each mip level) [S1].
*   **Isovalue Offset:** Samples for occlusion calculation are taken one half of a voxel under the surface in the direction of the gradient [S1].

### Steps

#### Preprocessing
1.  **Moment Calculation:** Create a new volume where each voxel contains the original value ($x$) and the squared value ($x^2$).
2.  **Separable Convolution:** Perform separable convolutions with the Gaussian kernel on the moment volumes to generate local distributions centered at each voxel.
3.  **Mip-map Construction:**
    *   For each subsequent mip level:
        *   Double the Gaussian kernel size.
        *   Down-sample the current volume by a factor of two.
        *   Store the resulting $M_1$ and $M_2$ values in the two-channel texture.
    *   [G] Initialize the base level (highest resolution) by convolving the original volume moments with the base $5 \times 5 \times 5$ kernel.

#### Rendering (Ambient Occlusion)
1.  **Surface Search:** Ray march from the camera to the isosurface. Perform a binary search to refine the surface intersection point $P$.
2.  **Gradient Calculation:** Calculate the surface normal (gradient) at $P$ using central differencing on the original volume data.
3.  **Isovalue Determination:** To handle partial volumes (where one material meets another), take an extra sample at $P_{iso} = P + 0.5 \cdot \Delta \cdot \nabla$, where $\Delta$ is the voxel size and $\nabla$ is the normalized gradient direction. Use the value at $P_{iso}$ as the effective isovalue $t$ for the occlusion query [S1].
4.  **Occlusion Query:**
    *   Sample the Variance Ambient Map at four mip levels (typically the first 4 levels) in the direction of the surface normal.
    *   For each sample, retrieve $M_1$ and $M_2$.
    *   Calculate $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$ from the moments.
    *   Evaluate $p_{max}(t)$ using the Chebyshev bound formula for each mip level.
5.  **Final Occlusion:** Take the **maximum** occlusion value among the sampled mip levels. This ensures that the finest detail that fits within the query radius is used, preventing over-occlusion from coarser approximations [S1].

#### Rendering (Soft Shadows)
1.  **Ray Casting:** Cast a ray from the surface point $P$ in the direction of the light source $L$.
2.  **Stepwise Evaluation:**
    *   Advance the ray in steps (e.g., every 4 steps or based on distance).
    *   At each step, query the Variance Ambient Map.
    *   Logarithmically increase the mip level as the ray travels further towards the light to simulate area light sources (larger filter regions for distant occluders) [S1].
    *   Evaluate $p_{max}(t)$ for the current sample.
3.  **Termination:** Stop when the ray leaves the volume or the occlusion probability reaches 1 (fully occluded).
4.  **Final Shadow:** The shadow factor is determined by the occlusion probability at the point of interest, typically taking the maximum occlusion encountered along the path or the value at the surface [S1].

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Aliasing:** Using a hemispherical filter region with cosine weighting requires non-separable blurring based on surface normal. This causes aliasing artifacts under linear interpolation of neighboring samples, especially at lower resolutions [S1]. VOM avoids this by using a radially symmetric kernel and offsetting the sampling location from the surface [S1].
*   **Accuracy Degradation:** The approximation becomes less accurate as the chosen isovalue moves towards the mean of the distribution. This is particularly problematic for non-bimodal data like MRI or distance fields, where the distribution is not clearly separated into "occluder" and "non-occluder" modes [S1].
*   **Concentration Bias:** Because a radially symmetric kernel is used instead of a normal-dependent hemispherical one, the occlusion becomes slightly more concentrated in the direction of the surface normal [S1].

## Operating characteristics

### Time and Memory Complexity
*   **Preprocessing:** $O(N \cdot K^3)$ where $N$ is the number of voxels and $K$ is the kernel size. The mip-map structure reduces memory usage significantly; at half resolution, the map is roughly $0.6 \times$ the size of the original volume (including mipmaps) for a two-channel 32-bit float texture [S1].
*   **Rendering (AO):** Constant time per pixel, requiring a fixed number of samples (e.g., 4) from the mip-mapped texture [S1].
*   **Rendering (Shadows):** Linear in the number of steps along the ray cast towards the light [S1].

### Measured Performance
*   Tested on a Mac Pro with a single NVIDIA GeForce 8800 GTS [S1].
*   Preprocessing can be greatly accelerated on the GPU [S1].
*   Lighting is calculated as a post-process, independent of volume size, except for soft shadows which require an extra ray cast [S1].

### Strengths and Limitations
*   **Strengths:**
    *   Produces soft shadow edges and ambient occlusion without explicit ray tracing of every voxel.
    *   Efficient memory footprint due to mip-mapping and moment storage.
    *   Allows for view-dependent ambient occlusion by adjusting surface normals during interactive rendering [S1].
*   **Limitations:**
    *   Accuracy is compromised for non-bimodal data distributions [S1].
    *   Occlusion is biased towards the surface normal direction due to the use of spherical kernels [S1].
    *   Not suitable for highly detailed, non-clustered data where the mean is a poor separator [S1].

## Background

### Origin
Variance Occlusion Maps were introduced by Eric Penner and Ross Mitchell in the paper "Isosurface Ambient Occlusion and Soft Shadows with Filterable Occlusion Maps" (2008) [S1]. The method builds upon the technique described by Donnelly et al. [DL06] for representing image distributions using the first two moments.

### History and Development
The technique extends the concept of Percentage Closer Filtering (PCF) and Variance Shadow Mapping from 2D depth buffers to 3D volume data. It addresses the need for efficient, filterable occlusion queries in volumetric rendering, particularly for isosurfaces. The authors noted that while hemispherical filters are theoretically more accurate for surface lighting, the computational cost of non-separable blurring based on normals led to aliasing, prompting the adoption of the radially symmetric kernel approach [S1].

### Usage
The method was implemented and demonstrated on medical imaging data (MRI) and synthetic volumes to show its applicability in generating realistic ambient occlusion and soft shadows for isosurfaces [S1].
