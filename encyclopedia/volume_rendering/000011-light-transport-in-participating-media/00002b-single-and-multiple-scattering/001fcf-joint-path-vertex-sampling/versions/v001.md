# Joint Path Vertex Sampling

Also called: 3-random-decision connection, connection subpath sampling

## Overview
Joint Path Vertex Sampling (JPVS) is a method for connecting a light subpath endpoint $\mathbf{a}$ and an eye subpath endpoint $\mathbf{d}$ in participating media by constructing a connection subpath with two new vertices $\mathbf{b}$ and $\mathbf{c}$ using three random decisions [S1]. It solves the problem of high-variance estimation in single and multiple scattering scenarios by sampling the connection geometry proportional to the product of geometry and scattering terms, rather than relying on visibility checks or fixed geometric constraints [S1]. It sets itself apart from siblings like Equiangular Sampling and Vertex-Vertex connection by using 3 random decisions (including direction sampling) to reduce variance, and unlike Path Guiding, it does not require storing sampling densities or handling visibility explicitly during construction [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** Vertex $\mathbf{a}$ (light subpath endpoint) with incident direction $\omega_{la}$; Vertex $\mathbf{d}$ (eye subpath endpoint) with outgoing direction $\omega_{dc}$; Configuration $\Xi$ (scene properties, phase functions, extinction coefficients).
*   **Output:** Vertices $\mathbf{b}$ and $\mathbf{c}$ forming the connection subpath $\mathbf{a} \to \mathbf{b} \to \mathbf{c} \to \mathbf{d}$.

**Data Structures:**
The method operates on standard vector and scalar types. Key geometric quantities are derived from the input vertices and sampled directions.
*   $\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{d}, \mathbf{b}, \mathbf{c}$: 3D positions.
*   $\omega_{la}, \omega_{dc}, \omega_{cb}$: Unit direction vectors.
*   $t_{dc}, t_{cb}, t_{ab}$: Scalar distances.
*   $G(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v})$: Geometry term between vertices $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$.
*   $\rho(\mathbf{u})$: Scattering term (phase function or albedo) at vertex $\mathbf{u}$.

**Target PDF:**
The goal is to sample $\mathbf{b}$ and $t_{dc}$ from a joint PDF proportional to the geometry and scattering terms of the connection subpath [S1]:
$$
p(\mathbf{b}, t_{\text{dc}} \mid \Xi) G(\mathbf{c}, \mathbf{d}) = C_\Xi G(\mathbf{abcd}) \rho(\mathbf{abc})
$$
where $G(\mathbf{abcd}) = G(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b})G(\mathbf{b}, \mathbf{c})G(\mathbf{c}, \mathbf{d})$ and $\rho(\mathbf{abc}) = \rho(\mathbf{a})\rho(\mathbf{b})\rho(\mathbf{c})$. The term $\rho(\mathbf{d})$ is excluded because the outgoing direction $\omega_{dc}$ and incident direction at $\mathbf{d}$ are given as input [S1]. $C_\Xi$ is a normalization factor dependent on the configuration [S1].

**Factorizations:**
Two factorizations of the joint PDF are defined, corresponding to the order of random decisions [S1].

1.  **Unidirectional Factorization:** Vertices $\mathbf{c}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ are sampled from the same direction starting from $\mathbf{d}$. The measure change requires dividing by $G(\mathbf{b}, \mathbf{c})$ [S1]:
    $$
    p(\mathbf{b}, t_{\text{dc}} \mid \Xi) = p(t_{\text{cb}}, \omega_{\text{cb}}, t_{\text{dc}} \mid \Xi) G(\mathbf{b}, \mathbf{c})
    $$
    The joint PDF factors into three conditional PDFs [S1]:
    $$
    p(t_{\text{cb}}, \omega_{\text{cb}}, t_{\text{dc}} \mid \Xi) = p(t_{\text{dc}} \mid \Xi) \cdot p(\omega_{\text{cb}} \mid t_{\text{dc}}, \Xi) \cdot p(t_{\text{cb}} \mid \omega_{\text{cb}}, t_{\text{dc}}, \Xi)
    $$
    Steps:
    1.  Sample distance $t_{dc}$ from $\mathbf{d}$ along $\omega_{dc}$ to get $\mathbf{c}$.
    2.  Sample direction $\omega_{cb}$ from $\mathbf{c}$.
    3.  Sample distance $t_{cb}$ from $\mathbf{c}$ along $\omega_{cb}$ to get $\mathbf{b}$.

2.  **Bidirectional Factorization:** Vertices $\mathbf{b}$ and $\mathbf{c}$ are sampled from opposite directions. The measure change requires dividing by $G(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b})$ [S1]:
    $$
    p(\mathbf{b}, t_{\text{dc}} \mid \Xi) = p(t_{\text{ab}}, \omega_{\text{ab}}, t_{\text{dc}} \mid \Xi) G(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b})
    $$
    The joint PDF factors into three conditional PDFs [S1]:
    $$
    p(t_{\text{ab}}, \omega_{\text{ab}}, t_{\text{dc}} \mid \Xi) = p(\omega_{\text{ab}} \mid \Xi) \cdot p(t_{\text{ab}} \mid \omega_{\text{ab}}, \Xi) \cdot p(t_{\text{dc}} \mid \omega_{\text{ab}}, t_{\text{ab}}, \Xi)
    $$
    Steps:
    1.  Sample direction $\omega_{ab}$ from $\mathbf{a}$.
    2.  Sample distance $t_{ab}$ from $\mathbf{a}$ along $\omega_{ab}$ to get $\mathbf{b}$.
    3.  Sample distance $t_{dc}$ from $\mathbf{d}$ along $\omega_{dc}$ to get $\mathbf{c}$.

**Analytic Importance Sampling (Unidirectional, Isotropic):**
For isotropic scattering, fully analytic formulas can be derived for the unidirectional factorization [S1].

*   **Geometric Quantities:**
    *   $h_{ca^\perp}$: Distance between vertex $\mathbf{a}$ and the ray $(\mathbf{c}, \omega_{cb})$.
    *   $t_{ca^\perp}$: Distance between vertex $\mathbf{c}$ and the projection of $\mathbf{a}$ onto the ray $(\mathbf{c}, \omega_{cb})$.
    *   $\theta_{cb}$: Angle between direction $\omega_{cb}$ and the line between $\mathbf{c}$ and $\mathbf{a}$ (length $t_{ca}$).

*   **Conditional PDFs:**
    1.  **$p(t_{cb} \mid \omega_{cb}, t_{dc}, \Xi)$:**
        $$
        p(t_{\text{cb}} \mid \omega_{\text{cb}}, t_{\text{dc}}, \Xi) = \frac{t_{\text{ca}} \sin \theta_{\text{cb}}}{\pi - \theta_{\text{cb}}} \frac{1}{h_{\text{ca}^\perp}^2 + (t_{\text{ca}^\perp} - t_{\text{cb}})^2}
        $$
        This PDF is exactly equal to that of equiangular sampling provided $\mathbf{a}$, $\omega_{cb}$, and $\mathbf{c}$ are given [S1].
    2.  **$p(\omega_{cb} \mid t_{dc}, \Xi)$:**
        $$
        p(\omega_{\text{cb}} \mid t_{\text{dc}}, \Xi) = \frac{\pi - \theta_{\text{cb}}}{\pi^3 \sin \theta_{\text{cb}}}
        $$
        This PDF diverges when $\theta_{cb}$ approaches zero. This singularity cancels out the singularity in the path contribution function, reducing variance [S1].
    3.  **$p(t_{dc} \mid \Xi)$:**
        Derived via marginalization. The exact formula depends on the specific configuration $\Xi$ and normalization factors $C_\Xi$ [S1].

**Pseudocode (Unidirectional, Isotropic):**
```python
def sample_jpvs_unidirectional(a, omega_la, d, omega_dc, sigma_t):
    """
    Samples vertices b and c for the connection subpath using unidirectional factorization.
    Assumes isotropic scattering.
    """
    # Step 1: Sample t_dc from d along omega_dc to get c
    # The PDF p(t_dc | Xi) is derived from marginalization.
    # For isotropic scattering, the marginal PDF for t_dc is proportional to
    # the integral of the joint PDF over t_cb and omega_cb.
    # A common approach for the marginal t_dc in this context involves
    # sampling from a distribution related to the geometry of a and d.
    # However, the source [S1] states that for isotropic scattering,
    # fully analytic formulas are derived. The specific marginal PDF for t_dc
    # is not explicitly written as a simple closed form in the provided snippets,
    # but it is part of the factorization.
    # We assume a sampling routine `sample_t_dc` exists based on the derived marginal.
    t_dc = sample_t_dc(a, d, omega_dc, sigma_t)
    c = d + t_dc * omega_dc

    # Step 2: Sample omega_cb from c
    # PDF: p(omega_cb | t_dc, Xi) = (pi - theta_cb) / (pi^3 * sin(theta_cb))
    # theta_cb is the angle between omega_cb and the vector (a - c).
    # Let v_ac = a - c. We need to sample a direction omega_cb such that
    # the angle theta_cb with v_ac follows the PDF above.
    # This requires sampling theta_cb from the marginal distribution of theta_cb,
    # then sampling the azimuthal angle uniformly around v_ac.
    # The marginal PDF for theta_cb is obtained by integrating the joint PDF.
    # From Eq 22 and 26 in [S1], the marginal for theta_cb is proportional to
    # (pi - theta_cb) / sin(theta_cb) * (1 / t_ca) ... wait, Eq 22 is the integral over t_cb.
    # The marginal PDF for theta_cb is proportional to (pi - theta_cb) / sin(theta_cb).
    # Normalization constant Z = integral_0^pi (pi - theta) / sin(theta) d_theta.
    # This integral diverges at theta=0 and theta=pi?
    # Actually, the PDF in Eq 27 is for omega_cb on the sphere.
    # To sample omega_cb, we sample theta_cb from p(theta_cb) and phi_cb uniformly.
    # p(theta_cb) is proportional to (pi - theta_cb) / sin(theta_cb).
    # Note: The source [S1] mentions the PDF diverges at theta_cb=0.
    # Sampling from this PDF cancels the singularity in the contribution.
    
    # Implementation of sampling theta_cb from p(theta) ~ (pi - theta)/sin(theta):
    # This is a non-trivial sampling problem. The source [S1] implies it is done.
    # We will use a placeholder for the sampling routine, noting the PDF.
    theta_cb = sample_theta_cb_from_pdf(lambda theta: (np.pi - theta) / np.sin(theta))
    phi_cb = random.uniform(0, 2 * np.pi)
    
    # Construct omega_cb from theta_cb and phi_cb relative to v_ac
    v_ac = a - c
    v_ac = v_ac / np.linalg.norm(v_ac)
    # Create a basis where z-axis is v_ac
    # ... (standard spherical coordinate conversion) ...
    omega_cb = spherical_to_vector(theta_cb, phi_cb, v_ac)
    
    # Step 3: Sample t_cb from c along omega_cb to get b
    # PDF: p(t_cb | omega_cb, t_dc, Xi) = (t_ca * sin(theta_cb) / (pi - theta_cb)) * (1 / (h_ca_perp^2 + (t_ca_perp - t_cb)^2))
    # This is a Cauchy-like distribution.
    # h_ca_perp and t_ca_perp are geometric quantities derived from a, c, and omega_cb.
    # Projection of a onto ray (c, omega_cb):
    # t_ca_perp = dot(a - c, omega_cb)
    # h_ca_perp = norm(a - c - t_ca_perp * omega_cb)
    
    v_ca = a - c
    t_ca_perp = np.dot(v_ca, omega_cb)
    h_ca_perp_sq = np.dot(v_ca, v_ca) - t_ca_perp**2
    h_ca_perp = np.sqrt(max(h_ca_perp_sq, 1e-8)) # Guard against zero
    t_ca = np.linalg.norm(v_ca)
    
    # PDF: p(t_cb) = K * 1 / (h_ca_perp^2 + (t_ca_perp - t_cb)^2)
    # where K = (t_ca * sin(theta_cb)) / (pi - theta_cb)
    # This is a Cauchy distribution centered at t_ca_perp with scale h_ca_perp.
    # Sampling from Cauchy: t_cb = t_ca_perp + h_ca_perp * tan(pi * (u - 0.5))
    # where u is uniform in (0, 1).
    u = random.uniform(0, 1)
    t_cb = t_ca_perp + h_ca_perp * np.tan(np.pi * (u - 0.5))
    
    # Ensure t_cb is positive
    if t_cb <= 0:
        t_cb = 1e-6 # [G] Fallback for degenerate case
        
    b = c + t_cb * omega_cb
    
    return b, c, t_dc, t_cb, omega_cb
```

## Subtopics

### [Bridge Sampling](3881-bridge-sampling.md)
Bridge Sampling is a method for sampling a subpath (bridge) of arbitrary vertex count connecting two vertices in a participating medium, where the probability density is proportional to all inner phase functions and reciprocal squared distance terms. It sets itself apart from sibling methods like Equiangular Sampling and MVNEE by fixing the endpoint via rotation and uniform scaling of a preliminary path, which preserves angles and handles highly-peaked forward scattering more robustly. Unlike Path Guiding, it does not use memory to store sampling densities or handle visibility explicitly during construction.

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** The unidirectional factorization requires 3 random decisions (distance, direction, distance) and geometric calculations. The bidirectional factorization also requires 3 random decisions. Analytic sampling for isotropic scattering is $O(1)$ per connection.
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(1)$ auxiliary memory per connection.
*   **Performance:** Reduces variance significantly compared to methods using 0, 1, or 2 random decisions (like vertex-vertex connection, equiangular sampling, and VRLs) by incorporating direction sampling [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Handles highly-peaked forward scattering more robustly than some alternatives; cancels out singularities in the path contribution function; no need for visibility checks or density storage.
*   **Limitations:** Derivation of analytic PDFs is complex for anisotropic scattering (requires tabulation as per Section 6 of [S1]); sampling from the divergent PDF for $\omega_{cb}$ requires care.
*   **When to use:** When variance reduction is critical for single/multiple scattering connections and isotropic or mildly anisotropic scattering is present.
*   **When not to use:** For highly anisotropic scattering where analytic formulas are not available and tabulation is too expensive, or when computational budget is extremely tight and variance is less critical than speed.

## Background
Joint Path Vertex Sampling was introduced by Novak et al. in 2013 [S1]. It extends previous methods for connecting light and eye subpaths by introducing a third random decision (direction sampling) to better approximate the optimal connection distribution. It is used in volumetric path tracing to improve the estimation of light transport in participating media.
