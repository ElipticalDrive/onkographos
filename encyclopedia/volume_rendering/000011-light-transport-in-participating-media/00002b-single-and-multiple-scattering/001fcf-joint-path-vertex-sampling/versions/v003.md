# Joint Path Vertex Sampling

Also called: 3-random-decision connection, connection subpath sampling

## Overview
Joint Path Vertex Sampling is a method for constructing a full light transport path by connecting a light subpath endpoint $\mathbf{a}$ and an eye subpath endpoint $\mathbf{d}$ via a connection subpath containing two new vertices $\mathbf{b}$ and $\mathbf{c}$ [S1]. It solves the problem of high-variance connections in participating media by using three random decisions (distance, direction, distance) to sample the intermediate segment, which cancels out geometric singularities that persist in methods using fewer random decisions [S1]. It sets itself apart from [Equiangular Sampling](link) and [Virtual Ray Lights](link) by explicitly sampling the direction $\boldsymbol{\omega}_{cb}$, thereby reducing variance in scenes with complex light transport [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs:**
*   Vertex $\mathbf{a}$ with incident direction $\boldsymbol{\omega}_{la}$ (from light subpath).
*   Vertex $\mathbf{d}$ with outgoing direction $\boldsymbol{\omega}_{dc}$ (from eye subpath).
*   Configuration $\Xi$ containing medium properties (extinction coefficients $\sigma_t$) and geometry.

**Outputs:**
*   Vertices $\mathbf{b}$ and $\mathbf{c}$ forming the connection subpath.
*   The joint probability density function (PDF) $p(\mathbf{b}, t_{dc} \mid \Xi)$ for the Metropolis-Hastings or MIS weight calculation.

**Data Structures:**
*   The method operates on the geometric configuration defined by $\mathbf{a}, \boldsymbol{\omega}_{la}, \mathbf{d}, \boldsymbol{\omega}_{dc}$.
*   Intermediate geometric quantities required for isotropic sampling:
    *   $h_{ca^\perp}$: Distance between vertex $\mathbf{a}$ and the ray $(\mathbf{c}, \boldsymbol{\omega}_{cb})$.
    *   $t_{ca^\perp}$: Distance between vertex $\mathbf{c}$ and the projection of $\mathbf{a}$ onto the ray $(\mathbf{c}, \boldsymbol{\omega}_{cb})$.
    *   $\theta_{cb}$: Angle between direction $\boldsymbol{\omega}_{cb}$ and the line segment connecting $\mathbf{c}$ and $\mathbf{a}$ (length $t_{ca}$).

**Target PDF:**
The goal is to sample $\mathbf{b}$ and $t_{dc}$ such that the joint PDF satisfies [S1]:
$$ p(\mathbf{b}, t_{dc} \mid \Xi) G(\mathbf{c}, \mathbf{d}) = C_\Xi G(\mathbf{abcd}) \rho(\mathbf{abc}) $$
where:
*   $G(\mathbf{abcd}) = G(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b})G(\mathbf{b}, \mathbf{c})G(\mathbf{c}, \mathbf{d})$ is the product of geometry terms.
*   $\rho(\mathbf{abc}) = \rho(\mathbf{a})\rho(\mathbf{b})\rho(\mathbf{c})$ is the product of scattering terms (phase functions or albedo).
*   $\rho(\mathbf{d})$ is excluded because $\boldsymbol{\omega}_{dc}$ and the incident direction at $\mathbf{d}$ are given inputs [S1].
*   $C_\Xi$ is a normalization factor dependent on the input configuration [S1].

**Factorizations:**
Two factorizations of the joint PDF are defined, corresponding to different orders of random decisions [S1].

1.  **Unidirectional Factorization:** Vertices $\mathbf{c}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ are sampled from the same direction starting from $\mathbf{d}$.
    *   Step 1: Sample distance $t_{dc}$ from $\mathbf{d}$ along $\boldsymbol{\omega}_{dc}$ to get $\mathbf{c}$.
    *   Step 2: Sample direction $\boldsymbol{\omega}_{cb}$ from $\mathbf{c}$.
    *   Step 3: Sample distance $t_{cb}$ from $\mathbf{c}$ along $\boldsymbol{\omega}_{cb}$ to get $\mathbf{b}$.
    *   PDF Factorization:
        $$ p(t_{cb}, \boldsymbol{\omega}_{cb}, t_{dc} \mid \Xi) = p(t_{dc} \mid \Xi) \cdot p(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{cb} \mid t_{dc}, \Xi) \cdot p(t_{cb} \mid \boldsymbol{\omega}_{cb}, t_{dc}, \Xi) $$
        Note: The measure change for sampling $\mathbf{b}$ as a distance/direction from $\mathbf{c}$ introduces a factor $G(\mathbf{b}, \mathbf{c})$ [S1].

2.  **Bidirectional Factorization:** Vertices $\mathbf{b}$ and $\mathbf{c}$ are sampled from opposite directions.
    *   Step 1: Sample direction $\boldsymbol{\omega}_{ab}$ from $\mathbf{a}$.
    *   Step 2: Sample distance $t_{ab}$ from $\mathbf{a}$ along $\boldsymbol{\omega}_{ab}$ to get $\mathbf{b}$.
    *   Step 3: Sample distance $t_{dc}$ from $\mathbf{d}$ along $\boldsymbol{\omega}_{dc}$ to get $\mathbf{c}$.
    *   PDF Factorization:
        $$ p(t_{ab}, \boldsymbol{\omega}_{ab}, t_{dc} \mid \Xi) = p(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{ab} \mid \Xi) \cdot p(t_{ab} \mid \boldsymbol{\omega}_{ab}, \Xi) \cdot p(t_{dc} \mid \boldsymbol{\omega}_{ab}, t_{ab}, \Xi) $$
        Note: The measure change for sampling $\mathbf{b}$ from $\mathbf{a}$ introduces a factor $G(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b})$ [S1].

**Analytic Sampling (Isotropic Scattering, Unidirectional):**
For isotropic scattering, fully analytic formulas for the PDFs and sampling routines can be derived [S1].

1.  **Sampling $t_{dc}$:**
    The marginal PDF $p(t_{dc} \mid \Xi)$ is derived by integrating out $\boldsymbol{\omega}_{cb}$ and $t_{cb}$. The specific analytic form depends on the medium's extinction coefficient $\sigma_t$ and the geometry, involving the normalization constant $C_\Xi$ [S1].

2.  **Sampling $\boldsymbol{\omega}_{cb}$:**
    Given $t_{dc}$, the conditional PDF for the direction $\boldsymbol{\omega}_{cb}$ is [S1]:
    $$ p(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{cb} \mid t_{dc}, \Xi) = \frac{\pi - \theta_{cb}}{\pi^3 \sin \theta_{cb}} $$
    where $\theta_{cb}$ is the angle between $\boldsymbol{\omega}_{cb}$ and the vector $\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{c}$ [S1].
    *   **Pitfall:** This PDF diverges when $\theta_{cb} \to 0$. This singularity cancels with the singularity in the path contribution function (specifically the geometry term $G(\mathbf{b}, \mathbf{c})$), reducing variance [S1]. Sampling from this PDF is crucial for this cancellation [S1].

3.  **Sampling $t_{cb}$:**
    Given $\boldsymbol{\omega}_{cb}$ and $t_{dc}$, the conditional PDF for the distance $t_{cb}$ is [S1]:
    $$ p(t_{cb} \mid \boldsymbol{\omega}_{cb}, t_{dc}, \Xi) = \frac{t_{ca} \sin \theta_{cb}}{\pi - \theta_{cb}} \frac{1}{h_{ca^\perp}^2 + (t_{ca^\perp} - t_{cb})^2} $$
    where $h_{ca^\perp}$ is the distance between $\mathbf{a}$ and the ray $(\mathbf{c}, \boldsymbol{\omega}_{cb})$, and $t_{ca^\perp}$ is the distance between $\mathbf{c}$ and the projection of $\mathbf{a}$ onto that ray [S1].
    *   This PDF is exactly equal to that of equiangular sampling provided $\mathbf{a}$, $\boldsymbol{\omega}_{cb}$, and $\mathbf{c}$ are given [S1].
    *   The denominator is the squared distance $t_{ba}^2$ [S1].

**Pseudocode (Unidirectional, Isotropic):**
```python
def sample_joint_path_vertex_sampling(a, omega_la, d, omega_dc, sigma_t):
    """
    Samples vertices b and c connecting light subpath endpoint a 
    and eye subpath endpoint d using unidirectional factorization.
    Assumes isotropic scattering.
    """
    # 1. Sample t_dc from d along omega_dc
    # The PDF p(t_dc | Xi) depends on the specific geometry and sigma_t.
    # For homogeneous media, this often involves exponential sampling 
    # adjusted by the geometric term integration.
    # Let's assume a generic sampler for the marginal p(t_dc | Xi) exists.
    t_dc = sample_t_dc_from_marginal(a, d, omega_dc, sigma_t)
    c = d + t_dc * omega_dc
    
    # 2. Sample omega_cb from c
    # The PDF is p(omega_cb | t_dc, Xi) proportional to (pi - theta_cb) / sin(theta_cb)
    # theta_cb is the angle between omega_cb and (a - c).
    # This requires sampling a direction on the hemisphere around c 
    # biased by the angle to a.
    omega_cb = sample_omega_cb_from_angle_distribution(c, a)
    
    # 3. Sample t_cb from c along omega_cb
    # The PDF is p(t_cb | omega_cb, t_dc, Xi)
    # p(t_cb) = K * 1 / (h_ca_perp^2 + (t_ca_perp - t_cb)^2)
    # This is a Cauchy/Lorentzian-like distribution.
    
    # Calculate geometric helpers
    vec_ca = a - c
    t_ca = length(vec_ca)
    # Project a onto ray (c, omega_cb)
    # t_ca_perp is the signed distance from c to the projection
    t_ca_perp = dot(vec_ca, omega_cb)
    # h_ca_perp is the perpendicular distance
    h_ca_perp_sq = t_ca**2 - t_ca_perp**2
    h_ca_perp = sqrt(max(0.0, h_ca_perp_sq))
    
    # theta_cb
    # cos_theta = dot(omega_cb, vec_ca) / t_ca
    # theta_cb = acos(cos_theta)
    # Note: The PDF formula uses theta_cb directly.
    theta_cb = acos(dot(omega_cb, vec_ca) / t_ca)
    
    # Normalization constant for t_cb PDF
    # Integral of 1/(A^2 + (B-x)^2) dx is (1/A) * atan((x-B)/A)
    # Limits 0 to infinity.
    # K = (t_ca * sin(theta_cb)) / (pi - theta_cb)
    # The PDF is K * 1 / (h_ca_perp^2 + (t_ca_perp - t_cb)^2)
    
    # Sample t_cb from this distribution
    # Let u be uniform [0, 1].
    # Inverse CDF for Cauchy-like distribution.
    # CDF(x) = (1/pi) * (atan((x - t_ca_perp)/h_ca_perp) - atan(-t_ca_perp/h_ca_perp))
    # x = h_ca_perp * tan( pi * u + atan(-t_ca_perp/h_ca_perp) ) + t_ca_perp
    # Ensure x > 0.
    
    u = random_uniform()
    atan_term = atan(-t_ca_perp / h_ca_perp)
    t_cb = h_ca_perp * tan(pi * u + atan_term) + t_ca_perp
    
    if t_cb < 0:
        t_cb = 0 # Handle boundary, though PDF is defined for t_cb >= 0
        
    b = c + t_cb * omega_cb
    
    # Calculate Joint PDF for MIS/Weighting
    # p(b, t_dc | Xi) = p(t_dc | Xi) * p(omega_cb | t_dc, Xi) * p(t_cb | omega_cb, t_dc, Xi)
    # Note: Measure changes apply.
    # For unidirectional: p(b, t_dc) = p(t_cb, omega_cb, t_dc) * G(b, c)
    
    pdf_t_dc = evaluate_marginal_t_dc(a, d, omega_dc, sigma_t, t_dc)
    pdf_omega_cb = (pi - theta_cb) / (pi**3 * sin(theta_cb))
    pdf_t_cb = (t_ca * sin(theta_cb) / (pi - theta_cb)) * (1 / (h_ca_perp**2 + (t_ca_perp - t_cb)**2))
    
    # Geometry term for measure change
    G_bc = geometry_term(b, c)
    
    joint_pdf = pdf_t_dc * pdf_omega_cb * pdf_t_cb * G_bc
    
    return b, c, joint_pdf
```

## Subtopics

### [Bridge Sampling](3881-bridge-sampling.md)
Bridge Sampling is a method for sampling a subpath (bridge) of arbitrary vertex count connecting two vertices in a participating medium, where the probability density is proportional to all inner phase functions and reciprocal squared distance terms. It sets itself apart from sibling methods like Equiangular Sampling and MVNEE by fixing the endpoint via rotation and uniform scaling of a preliminary path, which preserves angles and handles highly-peaked forward scattering more robustly. Unlike Path Guiding, it does not use memory to store sampling densities or handle visibility explicitly during construction.

### [Bidirectional Path Tracing](6666-bidirectional-path-tracing.md)
Bidirectional Path Tracing constructs a full light transport path by independently generating a light subpath and an eye subpath, then connecting their endpoints with a deterministic segment. It differs from unidirectional path tracing by including both emitted radiance and sensor importance in the path probability density function (PDF), thereby reducing variance in scenes with complex light transport.

## Operating characteristics
*   **Complexity:** The method requires 3 random decisions per connection, compared to 0, 1, or 2 for vertex-vertex connection, equiangular sampling, and VRLs [S1]. This increases computational cost per connection but reduces variance [S1].
*   **Performance:** The analytic formulas for isotropic scattering allow for efficient sampling without numerical integration or lookup tables [S1]. For anisotropic scattering, low-dimensional PDF tabulations can be constructed using geometric symmetries [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Significantly reduces variance by canceling out geometric singularities in the path contribution function [S1]. It is particularly effective in scenes with complex light transport where standard connection methods fail [S1].
*   **Limitations:** The PDF for $\boldsymbol{\omega}_{cb}$ diverges when $\theta_{cb} \to 0$, requiring careful implementation to ensure the cancellation with the geometry term is exact [S1]. The method assumes the input directions $\boldsymbol{\omega}_{la}$ and $\boldsymbol{\omega}_{dc}$ are given [S1].
*   **When to use:** Use when high-quality rendering of participating media with multiple scattering is required, and standard connection methods exhibit high variance.
*   **When not to use:** Use [Equiangular Sampling](link) or [VRLs](link) if computational budget is extremely tight and variance is acceptable, or if the medium is highly anisotropic and tabulation is preferred over analytic derivation.

## Background
Joint Path Vertex Sampling was introduced by Kulla and Fajardo in 2012 [S1]. It was originally developed to "cancel out" the weak singularity in the geometry term $G(\mathbf{b}, \mathbf{c})$ [S1]. The method extends earlier techniques like Equiangular Sampling and Virtual Ray Lights by adding a third random decision (direction sampling) to further reduce variance [S1]. It is a core component of modern volumetric path tracing algorithms, often combined with Multiple Importance Sampling (MIS) to balance different connection strategies [S1].
