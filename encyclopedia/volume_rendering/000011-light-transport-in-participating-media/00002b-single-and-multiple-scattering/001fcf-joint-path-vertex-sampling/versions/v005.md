# Joint Path Vertex Sampling

Also called: 3-random-decision connection, connection subpath sampling

## Overview
Joint Path Vertex Sampling (JPVS) is a method for constructing a full light transport path by connecting the endpoints of a light subpath and an eye subpath via a connection subpath containing two new vertices, using three random decisions [S1]. It solves the high variance associated with connecting subpaths in participating media by sampling the intermediate vertices' positions and directions from a joint probability density function (PDF) proportional to the geometry and scattering terms of the connection [S1]. It sets itself apart from siblings like Equiangular Sampling and Virtual Ray Lights (VRLs) by adding a direction sampling step (3 random decisions vs. 0, 1, or 2), which cancels out singularities in the geometry term and reduces variance [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs**
*   **Input:** Vertex $\mathbf{a}$ (endpoint of light subpath) with incident direction $\boldsymbol{\omega}_{la}$; vertex $\mathbf{d}$ (endpoint of eye subpath) with outgoing direction $\boldsymbol{\omega}_{dc}$ [S1].
*   **Output:** Vertices $\mathbf{b}$ and $\mathbf{c}$ forming the connection subpath, such that the full path is $\mathbf{x}_{l}\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}\mathbf{c}\mathbf{d}\mathbf{x}_{e}$ [S1].

**Data Structures**
The method operates on geometric primitives:
*   Vertex: 3D position $\mathbf{p}$.
*   Direction: Unit vector $\boldsymbol{\omega}$.
*   Distance: Scalar $t \ge 0$.
*   Configuration $\Xi$: The tuple $(\mathbf{a}, \boldsymbol{\omega}_{la}, \mathbf{d}, \boldsymbol{\omega}_{dc})$ [S1].

**Formulas**
The target joint PDF for the connection subpath vertices $\mathbf{b}, \mathbf{c}$ and distance $t_{dc}$ is proportional to the product of geometry terms $G(\mathbf{abcd})$ and scattering terms $\rho(\mathbf{abc})$ [S1]:
$$ p(\mathbf{b}, t_{\text{dc}} \mid \Xi) G(\mathbf{c}, \mathbf{d}) = C_\Xi G(\mathbf{abcd}) \rho(\mathbf{abc}) $$
where:
*   $G(\mathbf{abcd}) = G(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b})G(\mathbf{b}, \mathbf{c})G(\mathbf{c}, \mathbf{d})$ is the product of individual geometry terms [S1].
*   $\rho(\mathbf{abc}) = \rho(\mathbf{a})\rho(\mathbf{b})\rho(\mathbf{c})$ is the product of phase functions (or albedo) at vertices $\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}, \mathbf{c}$ [S1].
*   $\rho(\mathbf{d})$ is excluded because $\boldsymbol{\omega}_{dc}$ and the incident direction at $\mathbf{d}$ are given [S1].
*   $C_\Xi$ is a normalization factor depending on $\Xi$ [S1].

**Factorizations**
Two factorizations of the joint PDF are defined, corresponding to different orders of random decisions [S1]:

1.  **Unidirectional Factorization:** Vertices $\mathbf{c}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ are sampled from the same direction starting from $\mathbf{d}$ [S1].
    $$ p(t_{\text{cb}}, \boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{cb}}, t_{\text{dc}} \mid \Xi) = p(t_{\text{dc}} \mid \Xi) \cdot p(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{cb}} \mid t_{\text{dc}}, \Xi) \cdot p(t_{\text{cb}} \mid \boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{cb}}, t_{\text{dc}}, \Xi) $$
    The change of measure from position $\mathbf{b}$ to distance/direction $(t_{\text{cb}}, \boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{cb}})$ introduces $G(\mathbf{b}, \mathbf{c})$ [S1]:
    $$ p(\mathbf{b}, t_{\text{dc}} \mid \Xi) = p(t_{\text{cb}}, \boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{cb}}, t_{\text{dc}} \mid \Xi) G(\mathbf{b}, \mathbf{c}) $$

2.  **Bidirectional Factorization:** Vertices $\mathbf{b}$ and $\mathbf{c}$ are sampled from opposite directions [S1].
    $$ p(t_{\text{ab}}, \boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{ab}}, t_{\text{dc}} \mid \Xi) = p(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{ab}} \mid \Xi) \cdot p(t_{\text{ab}} \mid \boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{ab}}, \Xi) \cdot p(t_{\text{dc}} \mid \boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{ab}}, t_{\text{ab}}, \Xi) $$
    The change of measure introduces $G(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b})$ [S1]:
    $$ p(\mathbf{b}, t_{\text{dc}} \mid \Xi) = p(t_{\text{ab}}, \boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{ab}}, t_{\text{dc}} \mid \Xi) G(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}) $$

**Analytic Sampling (Unidirectional, Isotropic Scattering)**
For isotropic scattering, fully analytic formulas exist for the unidirectional factorization [S1]. The conditional PDFs are derived via marginalization [S1]:

1.  **$p(t_{\text{dc}} \mid \Xi)$:**
    $$ p(t_{\text{dc}} \mid \Xi) = C_\Xi \frac{\pi - \theta_{\text{cb}}}{t_{\text{ca}} \sin \theta_{\text{cb}}} $$
    where $t_{\text{ca}}$ is the distance between $\mathbf{c}$ and $\mathbf{a}$ [S1].

2.  **$p(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{cb}} \mid t_{\text{dc}}, \Xi)$:**
    $$ p(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{cb}} \mid t_{\text{dc}}, \Xi) = \frac{\pi - \theta_{\text{cb}}}{\pi^3 \sin \theta_{\text{cb}}} $$
    This PDF diverges when $\theta_{\text{cb}} \to 0$, canceling the singularity in the path contribution function [S1].

3.  **$p(t_{\text{cb}} \mid \boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{cb}}, t_{\text{dc}}, \Xi)$:**
    $$ p(t_{\text{cb}} \mid \boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{cb}}, t_{\text{dc}}, \Xi) = \frac{t_{\text{ca}} \sin \theta_{\text{cb}}}{\pi - \theta_{\text{cb}}} \frac{1}{h_{\text{ca}^\perp}^2 + (t_{\text{ca}^\perp} - t_{\text{cb}})^2} $$
    where:
    *   $h_{\text{ca}^\perp}$ is the distance between $\mathbf{a}$ and the ray $(\mathbf{c}, \boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{cb}})$ [S1].
    *   $t_{\text{ca}^\perp}$ is the distance between $\mathbf{c}$ and the projection of $\mathbf{a}$ onto the ray $(\mathbf{c}, \boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{cb}})$ [S1].
    *   $\theta_{\text{cb}}$ is the angle between $\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{cb}}$ and the line between $\mathbf{c}$ and $\mathbf{a}$ [S1].
    This PDF is identical to equiangular sampling given $\mathbf{a}, \boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{cb}}, \mathbf{c}$ [S1].

**Algorithm: Unidirectional Factorization Sampling**
1.  Sample $t_{\text{dc}}$ from $p(t_{\text{dc}} \mid \Xi)$. Compute $\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{d} + t_{\text{dc}} \boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{dc}}$ [S1].
2.  Sample $\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{cb}}$ from $p(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{cb}} \mid t_{\text{dc}}, \Xi)$ [S1].
3.  Sample $t_{\text{cb}}$ from $p(t_{\text{cb}} \mid \boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{cb}}, t_{\text{dc}}, \Xi)$. Compute $\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{c} + t_{\text{cb}} \boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{cb}}$ [S1].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls**
*   **Singularity:** The PDF for $\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{cb}}$ diverges as $\theta_{\text{cb}} \to 0$. Sampling from this PDF cancels the corresponding singularity in the geometry term, reducing variance [S1].
*   **Hidden Singularity:** The weak singularity in $G(\mathbf{b}, \mathbf{c})$ is canceled by the sampling, but a true singularity remains in the orientation of $\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{dc}}$, which must be handled by importance sampling $\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{dc}}$ [S1].

## Subtopics

### [Bridge Sampling](3881-bridge-sampling.md)
Bridge Sampling is a method for sampling a subpath (bridge) of arbitrary vertex count connecting two vertices in a participating medium, where the probability density is proportional to all inner phase functions and reciprocal squared distance terms. It sets itself apart from sibling methods like Equiangular Sampling and MVNEE by fixing the endpoint via rotation and uniform scaling of a preliminary path, which preserves angles and handles highly-peaked forward scattering more robustly. Unlike Path Guiding, it does not use memory to store sampling densities or handle visibility explicitly during construction.

### [Bidirectional Path Tracing](6666-bidirectional-path-tracing.md)
Bidirectional Path Tracing constructs a full light transport path by independently generating a light subpath and an eye subpath, then connecting their endpoints with a deterministic segment. It addresses the high variance of unidirectional sampling by ensuring both emitted radiance and sensor importance are included in the path probability density function (PDF). Unlike its sibling Bridge Sampling, it uses a zero-random-decision connection, which results in potentially infinite variance for the connecting segment terms.

### Virtual ray lights (VRLs)
Virtual ray lights (VRLs) are a many-light method reinterpreted as a bidirectional segment-segment connection technique that samples propagation distances from a joint conditional distribution proportional to the scattering and geometry terms [S1]. It uses 2 random decisions, sampling distances $t_{ab}$ and $t_{dc}$ from a joint distribution, contrasting with JPVS's 3 random decisions [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Variance Reduction:** Uses 3 random decisions (adding direction sampling) compared to 0, 1, or 2 in vertex-vertex connection, equiangular sampling, and VRLs, leading to significant variance reduction [S1].
*   **Complexity:** Analytic formulas are available for isotropic scattering in the unidirectional factorization [S1]. For anisotropic scattering, low-dimensional PDF tabulations can be constructed using geometric symmetries [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Cancels singularities in the geometry term, reducing variance in path contribution estimation [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Requires sampling of direction $\boldsymbol{\omega}_{dc}$ to handle hidden singularities [S1].

## Background
Joint Path Vertex Sampling was developed by Kulla and Fajardo [S1]. It extends earlier work on equiangular sampling and virtual ray lights by introducing a third random decision (direction sampling) to further reduce variance in volumetric path tracing [S1].
