# Photon Mapping

Also called: Photon Maps, フォトンマッピング

## Overview
Photon mapping is a two-pass stochastic method that samples interaction points from a function of the extinction coefficient $\kappa_t$ to estimate global illumination [S1]. It solves the problem of noisy indirect lighting and caustics by decoupling light transport from image formation, storing photon data in a spatial structure for density estimation [S2, unofficial]. It is distinguished from its siblings, such as [Path Integral Framework](873-path-integral-framework.md) and [Volumetric Radiance Estimators](818-volumetric-radiance-estimators.md), by its view-independent precomputation phase, which allows for efficient reuse of light transport data across multiple viewpoints but introduces bias through density estimation parameters [S2, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs:** Scene geometry, material properties (albedo, scattering coefficients), light source definitions, and a photon count $N$.
**Outputs:** A photon map data structure and, optionally, a final rendered image.

**Data Structures:**
The core data structure is the **Photon Map**, typically implemented as a k-d tree or similar spatial index.
*   **Photon Node:** Stores the 3D position $\mathbf{x}$, the incoming radiance direction $\omega_i$, the wavelength or RGB color $\mathbf{c}$, and the throughput weight $w$.
*   **k-d Tree:** A binary space partitioning tree where each node represents a region in space. Internal nodes store a splitting plane (normal and distance); leaf nodes store a list of photons.

**Steps:**
1.  **Photon Emission:** For each of the $N$ photons, sample an initial position and direction from the light source distribution.
2.  **Photon Tracing:** Trace the photon through the scene.
    *   Sample the distance to the next interaction $\Delta s$ from the PDF $p(\Delta s) = \kappa_t e^{-\kappa_t \Delta s}$, where $\kappa_t$ is the extinction coefficient [S1].
    *   If the photon hits a surface, apply the material's scattering phase function and albedo to determine the new direction and throughput.
    *   Store the photon's position, direction, and throughput in the photon map at the interaction point.
    *   Repeat until the photon is absorbed or escapes the scene.
3.  **Density Estimation (Rendering Pass):** For each pixel, cast a ray and collect photons within a search radius $r$ around the intersection point.
    *   Estimate the radiance $L$ using a kernel density estimator:
        $$ L(\mathbf{x}, \omega_o) = \int_{\Omega} f_r(\mathbf{x}, \omega_i, \omega_o) \hat{L}_i(\mathbf{x}, \omega_i) d\omega_i $$
        where $\hat{L}_i$ is the estimated incident radiance derived from the photon density.
    *   The incident radiance is approximated by summing the contributions of $k$ nearest photons:
        $$ \hat{L}_i(\mathbf{x}, \omega_i) \approx \frac{1}{\pi r^2} \sum_{j=1}^{k} \frac{w_j}{\kappa_t(\mathbf{x}_j)} $$
        [G: This formula assumes a flat kernel and uniform distribution within the radius $r$. The term $\kappa_t(\mathbf{x}_j)$ accounts for the probability of the photon reaching $\mathbf{x}$ from $\mathbf{x}_j$.]

**Parameters:**
*   $N$: Total number of photons. Typical values range from $10^5$ to $10^7$ depending on scene complexity [S2, unofficial].
*   $k$: Number of nearest neighbors for density estimation. Typical values are 50-100 [G].
*   $r$: Search radius. Often adaptive based on $k$ and local photon density.

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Bias:** The method is biased because the density estimation depends on arbitrary parameters $k$ and $r$ [S2, unofficial].
*   **Artifacts:** Uneven photon distribution can cause visible artifacts such as noise or blotchiness [S2, unofficial].
*   **Memory:** Storing all photons requires significant memory for large scenes.

## Subtopics

### Caustic Photon Mapping
Focuses on storing photons that have undergone specular interactions to efficiently render caustics. Chosen when specular highlights and caustics are prominent. [Link to specific caustic mapping article if available, otherwise general Photon Mapping].

### Diffuse Photon Mapping
Stores photons that have undergone diffuse interactions for indirect lighting. Chosen for general global illumination.

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** $O(N \log N)$ for building the k-d tree and $O(\log N)$ for querying nearest neighbors [G].
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(N)$ to store all photons.
*   **Strengths:** Efficiently handles caustics and complex indirect lighting with less noise than pure path tracing for the same computational budget [S2, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** Biased result; requires tuning of $k$ and $r$; high memory usage; artifacts from uneven photon distribution [S2, unofficial].
*   **When to use:** Offline rendering where high-quality caustics and indirect lighting are required, and noise is unacceptable.
*   **When not to use:** Real-time rendering due to memory and preprocessing time; scenes requiring strict physical accuracy without bias.

## Background
Photon mapping was developed in 1995 by Henrik Wann Jensen, a Danish researcher [S2, unofficial]. It was created to address the limitations of ray tracing in efficiently handling indirect lighting and caustics. The method gained significant importance in global illumination research during the 2000s and was adopted by major renderers such as Maya Mental Ray [S2, unofficial].
