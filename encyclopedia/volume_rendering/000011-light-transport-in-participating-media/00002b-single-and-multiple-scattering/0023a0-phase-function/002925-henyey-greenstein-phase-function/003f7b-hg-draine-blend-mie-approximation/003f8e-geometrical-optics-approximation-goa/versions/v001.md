# Geometrical Optics Approximation (GOA)

## Overview
Geometrical Optics Approximation (GOA) is a method for calculating light scattering by spherical particles by superposing classical diffraction, geometrical reflection, and transmission effects [S1]. It is used when the particle size is large relative to the wavelength of light, providing a computationally efficient alternative to rigorous Lorenz-Mie theory [S1]. Unlike Lorenz-Mie theory, whose complexity scales linearly with the size parameter, GOA maintains a runtime independent of the particle radius, making it suitable for large particles ($r \ge 2 \mu m$) where high accuracy is still required [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs:**
- Particle radius $r$.
- Wavelength of incident light $\lambda$.
- Complex relative refractive index $\eta = \eta_r + i\eta_i$, where $\eta_r$ is the real part and $\eta_i$ is the imaginary part [S1].
- Scattering angle $\theta \in [0, \pi]$ [S1].

**Outputs:**
- Scattering amplitude functions $S_1(\theta, r)$ and $S_2(\theta, r)$ for two orthogonal polarizations [S1].
- Extinction cross section $C_t(r)$ [S1].
- Absorption cross section $C_a(r)$ (for absorbing particles) [S1].

**Data Structures:**
No specific data structures are required beyond standard floating-point variables for the parameters and intermediate calculations. The method is analytical and stateless per particle evaluation.

**Parameters:**
- Maximum order of internal reflections $p_{max}$: Set to 3. Higher orders ($p > 3$) carry negligible energy and yield results almost identical to $p=100$ [S1].
- Integer $l$: Ensures the scattering angle $\theta$ is within $[0, \pi]$ [S1].
- Sign $q$: $q=1$ if the incident ray hits the upper hemisphere, $q=-1$ if it hits the lower hemisphere [S1].

**Step-by-Step Method:**

1.  **Calculate Size Parameter:**
    Compute the size parameter $\alpha$:
    $$ \alpha = \frac{2\pi r}{\lambda} $$
    [S1]

2.  **Calculate Diffraction Amplitude:**
    Compute the diffraction amplitude functions $S_{D,1}$ and $S_{D,2}$. These are identical and independent of the particle's composition [S1]:
    $$ S_{D,1}(\theta, r) = S_{D,2}(\theta, r) = \alpha^2 \frac{J_1(\alpha \sin \theta)}{\alpha \sin \theta} $$
    where $J_1$ is the first-order Bessel function of the first kind [S1].

3.  **Iterate Over Reflection Orders ($p$):**
    For each order $p$ from $0$ to $p_{max}$ (inclusive):

    a.  **Determine Incident and Transmission Angles:**
        The deflection angle $\theta_p$ depends on the incident angle $\theta_i$ and the transmission angle $\theta_t$. These are related by Snell's law:
        $$ \sin \theta_i = \eta \sin \theta_t $$
        [S1]
        Note: $\theta_i$ is the variable of integration/summation for the ray optics contribution. The deflection angle for the $p$-th ray is:
        $$ \theta_p = 2p\theta_t - 2\theta_i - (p-1)\pi $$
        [S1]

    b.  **Map Deflection to Scattering Angle:**
        The scattering angle $\theta$ is related to the deflection angle $\theta_p$ by:
        $$ \theta = q(\theta_p - 2\pi l) $$
        where $l$ is an integer chosen such that $\theta \in [0, \pi]$ [S1].

    c.  **Calculate Fresnel Coefficients:**
        Compute the Fresnel reflection coefficients $R_1(\theta_i)$ and $R_2(\theta_i)$ for the two polarizations [S1].

    d.  **Calculate Coefficient $\epsilon_j(\theta_i)$:**
        For polarization $j \in \{1, 2\}$:
        $$ \epsilon_j(\theta_i) = \begin{cases} R_j(\theta_i), & p = 0 \\ (1 - R_j(\theta_i)^2)(-R_j(\theta_i))^{p-1}, & p > 0 \end{cases} $$
        [S1]

    e.  **Calculate Phase Terms:**
        Calculate the path phase $\phi_p$ and the focal phase $\phi_f$ [S1]:
        $$ \phi_p = 2\alpha(\cos \theta_1 - p\eta \cos \theta_t) $$
        *Note: The source uses $\cos \theta_1$ in the formula for $\phi_p$. Given the context of Snell's law and standard GOA derivations, this likely refers to $\cos \theta_i$ or a specific geometric term. However, strictly following the source text provided:*
        $$ \phi_p = 2\alpha(\cos \theta_1 - p\eta \cos \theta_t) $$
        [S1]
        
        Calculate the sign factor $s$:
        $$ s = \operatorname{sgn}\left(\frac{d\theta_p}{d\theta_i}\right) = \operatorname{sgn}\left(2p \frac{\tan \theta_t}{\tan \theta_i} - 2\right) $$
        [S1]
        
        Then the focal phase:
        $$ \phi_f = \frac{\pi}{2} \left( 1 + p - 2l - \frac{s}{2} - \frac{q}{2} \right) $$
        [S1]

    f.  **Calculate Total Phase:**
        $$ \phi = \phi_p + \phi_f $$
        [S1]

    g.  **Calculate Scattering Amplitude for Order $p$:**
        For polarization $j \in \{1, 2\}$:
        $$ S_j^{(p)}(\theta, r) = \alpha \epsilon_j(\theta_i) \sqrt{\frac{\sin 2\theta_i}{2 \sin \theta \left| \frac{d\theta_p}{d\theta_i} \right|}} e^{i\phi} $$
        [S1]

4.  **Sum Amplitudes:**
    Combine the diffraction and reflection/transmission amplitudes to get the total scattering amplitude functions $S_j(\theta, r)$ [S1]:
    $$ S_j(\theta, r) = \begin{cases} \sum_{p=0}^{\infty} S_j^{(p)}(\theta, r) + S_{D,j}(\theta, r), & \theta \in \left[0, \frac{\pi}{2}\right) \\ \sum_{p=0}^{\infty} S_j^{(p)}(\theta, r), & \theta \in \left[\frac{\pi}{2}, \pi\right] \end{cases} $$
    In practice, the sum is truncated at $p=3$ [S1].

5.  **Calculate Extinction Cross Section ($C_t$):**
    An analytical expression for $C_t(r)$ can be derived [S1]:
    $$ C_t(r) = 2\pi r^2 + \frac{2\pi r}{|k|} \sum_{p \in \mathcal{P}} \frac{\epsilon_j(0)}{|p/\eta - 1|} \cos(\phi_p + \phi_f) $$
    where $\mathcal{P} = \{1, 3, 5, \dots\}$ is the set of odd integers, and $k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}$ [S1].
    
    *Simplification:* Since $C_t$ relies on $S$ at $\theta=0$ and higher orders contribute little to forward scattering, the summation can be simplified by setting $p=1$ [S1]:
    $$ C_t(r) = 2\pi r^2 + \frac{4r\lambda\eta^2}{(\eta+1)^2|\eta-1|} \sin\left(\frac{4\pi r}{\lambda}(1-\eta)\right) $$
    [S1]

6.  **Calculate Absorption Cross Section ($C_a$):**
    For absorbing particles ($\eta_i > 0$), the absorption cross section is approximated by [S1]:
    $$ C_a(r) = \frac{16\pi^2 r^3 \eta_i}{3 \lambda \eta_r} \left[ \eta_r^3 - (\eta_r^2 - 1)^{\frac{3}{2}} \right] $$
    [S1]
    Alternatively, $C_a(r) = C_t(r) - C_s(r)$, where $C_s(r)$ is the scattering cross section calculated by integrating the squared amplitudes [S1].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
- **Small Particles:** GOA is validated for $r \ge 2 \mu m$. Discrepancies with Lorenz-Mie theory appear when $r \approx 1 \mu m$ and become large as $r$ decreases further [S1].
- **Absorbing Particles:** The formula for $C_a$ assumes specific approximations for the internal field. Ensure $\eta_r$ and $\eta_i$ are correctly extracted from the complex refractive index [S1].
- **Total Internal Reflection:** When $\sin \theta_i > \eta$, total internal reflection occurs. The ray does not transmit. The code must handle this by skipping the transmission contribution for that $p$ [S1].
- **Derivative Zero:** If $d\theta_p/d\theta_i = 0$, the amplitude formula involves division by zero. This corresponds to a rainbow angle. Special handling or limiting behavior is required, though not detailed in the source [S1].

## Operating characteristics
- **Time Complexity:** The runtime for GOA is independent of the particle radius $r$ [S1]. In contrast, Lorenz-Mie theory runtime grows linearly with the size parameter $\alpha$ (and thus $r$) [S1].
- **Memory Complexity:** Low. The method is analytical and does not require storing large series coefficients or tables, unlike Lorenz-Mie which may require precomputed tables for efficiency [S1].
- **Accuracy:** The relative mean squared error (RelMSE) between GOA and Lorenz-Mie theory is less than 0.01 when $r \ge 2 \mu m$ [S1].
- **Strengths:** High efficiency for large particles; comparable accuracy to Lorenz-Mie for $r \ge 2 \mu m$; simple analytical formulas for cross sections [S1].
- **Limitations:** Inaccurate for small particles ($r < 2 \mu m$); does not account for wave effects like interference ripples as accurately as Lorenz-Mie for very large particles, though it captures them better than simpler approximations [S1].
- **When to Use:** Use GOA when $r \ge 2 \mu m$. Switch to Lorenz-Mie theory when $r < 2 \mu m$ to balance accuracy and time complexity [S1].

## Background
Geometrical Optics Approximation (GOA) was introduced into computer graphics in the paper "Rendering discrete participating media using geometrical optics approximation" [S1]. It builds upon classical optics literature [18, 19, 61–65] cited in the source [S1]. The method is particularly useful in rendering translucent materials with large grain sizes, where rigorous Mie theory is too computationally expensive [S1].
