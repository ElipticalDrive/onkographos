# BSSRDF Approximation with Dipole and Single Scattering

Also called: BSSRDF Model with Dipole Diffusion Approximation, BSSRDF Rendering with Dipole Diffusion and Single Scattering, BSSRDF approximation, Dipole BSSRDF, Practical Model for Subsurface Light Transport

## Overview
This method approximates subsurface light transport by combining a dipole diffusion model for multiple scattering with a single-scattering term, allowing light to enter at one surface point and exit at another. It solves the problem of rendering translucent materials (e.g., skin, marble, milk) more efficiently than full Monte Carlo integration while capturing the diffuse glow that simple BRDFs miss. It differs from its parent [Volume Single Scattering (Monte Carlo with Point Light)](2293-volume-single-scattering-monte-carlo-with-point-light.md) by replacing the volumetric ray-marching of single scattering with an analytical diffusion solution for the bulk transport, and from [Analytical Approximation of Single Scattering Radiance](4244-analytical-approximation-of-single-scattering-radiance.md) by explicitly modeling the spatial spread of light via the dipole source configuration rather than just the angular distribution.

## How it works

### Inputs and Outputs
**Inputs:**
- Surface point of exit $x_o$ and outgoing direction $\omega_o$.
- Surface point of entry $x_i$ and incoming direction $\omega_i$.
- Material properties: absorption coefficient $\sigma_a$, scattering coefficient $\sigma_s$, mean cosine of scattering angle $g$, relative index of refraction $\eta$.
- Incident radiance $L_i(x_i, \omega_i)$ from the light source.
- Distance $s_i$ from the camera to the entry point $x_i$ (or observed distance).

**Outputs:**
- Outgoing radiance $L_o(x_o, \omega_o)$.

### Data Structures
The method relies on scalar and vector quantities derived from the radiative transfer equation. No complex data structures are required beyond standard geometric primitives (points, vectors) and material parameters.

Key derived parameters:
- Reduced scattering coefficient: $\sigma_{s0} = \sigma_s (1 - g)$
- Reduced extinction coefficient: $\sigma_{t0} = \sigma_{s0} + \sigma_a$
- Diffusion constant: $D = \frac{1}{3\sigma_{t0}}$
- Effective transport coefficient: $\sigma_{tr} = \sqrt{3\sigma_a \sigma_{t0}}$

### Formulas

**1. Radiative Transport and Diffusion Approximation**
Light propagation is governed by the radiative transport equation:
$$ (\omega \cdot \nabla)L(x, \omega) = -\sigma_t L(x, \omega) + \sigma_s \int_{4\pi} p(\omega, \omega') L(x, \omega') d\omega' + Q(x, \omega) $$
Under the diffusion approximation, radiance is expanded into fluence $\phi(x)$ and vector irradiance $E(x)$:
$$ L(x, \omega) = \frac{\phi(x)}{4\pi} + \frac{3}{4\pi} \omega \cdot E(x) $$
This yields the diffusion equation for fluence:
$$ D \nabla^2 \phi(x) = \sigma_a \phi(x) - Q_0(x) + 3D \nabla \cdot Q_1(x) $$
For an isotropic point source in an infinite medium, the fluence solution is:
$$ \phi(x) = \frac{\Phi e^{-\sigma_{tr} r(x)}}{4\pi D r(x)} $$
where $\Phi$ is source power and $r(x)$ is distance to the source.

**2. BSSRDF Definition**
The BSSRDF $S$ relates outgoing radiance at $x_o$ to incident flux at $x_i$:
$$ dL_o(x_o, \omega_o) = S(x_i, \omega_i; x_o, \omega_o) d\Phi_i(x_i, \omega_i) $$
The total outgoing radiance is computed by integrating over the surface area $A$ and incoming directions:
$$ L_o(x_o, \omega_o) = \int_A \int_{2\pi} S(x_i, \omega_i; x_o, \omega_o) (\omega_i \cdot \omega_o) L_i(x_i, \omega_i) d\omega_i dA(x_i) $$

**3. Dipole Approximation for BSSRDF**
For a semi-infinite plane-parallel medium, the diffuse BSSRDF $R_d$ is approximated using two isotropic point sources: a primary source at depth $z_1 = 1/\sigma_{t0}$ and a negative "sink" source at depth $z_2 = 4AD$, where $A$ is a boundary condition constant (often approximated or derived from $\eta$). The radiant exitance is the gradient of fluence at the surface.

**4. Single Scattering Term**
The single scattering component $L_o^{(1)}$ is computed analytically to handle near-surface effects and sharp shadows:
$$ L_o^{(1)}(x_o, \omega_o) = \frac{\sigma_s(x_o) F p(\omega_i \cdot \omega_o)}{\sigma_{tc}} e^{-s_{0i} \sigma_t(x_i)} e^{-s_{0o} \sigma_t(x_o)} L_i(x_i, \omega_i) $$
Where:
- $\sigma_{tc}$ is the total cross section (often $\sigma_t$).
- $F$ is the Fresnel transmission coefficient.
- $p$ is the phase function.
- $s_{0i}$ is the refracted distance through the material.
- $s_{0o}$ is the distance along the outgoing ray.

### Steps

1.  **Calculate Material Parameters:**
    Compute $\sigma_{s0}$, $\sigma_{t0}$, $D$, and $\sigma_{tr}$ from input $\sigma_a, \sigma_s, g$.

2.  **Determine Entry and Exit Points:**
    Identify $x_i$ (where light enters) and $x_o$ (where light exits). In a ray-tracing context, $x_o$ is the intersection point on the surface. $x_i$ is determined by tracing the light ray into the object.

3.  **Compute Refracted Distances:**
    -   **Outgoing distance ($s_{0o}$):** Pick a random distance along the refracted outgoing ray for single scattering evaluation:
        $$ s_{0o} = \frac{\ln(\xi)}{\sigma_t(x_o)} $$
        where $\xi \in ]0, 1]$ is a uniform random number [S1, unofficial].
    -   **Incoming refracted distance ($s_{0i}$):** Approximate the distance the light travels inside the medium using Snell's law. Assuming the light source is far away:
        $$ s_{0i} = s_i \frac{|\omega_i \cdot n_i|}{\sqrt{1 - \eta^2 (1 - |\omega_i \cdot n(x_i)|^2)}} $$
        where $s_i$ is the observed distance, $\eta$ is the index of refraction, $n_i$ is the normal at $x_o$, and $n(x_i)$ is the normal at $x_i$ [S1, unofficial].

4.  **Evaluate Diffusion Term (Multiple Scattering):**
    Calculate the fluence $\phi(x_o)$ using the dipole solution. Place a primary source at $z_1 = 1/\sigma_{t0}$ below $x_i$ and a sink at $z_2 = 4AD$. Compute the gradient of the fluence at $x_o$ to find the exitance.
    *Note:* For curved surfaces, enforce a minimum distance of $1/\sigma_{t0}$ between the source and the surface point to avoid singularities [S1, unofficial].

5.  **Evaluate Single Scattering Term:**
    Compute $L_o^{(1)}$ using the formula in Section 3.4. This term accounts for the direct path from $x_i$ to $x_o$ with one scattering event.

6.  **Combine Terms:**
    The final radiance is the sum of the diffuse BSSRDF contribution (from step 4) and the single scattering contribution (from step 5). If integrating over an area, sum these contributions for all relevant $x_i$ points.

### Pseudocode

```python
import math

def compute_bssrdf_dipole_single_scattering(
    x_o, omega_o,      # Exit point and direction
    x_i, omega_i,      # Entry point and direction
    s_i,               # Observed distance to entry point
    sigma_a, sigma_s,  # Absorption and scattering coefficients
    g,                 # Mean cosine of scattering angle
    eta,               # Index of refraction
    L_i,               # Incident radiance at x_i
    A_const            # Boundary condition constant (e.g., derived from eta)
):
    # 1. Material Parameters
    sigma_s0 = sigma_s * (1.0 - g)
    sigma_t0 = sigma_s0 + sigma_a
    sigma_t = sigma_s + sigma_a
    D = 1.0 / (3.0 * sigma_t0)
    sigma_tr = math.sqrt(3.0 * sigma_a * sigma_t0)
    
    # 2. Refracted Distances
    # Outgoing random distance for single scattering
    xi = random_uniform(0.0, 1.0)
    s_0o = math.log(xi) / sigma_t
    
    # Incoming refracted distance using Snell's law approximation
    # n_i is normal at x_o, n_xi is normal at x_i
    dot_ni_omega_i = dot(omega_i, normal_at(x_o))
    dot_nxi_omega_i = dot(omega_i, normal_at(x_i))
    
    denominator = math.sqrt(1.0 - (eta**2) * (1.0 - dot_nxi_omega_i**2))
    if denominator == 0:
        s_0i = 0.0 # Avoid division by zero
    else:
        s_0i = s_i * abs(dot_ni_omega_i) / denominator

    # 3. Single Scattering Term
    # Fresnel transmission F and Phase function p
    F = fresnel_transmission(omega_i, omega_o, eta)
    p = phase_function(dot(omega_i, omega_o))
    
    # Attenuation along path
    # Note: sigma_tc is typically sigma_t in this context
    single_scattering_term = (
        (sigma_s * F * p) / sigma_t *
        math.exp(-s_0i * sigma_t) *
        math.exp(-s_0o * sigma_t) *
        L_i
    )

    # 4. Diffusion Term (Dipole Approximation)
    # Distance from entry point x_i to exit point x_o on surface
    r_surface = distance(x_i, x_o)
    
    # Source positions relative to x_i (depth into medium)
    z1 = 1.0 / sigma_t0
    z2 = 4.0 * A_const * D
    
    # Distances from sources to exit point x_o
    # Assuming local flatness, sources are directly below x_i
    # Vector from x_i to x_o projected onto surface normal
    # For simplicity, assuming semi-infinite plane approximation locally
    dist_source1 = math.sqrt(r_surface**2 + z1**2)
    dist_source2 = math.sqrt(r_surface**2 + z2**2)
    
    # Fluence from primary source
    phi1 = (math.exp(-sigma_tr * dist_source1)) / (4.0 * math.pi * D * dist_source1)
    
    # Fluence from sink source (negative)
    phi2 = -(math.exp(-sigma_tr * dist_source2)) / (4.0 * math.pi * D * dist_source2)
    
    total_phi = phi1 + phi2
    
    # Gradient of fluence to get exitance (approximate for flat surface)
    # Exitance M = -D * d(phi)/dz at z=0
    # d(phi)/dz approx (phi(z=epsilon) - phi(z=0)) / epsilon
    # Or analytically for point source at depth z: d/dz (exp(-sigma_tr*r)/r)
    # Simplified exitance calculation for dipole:
    # M = (sigma_tr * phi1 * (1 + sigma_tr * z1)) / (4 * pi * z1^2) ... 
    # Using the gradient of the fluence solution directly:
    
    # Gradient of 1/r * exp(-sigma_tr * r) w.r.t z at surface
    # d/dz (exp(-sigma_tr * sqrt(r^2+z^2)) / sqrt(r^2+z^2))
    # evaluated at z=0 for source at depth z_s:
    # Result is proportional to (1 + sigma_tr * z_s) * exp(-sigma_tr * r) / r^2
    
    exitance1 = (sigma_tr * z1 + 1.0) * math.exp(-sigma_tr * dist_source1) / (dist_source1**2)
    exitance2 = (sigma_tr * z2 + 1.0) * math.exp(-sigma_tr * dist_source2) / (dist_source2**2)
    
    diffusion_radiance = D * (exitance1 - exitance2) / (4.0 * math.pi) # Normalization factor may vary
    
    # 5. Combine
    # The BSSRDF S is effectively the coefficient relating flux to radiance.
    # Here we return the computed radiance contribution.
    return diffusion_radiance + single_scattering_term
```

### Parameters
- $\sigma_a$: Absorption coefficient. Typical values for skin: $0.01 - 0.1 \text{ mm}^{-1}$.
- $\sigma_s$: Scattering coefficient. Typical values for skin: $1.0 - 10.0 \text{ mm}^{-1}$.
- $g$: Mean cosine of scattering angle. Typical values for skin: $0.7 - 0.9$ (forward scattering).
- $\eta$: Index of refraction. Typical value for skin: $1.4$.
- $A$: Boundary condition constant. Often approximated as $A \approx \frac{1}{3} \frac{1+R_{eff}}{1-R_{eff}}$ where $R_{eff}$ is the effective reflectance [S1, unofficial].

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
- **Hair Darkness:** The back of the head appears slightly darker in BSSRDF simulations compared to full Monte Carlo. This is attributed to the forced $1/\sigma_{t0}$ distance in the diffusion approximation, which may not accurately model light transport in thin or highly curved regions [S1, unofficial].
- **Curved Surfaces:** On highly curved surfaces, the assumption of a flat semi-infinite plane breaks down. The method handles this by evaluating the diffusion term with a minimum distance of $1/\sigma_{t0}$ to eliminate singularities at sharp edges [S1, unofficial].
- **Singularities:** Placing the source arbitrarily close to the exit point $x_o$ can cause numerical instability. The minimum distance constraint prevents this.

## Operating characteristics

**Time Complexity:**
The method is significantly faster than full Monte Carlo integration. In the source example, rendering a milk glass took 4 minutes with BSSRDF compared to 2 minutes for a simple BRDF, and vastly faster than the 1250 minutes required for a full Monte Carlo simulation of the same scene [S1, unofficial]. The complexity is dominated by the evaluation of the dipole terms and the single scattering term, which are analytical and do not require path tracing.

**Memory Complexity:**
Low. The method requires storing only material parameters and geometric data. It does not require storing photon maps or volumetric grids, unlike photon mapping methods which require memory proportional to the number of photons [S1, unofficial].

**Strengths:**
- Captures the diffuse glow of subsurface scattering.
- Much faster than Monte Carlo methods.
- Analytical solution allows for efficient evaluation.
- Handles both single and multiple scattering effects.

**Limitations:**
- Approximation errors in thin or highly curved regions (e.g., hair).
- Assumes semi-infinite plane geometry locally; global geometry effects are approximated.
- Does not handle multiple layers or complex global illumination natively (though extensions are planned) [S1, unofficial].

**When to Use:**
- Rendering translucent materials like skin, marble, milk, or wax.
- When performance is critical and full Monte Carlo is too slow.
- When the geometry is relatively smooth or can be approximated as locally flat.

**When Not to Use:**
- When high accuracy is required for thin structures (e.g., hair, leaves).
- When the material has multiple layers with distinct optical properties.
- When global illumination effects within the volume are complex and non-local.

## Background

The BSSRDF (Bidirectional Surface Scattering Reflectance Distribution Function) was introduced to model light transport in translucent materials where light enters at one point and exits at another. The dipole approximation for the BSSRDF was presented in a paper for the SIGGRAPH conference proceedings [S1, unofficial]. The research was funded by the National Science Foundation (IIS-0085864) and DARPA (DABT63-95-C-0085) [S1, unofficial]. The model is based on the diffusion approximation of the radiative transfer equation, which assumes that light distribution becomes isotropic in highly scattering media. The method combines this diffusion model with a single-scattering term to improve accuracy near the surface.
