# Extended Surface Boundary Derivative

Also called: Extended boundary derivative, Extended form of surface boundary derivatives

## Overview
The Extended Surface Boundary Derivative is a method for computing the gradient of rendered radiance with respect to scene parameters by treating silhouette occlusion as a probabilistic surface existence rather than a hard geometric boundary. It solves the problem that conventional boundary derivatives are undefined or zero for non-silhouette intersections, allowing optimization of concave regions and general surface interactions. It differs from its sibling, [Extended Surface Derivatives](23191-extended-surface-derivatives.md), by specifically extending the *boundary* term (occlusion change) to arbitrary surface normals, whereas the sibling likely extends the shading term. It builds on [Relative Motion Derivative](23179-relative-motion-derivative.md) by providing the specific geometric formulation for the boundary component in the many-worlds transport equation.

## How it works
### Inputs and Outputs
**Inputs:**
*   Scene parameters $\theta$ (e.g., implicit field values).
*   Ray origin $\mathbf{x}_a$ and direction $\boldsymbol{\omega}$.
*   Interaction point $\mathbf{x}_b$ on a surface.
*   Surface normal $\mathbf{N}_b$ at $\mathbf{x}_b$.
*   Occupancy value $\alpha(\mathbf{x}_b)$ and its derivative $\partial_\theta \alpha(\mathbf{x}_b)$.
*   Outgoing radiance $L_d(\mathbf{x}_b, -\boldsymbol{\omega})$ and visibility weight $W_o(\mathbf{x}_b, -\boldsymbol{\omega})$.

**Outputs:**
*   The boundary derivative contribution $\left[ \frac{\partial I}{\partial \theta} \right]_b$ to the total image gradient.

### Data Structures
The method operates on vector quantities in 3D Euclidean space. No specific sparse data structures are required for the derivative calculation itself, though the occupancy field $\alpha$ is typically stored in a grid or neural network.

### Steps
1.  **Define Extended Silhouette Direction:** Calculate the in-surface direction $\mathbf{t}_b$ along which the dot product $\boldsymbol{\omega} \cdot \mathbf{N}_b$ remains constant. This is the projection of the viewing direction onto the tangent plane, or specifically the direction orthogonal to the projection of the normal.
    *   Mathematically, $\mathbf{t}_b$ is orthogonal to $\mathbf{N}_b$ and lies in the plane spanned by $\mathbf{N}_b$ and $\boldsymbol{\omega}$.
    *   $\mathbf{t}_b = \frac{\mathbf{N}_b \times (\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{N}_b)}{\|\mathbf{N}_b \times (\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf}_b)\|}$ (assuming $\boldsymbol{\omega} \cdot \mathbf{N}_b \neq \pm 1$). If $\boldsymbol{\omega} \cdot \mathbf{N}_b = 0$ (true silhouette), $\mathbf{t}_b$ is the standard silhouette tangent.

2.  **Define Viewing Direction Normal:** Calculate $\mathbf{n}_b$, the normal to the viewing direction in the context of the extended boundary. $\mathbf{n}_b$ is orthogonal to both $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ and $\mathbf{t}_b$.
    *   $\mathbf{n}_b = \frac{\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{t}_b}{\|\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{t}_b\|}$.
    *   Note: In the extended case, $\mathbf{N}_b \neq \mathbf{n}_b$. They are equal only on the visibility silhouette where $\boldsymbol{\omega} \cdot \mathbf{N}_b = 0$.

3.  **Define Orthonormal Basis:** Construct a unit vector $\mathbf{m}_b$ in the surface plane such that $\{\mathbf{t}_b, \mathbf{N}_b, \mathbf{m}_b\}$ forms an orthonormal basis.
    *   $\mathbf{m}_b = \mathbf{N}_b \times \mathbf{t}_b$.
    *   Note: $\mathbf{m}_b$, $\mathbf{n}_b$, and $\mathbf{N}_b$ are co-planar because they are all orthogonal to $\mathbf{t}_b$.

4.  **Compute Local Motion:** Calculate the local motion $\mathbf{v}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_b)$, which quantifies the rate at which occlusion changes at the interaction point.
    *   $\mathbf{v}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_b) = \partial_\theta \alpha(\mathbf{x}_b)$.
    *   This is defined in canonical space.

5.  **Calculate Geometric Factors:** Compute the terms required for the simplified integral.
    *   Identity check: $\|\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{t}_b\| \|\mathbf{n}_b \times \mathbf{m}_b\| = |\boldsymbol{\omega} \cdot \mathbf{m}_b|$. This identity simplifies the derivation from the general many-worlds boundary integral.
    *   The simplified boundary derivative is given by:
        $$ \left[ \frac{\partial I}{\partial \theta} \right]_b = \int_{\mathcal{R}^3} \int_{\mathcal{S}^2} W_o(\mathbf{x}_b, -\boldsymbol{\omega}) L_d(\mathbf{x}_b, -\boldsymbol{\omega}) \mathbf{v}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_b) d\boldsymbol{\omega} dV(\mathbf{x}_b) $$
    *   In practice, for a single ray interaction, this collapses to a point evaluation proportional to $W_o L_d \mathbf{v}_\theta$.

### Formulas
*   **Local Motion:**
    $$ \mathbf{v}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_b) := \partial_\theta \mathbf{x}_b \cdot \mathbf{N}_b = \partial_\theta \alpha(\mathbf{x}_b) \quad (17, 25) $$
*   **Viewing Direction Normal:**
    $$ \mathbf{n}_b \perp \boldsymbol{\omega}, \quad \mathbf{n}_b \perp \mathbf{t}_b $$
*   **Orthonormal Basis:**
    $$ \{\mathbf{t}_b, \mathbf{N}_b, \mathbf{m}_b\} \text{ is orthonormal} $$
*   **Identity:**
    $$ \|\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{t}_b\| \|\mathbf{n}_b \times \mathbf{m}_b\| = |\boldsymbol{\omega} \cdot \mathbf{m}_b| \quad (23) $$
*   **Final Simplified Boundary Derivative:**
    $$ \left[ \frac{\partial I}{\partial \theta} \right]_b = \int_{\mathcal{R}^3} \int_{\mathcal{S}^2} W_o(\mathbf{x}_b, -\boldsymbol{\omega}) L_d(\mathbf{x}_b, -\boldsymbol{\omega}) \mathbf{v}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_b) d\boldsymbol{\omega} dV(\mathbf{x}_b) \quad (24) $$

### Parameters
*   $\theta$: Scene parameters.
*   $\alpha(\mathbf{x}_b)$: Occupancy value at $\mathbf{x}_b$.

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Gradient Norm Bias:** Prior work using implicit fields defines motion in scene space with a Jacobian determinant $\partial_{\theta} \mathbf{p} \cdot \mathbf{N} = \frac{\partial_{\theta} \alpha(\mathbf{p})}{\|\nabla \alpha(\mathbf{p})\|}$ [S1]. This scaling factor biases the many-worlds derivative by erroneously considering neighboring occupancy values.
*   **Infinite Gradient:** The term $\|\nabla \alpha\|$ can be infinitely large near the mean surface, causing instability if the prior formulation is used.
*   **Zero Gradient:** During initialization, if all positions share the same occupancy value, $\|\nabla \alpha\|$ drops to zero, causing division by zero or vanishing gradients in the prior formulation.
*   **Solution:** The Extended Surface Boundary Derivative avoids this by defining $\mathbf{v}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_b) = \partial_\theta \alpha(\mathbf{x}_b)$ directly in canonical space, optimizing the surface patch directly by modifying its occupancy value rather than locally deforming it [S1].

### Pseudocode
```python
def extended_surface_boundary_derivative(
    omega: Vector3,      # Viewing direction
    x_b: Vector3,        # Interaction point
    N_b: Vector3,        # Surface normal at x_b
    d_alpha_d_theta: float, # Derivative of occupancy w.r.t. theta
    W_o: float,          # Visibility weight
    L_d: float           # Outgoing radiance
) -> float:
    """
    Computes the extended surface boundary derivative contribution.
    
    Args:
        omega: Unit vector of viewing direction.
        x_b: Position of surface interaction.
        N_b: Normalized surface normal at x_b.
        d_alpha_d_theta: Gradient of occupancy value alpha w.r.t. scene parameters theta.
        W_o: Visibility weight W_o(x_b, -omega).
        L_d: Outgoing radiance L_d(x_b, -omega).
        
    Returns:
        The boundary derivative contribution [dI/dtheta]_b.
    """
    
    # Step 1: Compute extended silhouette direction t_b
    # t_b is the in-surface direction along which omega . N_b is constant.
    # It is orthogonal to N_b and lies in the plane of N_b and omega.
    # t_b = (N_b x (omega x N_b)) / ||N_b x (omega x N_b)||
    
    # Handle degenerate case where omega is parallel to N_b
    if abs(dot(omega, N_b)) > 0.999999:
        # If ray is parallel to normal, any tangent is valid. 
        # Use a fixed arbitrary tangent orthogonal to N_b.
        # [G] Pick an arbitrary vector not parallel to N_b
        if abs(N_b.x) < 0.9:
            arbitrary = Vector3(1, 0, 0)
        else:
            arbitrary = Vector3(0, 1, 0)
        t_b = normalize(cross(N_b, arbitrary))
    else:
        # Compute cross product N_b x (omega x N_b)
        # Using vector triple product identity: A x (B x C) = B(A.C) - C(A.B)
        # N_b x (omega x N_b) = omega(dot(N_b, N_b)) - N_b(dot(N_b, omega))
        # Since N_b is unit, dot(N_b, N_b) = 1
        temp = omega - N_b * dot(N_b, omega)
        norm_temp = length(temp)
        if norm_temp < 1e-8:
            # Degenerate case: omega parallel to N_b
            if abs(N_b.x) < 0.9:
                arbitrary = Vector3(1, 0, 0)
            else:
                arbitrary = Vector3(0, 1, 0)
            t_b = normalize(cross(N_b, arbitrary))
        else:
            t_b = temp / norm_temp

    # Step 2: Compute viewing direction normal n_b
    # n_b is orthogonal to omega and t_b
    # n_b = (omega x t_b) / ||omega x t_b||
    
    cross_omega_t = cross(omega, t_b)
    norm_cross = length(cross_omega_t)
    
    if norm_cross < 1e-8:
        # omega is parallel to t_b. This implies omega is in the tangent plane.
        # n_b is orthogonal to both.
        # [G] Use N_b as n_b if not parallel, else arbitrary
        if abs(dot(N_b, cross_omega_t)) > 1e-8: # Should not happen if logic is correct
             n_b = N_b
        else:
             # Fallback: n_b is orthogonal to omega.
             # Since omega is in tangent plane, N_b is orthogonal to omega.
             n_b = N_b
    else:
        n_b = cross_omega_t / norm_cross

    # Step 3: Compute m_b
    # m_b = N_b x t_b
    m_b = cross(N_b, t_b)
    # m_b is already unit length if N_b and t_b are orthonormal

    # Step 4: Compute local motion v_theta
    # v_theta = d_alpha_d_theta
    v_theta = d_alpha_d_theta

    # Step 5: Compute final derivative
    # [dI/dtheta]_b = W_o * L_d * v_theta
    # Note: The integral form simplifies to this point evaluation for a single interaction.
    
    return W_o * L_d * v_theta
```

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** $O(1)$ per surface interaction for the derivative calculation, assuming the occupancy gradient $\partial_\theta \alpha$ is available.
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(1)$ additional memory.
*   **Strengths:**
    *   Well-defined for any surface, not just visibility silhouettes.
    *   Allows optimization of concave regions (inner valleys) that have zero shading derivative under uniform lighting.
    *   Avoids bias from neighboring occupancy values by operating in canonical space.
    *   Stable during initialization (no division by zero from $\|\nabla \alpha\|$).
*   **Limitations:**
    *   Requires computation of extended geometric basis vectors ($\mathbf{t}_b, \mathbf{n}_b, \mathbf{m}_b$).
    *   Relies on the validity of the many-worlds transport formulation.
*   **When to use:** When optimizing implicit surfaces or neural radiance fields where gradients are needed for non-silhouette points, or when standard boundary derivatives vanish.
*   **When not to use:** When computational cost of basis vector calculation is prohibitive and only silhouette gradients are needed.

## Background
The Extended Surface Boundary Derivative was introduced in "Many-Worlds Inverse Rendering" [S1]. It addresses the limitation of conventional boundary derivatives which are restricted to visibility silhouettes. The method interprets silhouette occlusion as probabilistic surface existence, allowing the derivative to be extended to hypothetical surfaces with non-tangential ray intersections. It contrasts with prior work [Stam and Schmidt 2011] that uses an implicit field representation with a Jacobian determinant involving $\|\nabla \alpha\|$, which can cause bias and instability.
