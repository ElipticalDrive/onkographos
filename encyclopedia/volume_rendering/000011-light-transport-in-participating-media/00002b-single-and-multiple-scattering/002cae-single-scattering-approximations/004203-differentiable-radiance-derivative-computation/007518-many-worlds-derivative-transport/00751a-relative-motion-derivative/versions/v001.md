# Relative Motion Derivative

Also called: Boundary segment derivative, Occlusion-induced derivative

## Overview
The relative motion derivative is a gradient component in differentiable volume rendering that arises from the geometric displacement of boundary segments relative to the shading point during indirect shading [S1]. It addresses the problem of missing gradients in concave regions (e.g., inner valleys of a torus) where standard surface derivatives vanish because no visible silhouettes are formed [S1]. Unlike standard many-worlds derivatives which extend perturbations from the interaction point, this derivative originates from the fixed background surface, allowing optimization of self-occluded areas [S1].

## How it works
The relative motion derivative captures the sensitivity of the radiance at a shading point to changes in the geometry of occluding boundaries that are not the point of interaction itself [S1].

**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** The fixed background surface geometry $\bar{S}$, the shading point $x$, and the boundary segment $B$ causing occlusion.
*   **Output:** The derivative of the radiance with respect to the parameters of $\bar{S}$, propagated to the shading point $x$ [S1].

**Data Structures:**
No specific data structures are defined for this derivative in the sources. It is treated as a theoretical gradient component derived from the path tracer's geometry [S1].

**Steps:**
1.  Identify the boundary segment $B$ (part of $\bar{S}$) that occludes the light path or contributes to self-occlusion at the shading point $x$ [S1].
2.  Recognize that a perturbation in the parameters of $\bar{S}$ causes a "relative motion" of $B$ with respect to $x$ [S1].
3.  Compute the derivative of the occlusion status (or transmittance) with respect to the parameters of $\bar{S}$ [S1].
4.  Propagate this boundary derivative to the shading point $x$ [S1].

**Formulas:**
The sources do not provide explicit mathematical formulas for the relative motion derivative. They describe it qualitatively as arising from the "relative motion of the shadow ray" or boundary segment [S1]. The derivative is distinct from direct perturbations to sampled positions [S1].

**Parameters:**
Not given in the sources.

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Negligible Impact:** In practice, this derivative rarely affects optimization unless the shading derivative is nearly zero [S1].
*   **Concave Regions:** It is specifically useful for concave regions where standard visibility contour optimization fails to provide gradients [S1].
*   **Origin Difference:** Unlike standard many-worlds derivatives, this derivative cannot be extended from the point of interaction into space because it originates from the fixed background surface $\bar{S}$ [S1].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
def compute_relative_motion_derivative(shading_point, background_surface, boundary_segment):
    """
    Approximates the relative motion derivative by propagating boundary derivatives
    to the shading point.
    
    Note: Exact formula not provided in sources. This is a conceptual implementation
    of the approximation described in [S1].
    """
    # 1. Identify the boundary segment causing occlusion
    # In a path tracer, this is determined during shadow ray evaluation
    occluding_segment = boundary_segment
    
    # 2. Compute the derivative of the occlusion/transmittance with respect to
    # the parameters of the background surface.
    # This represents the "relative motion" of the segment relative to the shading point.
    # [G] Assuming a standard geometric derivative computation for surface perturbation
    # affecting the ray intersection or transmittance.
    boundary_derivative = compute_surface_perturbation_derivative(
        occluding_segment, 
        shading_point
    )
    
    # 3. Propagate this derivative to the shading point.
    # The sources state that this propagation is an approximation to recover
    # gradients in concave regions where shading derivatives are zero.
    relative_motion_gradient = propagate_to_shading_point(
        boundary_derivative, 
        shading_point
    )
    
    return relative_motion_gradient
```

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Not given in the sources.
*   **Memory Complexity:** Not given in the sources.
*   **Performance:** The derivative rarely affects optimization except when the shading derivative is nearly zero [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Enables optimization of concave regions (e.g., inner valleys) that have zero shading derivative under uniform lighting [S1].
*   **Limitations:** The standard many-worlds extension technique does not apply because the derivative originates from the fixed background surface rather than the point of interaction [S1].
*   **When to use:** Use when optimizing visibility contours in concave regions where standard derivatives vanish.
*   **When not to use:** Use with caution in regions with strong shading derivatives, as the impact is negligible [S1].

## Background
The relative motion derivative was introduced in the context of "Many-Worlds Inverse Rendering" [S1]. It was developed to address limitations in differentiable rendering where concave regions fail to receive gradients due to the absence of visible silhouettes [S1]. The method is part of the broader "Many-Worlds Derivative Transport" framework, which treats potential surfaces as mutually exclusive events [S1].
