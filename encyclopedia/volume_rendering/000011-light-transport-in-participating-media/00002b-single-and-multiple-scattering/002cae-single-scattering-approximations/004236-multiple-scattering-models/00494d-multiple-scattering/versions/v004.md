# Multiple Scattering

Also called: Multiple Scattering Simulation

## Overview
Multiple scattering simulates the effect resulting from more than one scattering bounce within a participating medium [S1]. It solves the problem of rendering dense media, such as thick clouds or smoke, where single scattering approximations fail to capture the correct brightness and color, rendering them dark and lifeless [S2, unofficial]. It is distinguished from single scattering by the inclusion of higher-order scattering terms in the radiative transfer equation, requiring the simulation of photons bouncing dozens or hundreds of times before escaping [S2, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** Volume density fields (e.g., OpenVDB), extinction coefficient $\sigma_t$, scattering coefficient $\sigma_s$, phase function $p(\omega_i, \omega_o)$, and a maximum bounce count parameter (Volume Trace Depth) [S1][S2, unofficial].
*   **Output:** Final radiance $L$ including the multiple-scattered component $L_{MS}$ [Parent].

**Data Structures:**
The method relies on standard volume rendering data structures. Density is typically stored in sparse voxel grids (e.g., OpenVDB) to allow efficient traversal [S2, unofficial]. The radiance accumulation buffer stores the integrated transmittance and emitted/scattered light along the ray [S1].

**Steps:**
1.  **Ray Initialization:** Cast a primary ray from the camera into the scene. Initialize accumulated transmittance $T = 1.0$ and accumulated radiance $L_{acc} = 0.0$ [S1][S2, unofficial].
2.  **Marching Loop:** Step through the volume along the ray. At each step, sample the density $\sigma_t$ and scattering coefficient $\sigma_s$ [S2, unofficial].
3.  **Scattering Event:** Determine if a scattering event occurs based on the optical depth. If a scatter occurs, calculate the new direction using the phase function $p(\omega_i, \omega_o)$ [S1][S2, unofficial].
4.  **Bounce Accumulation:** Add the scattered light contribution to $L_{acc}$, weighted by the current transmittance $T$ and the single scattering albedo $\omega_0 = \sigma_s / \sigma_t$ [Parent, S2].
5.  **Update Transmittance:** Update $T$ by the extinction over the step size: $T_{new} = T \cdot e^{-\sigma_t \Delta s}$ [S1][S2, unofficial].
6.  **Bounce Limit Check:** Increment the bounce counter. If the counter exceeds the **Volume Trace Depth** parameter, terminate the multiple scattering contribution for this ray segment [S2, unofficial].
7.  **Iteration:** If the bounce limit is not reached and the ray is still inside the volume, repeat from step 2 with the new direction [S2, unofficial].
8.  **Final Compositing:** Combine $L_{acc}$ with the background and object radiance [Parent].

**Parameters:**
*   **Volume Trace Depth:** Controls the number of scattering bounces simulated. Typical values tested include 1 (single scattering), 5, and 31 [S2, unofficial].
*   **Scattering Strength:** The scattering coefficient must be high enough for the intensity of scattered light to be visible [S2, unofficial].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Noise:** Unbiased rendering of multiple scattering can be noisy due to the high variance of paths with many bounces [S1].
*   **Denoising Difficulty:** Volumes are notoriously harder to denoise than surfaces because helper data like surface normals does not exist inside a cloud [S2, unofficial].
*   **Performance:** Every added bounce multiplies the computational work, making dense media expensive to render [S2, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Exponential with respect to the number of bounces. Simulating multiple scattering involves following each ray through many additional bounces, and every added bounce multiplies the computational work [S2, unofficial].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Depends on the volume data structure (e.g., OpenVDB) and the recursion depth for path tracing [S2, unofficial].
*   **Strengths:** Captures real-world effects like scattering in clouds and subsurface scattering where light bounces many times [S1]. A thick cumulus cloud reflects up to 90 percent of incident light, which single scattering cannot replicate [S2, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** Computationally expensive; unbiased rendering is noisy [S1]. Volumes are harder to denoise than surfaces [S2, unofficial].
*   **When to use:** For dense media like clouds, smoke, or thick fog where single scattering appears dark and lifeless [S2, unofficial].
*   **When not to use:** For thin media like light haze, where single scattering is sufficient and faster [S2, unofficial]. For materials with well-defined surfaces like skin or milk, subsurface scattering is more efficient [S1].

## Background
The distinction between single and multiple scattering is fundamental in volume rendering. Single scattering simulates only one bounce, which is sufficient for thin media but fails for dense clouds [S2, unofficial]. Multiple scattering requires simulating photons bouncing dozens or hundreds of times [S2, unofficial]. The effect is required for real-world phenomena like cloud scattering and subsurface scattering [S1]. In practice, unbiased rendering of these effects is noisy, leading to limitations in default render settings (e.g., zero bounces in Cycles by default) [S1].
