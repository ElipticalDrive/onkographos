# Multiple Scattering

Also called: Multiple Scattering Simulation

## Overview
Multiple scattering simulates the effect resulting from more than one scattering bounce, where photons bounce dozens or hundreds of times inside a volume before escaping [S1][S2, unofficial]. It solves the problem of rendering dense media, such as thick cumulus clouds or subsurface scattering materials, which appear dark and lifeless under single scattering approximations [S2, unofficial]. Unlike single scattering, which suffices for thin media like light haze, multiple scattering is required to capture the high reflectivity of dense volumes, where a thick cloud can reflect up to 90 percent of incident light [S2, unofficial].

## How it works
Multiple scattering extends the ray marching process by allowing rays to undergo multiple direction changes (bounces) within the participating medium. The method follows each ray through the volume, accumulating in-scattered light from all directions at each step, rather than stopping after the first interaction.

**Inputs and Outputs**
*   **Inputs:** Volume density field (e.g., OpenVDB), extinction coefficient $\sigma_t$, scattering coefficient $\sigma_s$, absorption coefficient $\sigma_a$, phase function $p(\omega_i, \omega_o)$, light sources, and camera ray.
*   **Outputs:** Radiance $L$ along the ray.

**Data Structures**
*   **Volume Grid:** Typically stored in formats like OpenVDB, which holds sparse voxel data for efficient traversal [S2, unofficial].
*   **Ray State:** Position, direction, accumulated transmittance, and accumulated radiance.

**Parameters**
*   **Volume Trace Depth:** Controls the maximum number of scattering bounces simulated [S2, unofficial]. Typical values tested include 1, 5, and 31 [S2, unofficial].
*   **Scattering Strength:** Must be high enough for the intensity of scattered light to be visible [S2, unofficial].

**Algorithm Steps**
1.  Initialize accumulated radiance $L_{acc} = 0$ and transmittance $T = 1$.
2.  March along the camera ray through the volume grid.
3.  At each step, sample the local density and coefficients.
4.  Calculate the probability of scattering based on $\sigma_s$ and step size.
5.  If scattering occurs:
    a.  Sample a new direction using the phase function $p(\omega_i, \omega_o)$.
    b.  Add in-scattered light from all directions (or approximated sources) to $L_{acc}$, weighted by $T$.
    c.  Update $T$ by the extinction along the step.
    d.  If the bounce count is less than `Volume Trace Depth`, continue marching in the new direction; otherwise, terminate the path.
6.  Return $L_{acc}$.

**Pitfalls**
*   **Noise:** Unbiased rendering of multiple scattering can be noisy due to the high variance in path lengths and scattering events [S1].
*   **Performance:** Every added bounce multiplies the computational work, making dense media expensive to render [S2, unofficial].
*   **Denoising Difficulty:** Volumes are notoriously harder to denoise than surfaces because helper data like surface normals does not exist inside a cloud [S2, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Increases significantly with the number of bounces. Each additional bounce multiplies the work required per ray [S2, unofficial].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Depends on the volume data structure (e.g., OpenVDB) and the depth of the ray tree.
*   **Strengths:** Accurately renders dense, bright media like clouds and smoke, which single scattering fails to represent correctly [S2, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** Computationally expensive; prone to noise in unbiased implementations; difficult to denoise due to lack of geometric auxiliary data [S1][S2, unofficial].
*   **When to use:** For dense clouds, smoke, or subsurface scattering materials where light penetrates deeply and scatters multiple times [S1].
*   **When not to use:** For thin media like light haze, where single scattering is sufficient and faster [S2, unofficial].

## Background
The distinction between single and multiple scattering is fundamental in volume rendering. Single scattering simulates only one bounce, which is efficient but visually inadequate for dense media [S2, unofficial]. Multiple scattering was recognized as necessary for real-world effects like scattering in clouds or subsurface scattering, which require many scattering bounces [S1]. In practice, unbiased rendering engines often limit the number of bounces by default (e.g., zero in Cycles) to manage noise and render times, requiring explicit enabling for multiple scattering [S1]. For materials like skin or milk, subsurface scattering methods (e.g., random walk) are often preferred over general volume multiple scattering for efficiency and control [S1]. The industry standard for storing volume data, OpenVDB, was developed at DreamWorks Animation and open-sourced in 2012, facilitating the storage and traversal of the complex density fields required for these simulations [S2, unofficial].
