# Multiple Scattering

Also called: Multiple Scattering Simulation

## Overview
Multiple scattering simulates the effect resulting from more than one scattering bounce within a participating medium [S1]. It solves the problem of rendering dense media, such as thick clouds or smoke, where single scattering approximations fail to capture the bright, lifeless appearance caused by photons bouncing dozens or hundreds of times before escaping [S2, unofficial]. It is distinguished from single scattering approximations by its explicit or iterative accumulation of indirect in-scattering from multiple directions, whereas single scattering only accounts for direct light interaction [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** Volume density field, scattering coefficient $\sigma_s$, absorption coefficient $\sigma_a$, phase function $p(\omega_i, \omega_o)$, and a **Volume Trace Depth** parameter controlling the maximum number of bounces [S2, unofficial].
*   **Output:** Radiance $L$ for each pixel, accounting for light that has undergone $n > 1$ scattering events.

**Data Structures:**
*   **Volume Trace Depth:** An integer parameter specifying the maximum number of scattering bounces to simulate. Typical tested values include 1 (single scattering), 5, and 31 [S2, unofficial].
*   **Scattering Strength:** A parameter in the volume shader that must be sufficiently high for the intensity of scattered light to be visible [S2, unofficial].

**Method:**
The core mechanism involves following rays through the volume and accumulating radiance from multiple bounces. While the parent article [10687-multiple-scattering-models.md] outlines general approaches (recursive ray casting, approximate integration, hybrid), this specific method focuses on the conceptual implementation of multi-bounce accumulation.

1.  **Initialize:** Set the current radiance $L_{current}$ to the emitted light or direct illumination at the starting point. Set bounce counter $n = 0$.
2.  **Ray Marching:** Trace a ray through the volume. At each step, compute the optical depth and sample the density.
3.  **Bounce Accumulation:**
    *   If $n <$ Volume Trace Depth:
        *   Compute the in-scattering term. This requires integrating radiance arriving from all directions $\omega_i$, weighted by the phase function and scattering coefficient:
            $$ L_{in}(x, \omega_o) = \sigma_s(x) \int_{S^2} p(\omega_i, \omega_o) L(x, \omega_i) d\omega_i $$
        *   This integration can be performed via:
            *   **Recursive Ray Casting:** Explicitly tracing new rays for indirect bounces [10687-multiple-scattering-models.md].
            *   **Approximate Integration:** Using precomputed filters or angular distributions to estimate the integral without explicit path tracing [10687-multiple-scattering-models.md].
        *   Add $L_{in}$ to $L_{current}$.
        *   Increment $n$.
    *   If $n \ge$ Volume Trace Depth:
        *   Terminate the bounce chain.
4.  **Attenuation:** Apply Beer-Lambert attenuation for the path segment traveled.
5.  **Composite:** Combine the accumulated radiance with the background and any direct transmission.

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Noise:** Unbiased rendering of multiple scattering can be noisy [S1].
*   **Performance:** The number of bounces must be limited to keep render times acceptable, as every added bounce multiplies computational work [S2, unofficial].
*   **Denoising:** Volumes are notoriously harder to denoise than surfaces because helper data like surface normals does not exist inside a cloud [S2, unofficial].
*   **Visibility:** Scattering strength must be high enough for the intensity of scattered light within the volume to be visible [S2, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Complexity:** Time complexity increases exponentially with the number of bounces, as every added bounce multiplies the work [S2, unofficial].
*   **Memory:** Depends on the storage of the volume density field (e.g., OpenVDB) and the recursion depth or filter sizes used for in-scattering approximation [S2, unofficial].
*   **Strengths:** Accurately renders dense media like thick cumulus clouds, which reflect up to 90 percent of incident light, and subsurface scattering effects [S2, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** Computationally expensive; noisy in unbiased rendering; difficult to denoise due to lack of surface normals [S1][S2, unofficial].
*   **When to use:** For dense clouds, smoke, or materials like skin and milk where multiple bounces are significant [S1]. For skin and milk, subsurface scattering (random walk) may be more efficient [S1].
*   **When not to use:** For thin media like light haze, where single scattering is sufficient and faster [S2, unofficial].

## Background
The distinction between single and multiple scattering is fundamental in volume rendering. Single scattering simulates only one bounce, which looks fine for thin media but renders dense clouds dark and lifeless [S2, unofficial]. Multiple scattering accounts for photons bouncing dozens or hundreds of times, which is required for real-world effects like scattering in clouds [S1]. The concept is implemented in various renderers, such as Blender's Cycles, where the number of bounces is a configurable parameter (defaulting to zero for performance) [S1]. The industry standard for storing volume data, OpenVDB, was developed at DreamWorks Animation and open-sourced in 2012, facilitating the transport of complex volume data for such rendering [S2, unofficial].
