# Riley et al. Angular Distribution Renderer
Also called: Eye Ray Rendering with Angular Distribution, Final Gather with Angular Distribution, Riley et al

## Overview
The Riley et al. Angular Distribution Renderer is a single-pass volume rendering technique designed to approximate multiple scattering effects in participating media, specifically targeting the forward-scattering behavior of large atmospheric particles like water and ice [S2]. It solves the problem of premature darkening in low-albedo media by using pre-computed angular distribution functions to direct in-scattered light toward the eye, capturing chromatic effects such as rainbows and halos [S1][S2]. Unlike sibling methods that may rely on stochastic tracing or complex multi-bounce approximations, this approach leverages a half-angle slicing scheme and hardware-accelerated buffers to achieve efficiency in a single pass [S2].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** Volume density field, extinction coefficient $\sigma_t$, scattering coefficient $\sigma_s$, phase function parameters, light direction $\Omega_{lt}$, view direction $\Omega$, and a user-adjustable backscatter term $\xi$ [S1][S2].
*   **Outputs:** Eye buffer (accumulated radiance) and Light buffer (attenuation) [S2].

**Data Structures:**
1.  **Eye Buffer:** A 2D texture storing accumulated radiance $L$ for each pixel. The alpha channel stores the total transparency of the volume [S1][S2].
2.  **Light Buffer:** A 2D texture storing the attenuation of light as it traverses the volume from the light source perspective [S2].
3.  **Angular Distribution Function:** A pre-computed lookup table or function mapping the number of scattering events to the angular distribution of light [S2].

**Numbered Steps:**
1.  **Slicing:** Slice the volume data at an angle halfway between the light direction $\Omega_{lt}$ and the eye direction $\Omega$ (half-angle slicing) [S2]. This orientation is suitable for both perspectives, allowing a single pass to update both buffers [S2].
2.  **Light Buffer Pass:** Render the volume from the light perspective to the Light Buffer. This step stores the attenuation of light as it traverses the volume [S2].
3.  **Eye Buffer Pass:** Render the volume from the eye perspective to the Eye Buffer. For each sample along the eye ray, accumulate in-scattered intensity [S1].
4.  **Accumulation Formula:** For a pixel, the radiance $L(\vec{s}, \Omega)$ is computed by summing contributions from sampling slabs $i$ from the front to the back of the ray [S1]:
    $$ L(\vec{s}, \Omega) = \sum_{i=front}^{back} T(\vec{s}, \vec{s}_f + i\vec{m}\Omega \Delta) I(\vec{s}_f + i\vec{m}\Omega \Delta) \times \left( A_{ms\lambda}^{\lfloor n(\vec{s}_f + i\vec{m}\Omega \Delta) + 1 \rfloor} (\Omega - \Omega_{lt}) + \xi \right) \beta_{sca} \Delta $$
    Where:
    *   $\vec{s}$ is the current position on the eye ray.
    *   $\vec{s}_f$ is the beginning of the ray (front).
    *   $T(\vec{s}, \vec{s}')$ is the transmittance between $\vec{s}$ and $\vec{s}'$.
    *   $I(\vec{s}')$ is the intensity at sample $\vec{s}'$.
    *   $n(\vec{s})$ is the expected number of scattering events reaching sample $\vec{s}$ [S1].
    *   The term $n(\vec{s}_f + i\vec{m}\Omega \Delta) + 1$ indicates that the angular distribution function is applied for one more scattering event than expected, as this final event directs light into the eye [S1].
    *   $A_{ms\lambda}^k (\Omega - \Omega_{lt})$ is the multiple scattering angular distribution function for $k$ events, evaluated at the angle between the view and light directions [S1].
    *   $\xi$ is the user-adjustable term added to the phase function to model backscattered light from further along the path, preventing premature darkening [S1].
    *   $\beta_{sca}$ is the scattering coefficient [S1].
    *   $\Delta$ is the step size [S1].
5.  **Direct Sunlight:** Add the direct contribution of the sun in a distant light pass. The total transparency stored in the alpha channel of the Eye Buffer attenuates the sunlight, combined with atmospheric attenuation [S1].
6.  **Post-Processing:** Add skylight and aerial perspective effects based on a simplified single-scattering analytic model to increase realism [S2].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Premature Darkening:** Rendering without backscattered light causes the region where light first strikes the cloud to appear unnaturally dark. The term $\xi$ corrects this [S1].
*   **Hardware Limitations:** Linear interpolation is not available for floating-point textures in some graphics hardware, so intensity loss for the worst-case attenuation (highest number of scattering events) is implemented for efficiency [S1].
*   **Dense Regions:** The model is least accurate in dense inner regions of the cloud [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Single pass through the volume, making it efficient for real-time or near-real-time rendering on programmable graphics hardware [S2].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storage for Eye Buffer and Light Buffer, both of size proportional to the screen resolution [S2].
*   **Strengths:** Captures essential single-scattering effects and avoids premature darkening in low-albedo models. Efficiently renders fine angularly dependent chromatic effects like rainbows, halos, and glories [S1][S2].
*   **Limitations:** Least accurate in dense inner regions of the cloud. Relies on pre-computed angular distribution functions, which may limit flexibility for arbitrary phase functions [S1][S2].
*   **When to Use:** Suitable for atmospheric phenomena involving large water and ice particles where forward scattering is dominant and computational efficiency is critical [S2].
*   **When Not to Use:** Not suitable for dense, highly absorbing media where multiple scattering is complex and isotropic, or when high accuracy in dense inner regions is required [S1].

## Background
The method was proposed by Riley et al. [76] as part of a multiple model system for fast rendering of atmospheric effects [S2]. It was designed to handle the forward multi-scattering behavior of large water and ice atmospheric particles, generating fine angularly dependent chromatic effects [S2]. The approach builds on the half-angle slicing scheme introduced by Kniss et al. [45] [S2]. It is cited in surveys of participating media rendering techniques as an example of efficient, hardware-accelerated multiple scattering approximation [S2].
