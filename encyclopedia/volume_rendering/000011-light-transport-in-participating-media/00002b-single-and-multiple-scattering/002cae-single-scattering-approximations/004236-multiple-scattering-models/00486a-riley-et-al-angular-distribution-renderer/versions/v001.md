# Riley et al. Angular Distribution Renderer

Also called: Eye Ray Rendering with Angular Distribution, Final Gather with Angular Distribution, Riley et al

## Overview
The Riley et al. Angular Distribution Renderer is a single-pass volume rendering method designed to approximate multiple scattering effects in participating media, specifically targeting atmospheric phenomena like clouds and hazes [S1, S2]. It solves the computational cost of full path tracing by using pre-computed angular distribution functions to direct in-scattered light into the eye ray, effectively capturing forward multi-scattering behavior for large water and ice particles [S2]. Unlike stochastic methods that trace random walks, this approach is deterministic and optimized for programmable graphics hardware, avoiding the premature darkening artifacts common in low-albedo models [S1].

## How it works
The method renders the volume in a single pass using a half-angle slicing scheme, accumulating light into an eye buffer while simultaneously tracking attenuation in a light buffer [S2]. It approximates the radiative transfer equation by applying an angular distribution function that accounts for one additional scattering event than the expected count at each sample point, ensuring light is directed toward the eye [S1].

**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** Volume density field, extinction coefficient $\sigma_t$, scattering coefficient $\sigma_s$, absorption coefficient $\sigma_a$, phase function parameters, light direction $\Omega_{lt}$, view direction $\Omega$, user-adjustable backscatter term $\xi$, and the expected number of scattering events $n(\vec{s})$ at position $\vec{s}$ [S1, S2].
*   **Outputs:** Eye buffer (accumulated radiance $L$) and Light buffer (attenuation/transparency) [S2].

**Data Structures:**
*   **Eye Buffer:** Accumulates in-scattered radiance. Stores total transparency in the alpha channel to attenuate direct sunlight [S1, S2].
*   **Light Buffer:** Stores the attenuation of light as it traverses the volume.
*   **Angular Distribution Function:** A pre-computed lookup or function $A_{ms}^\lambda$ that maps the number of scattering events and phase angles to intensity [S1, S2].

**Numbered Steps:**
1.  **Slicing:** Slice the volume data at an angle halfway between the light direction $\Omega_{lt}$ and the view direction $\Omega$ (half-angle slicing) [S2].
2.  **Light Buffer Pass:** Render the volume to the light buffer to store attenuation. This step calculates the transparency of the volume along the light path [S2].
3.  **Eye Buffer Pass:** Render the volume to the eye buffer. For each sample point $\vec{s}$ along the eye ray, calculate the in-scattered intensity using the angular distribution function [S1].
4.  **Accumulation:** Accumulate the in-scattered intensity into the eye buffer using front-to-back compositing [S1].
5.  **Post-Processing:** Add skylight and aerial perspective effects based on a simplified single-scattering analytic model [S2]. Apply the stored total transparency from the eye buffer's alpha channel to attenuate direct sunlight [S1].

**Formulas:**
The accumulation for a pixel is given by Equation 26 in [S1]:

$$
L(\vec{s}, \Omega) = \sum_{i=front}^{back} T(\vec{s}, \vec{s}_f + i\vec{m}\Omega \Delta) I(\vec{s}_f + i\vec{m}\Omega \Delta) \times \left( A_{ms}^\lambda \lfloor n(\vec{s}_f + i\vec{m}\Omega \Delta) + 1 \rfloor (\Omega - \Omega_{lt}) + \xi \beta_{sca} \Delta \right)
$$

Where:
*   $L(\vec{s}, \Omega)$ is the accumulated radiance.
*   $T(\vec{s}, \vec{s}_f + i\vec{m}\Omega \Delta)$ is the transmittance from the start of the ray $\vec{s}_f$ to the current sample.
*   $I(\vec{s}_f + i\vec{m}\Omega \Delta)$ is the incident light intensity at the sample point.
*   $n(\vec{s})$ is the expected number of scattering events at position $\vec{s}$.
*   $A_{ms}^\lambda$ is the multiple scattering angular distribution function for wavelength $\lambda$.
*   $\Omega$ is the view direction, $\Omega_{lt}$ is the light direction.
*   $\xi$ is a user-adjustable term added to the phase function to model backscattered light from further along the path, preventing premature darkening [S1].
*   $\beta_{sca}$ is the scattering coefficient (equivalent to $\sigma_s$) [S1].
*   $\Delta$ is the step size.

**Implementation Note:**
The product term in the original derivation is not practical for graphics hardware due to floating-point texture interpolation limitations. Instead, intensity loss for the worst-case attenuation (highest number of scattering events) is implemented for efficiency [S1].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
def render_riley_angular_distribution(volume, light_dir, view_dir, xi, step_size):
    # Initialize buffers
    eye_buffer = zeros_like(view_directions)
    light_buffer = zeros_like(light_directions)
    
    # Half-angle slicing direction
    slice_dir = normalize(light_dir + view_dir)
    
    # Pass 1: Light Buffer (Attenuation)
    for each ray in light_buffer:
        transmittance = 1.0
        for sample in volume_along_ray:
            sigma_t = volume.extinction_coefficient(sample)
            transmittance *= exp(-sigma_t * step_size)
            # Store attenuation in light buffer
        light_buffer[ray] = transmittance

    # Pass 2: Eye Buffer (In-scattering Accumulation)
    for each eye_ray in eye_buffer:
        accumulated_radiance = 0.0
        current_transmittance = 1.0
        ray_origin = eye_ray.origin
        
        for i, sample in enumerate(volume_along_eye_ray):
            # Distance from start of ray
            dist = length(sample - ray_origin)
            
            # Get transmittance from light buffer for this sample
            # Note: In practice, this requires sampling the light buffer 
            # at the position of the current eye ray sample projected onto light rays
            light_transmittance = sample_light_buffer(sample, light_dir)
            
            # Expected number of scattering events at this sample
            n_s = compute_expected_scattering_events(sample)
            
            # Angular distribution for n+1 events
            # A_ms_lambda is the pre-computed angular distribution function
            phase_value = angular_distribution_function(
                n_s + 1, 
                angle_between(sample, light_dir, view_dir)
            )
            
            # Add backscatter term xi
            # beta_sca is scattering coefficient
            sigma_s = volume.scattering_coefficient(sample)
            in_scatter_intensity = phase_value + xi * sigma_s * step_size
            
            # Accumulate
            # T(s, s_f + i*m*Omega*Delta) is the transmittance from start to current sample
            # This is approximated by current_transmittance in front-to-back compositing
            accumulated_radiance += current_transmittance * in_scatter_intensity
            
            # Update transmittance for next step
            sigma_t = volume.extinction_coefficient(sample)
            current_transmittance *= exp(-sigma_t * step_size)
            
        eye_buffer[eye_ray] = accumulated_radiance

    # Post-processing: Add skylight and aerial perspective
    # Use alpha channel of eye buffer to attenuate direct sunlight
    final_image = apply_skylight(eye_buffer, light_transmittance)
    return final_image
```

**Parameters:**
*   $\xi$: User-adjustable term for backscatter. Typical values are not specified in the sources, but it is critical for preventing darkening at the cloud's illuminated edge [S1].
*   $n(\vec{s})$: Expected number of scattering events. This is a derived quantity based on optical depth and albedo [S1].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Premature Darkening:** Rendering without the backscatter term $\xi$ causes the region where light first strikes the cloud to appear unnaturally dark [S1].
*   **Hardware Limitations:** The original product formulation is inefficient on graphics hardware; the worst-case attenuation approximation is necessary for performance [S1].
*   **Dense Regions:** The model is least accurate in dense inner regions of the cloud [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Single pass through the volume for both light and eye buffers, making it $O(N)$ where $N$ is the number of samples [S1, S2].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storage for two buffers (eye and light) plus the volume data [S2].
*   **Performance:** Designed for efficient rendering on programmable graphics hardware [S2].
*   **Strengths:** Captures essential single-scattering effects and forward multi-scattering behavior for large particles (rainbows, halos, glories) without the cost of full path tracing [S1, S2]. Avoids premature darkening [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Least accurate in dense inner regions of clouds [S1]. Approximation of the scattering integral may lack physical accuracy compared to stochastic methods [S2].
*   **When to Use:** Real-time or interactive rendering of atmospheric phenomena where forward scattering dominates and high performance is required [S2].
*   **When Not to Use:** When physically accurate multiple scattering in dense, optically thick media is required, or when backscattering effects are critical and $\xi$ cannot be tuned appropriately [S1].

## Background
The method was proposed by Riley et al. [76] in the context of fast rendering of atmospheric effects [S2]. It is part of a multiple model system designed to generate fine angularly dependent chromatic effects such as rainbows, halos, and glories [S2]. The core of the system is an angular distribution renderer developed to handle the forward multi-scattering behavior of large water and ice atmospheric particles [S2]. It builds on the half-angle slicing scheme of Kniss et al. [45] [S2].
