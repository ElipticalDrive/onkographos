# Riley et al. Angular Distribution Renderer

Also called: Eye Ray Rendering with Angular Distribution, Final Gather with Angular Distribution, Riley et al

## Overview
The Riley et al. Angular Distribution Renderer is a single-pass volume rendering method designed to approximate multiple scattering effects in participating media, specifically targeting atmospheric phenomena like clouds where forward scattering dominates [S1, S2]. It solves the problem of premature darkening in low-albedo media by using pre-computed angular distribution functions to direct light into the eye ray after a specific number of scattering events [S1]. Unlike sibling methods that may rely on stochastic tracing or complex convolution, this approach uses a deterministic half-angle slicing scheme optimized for programmable graphics hardware, allowing for efficient accumulation of inscattered light in a single pass [S2].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** Volume data (extinction $\sigma_t$, scattering $\sigma_s$, phase function parameters), view direction $\Omega$, light direction $\Omega_{lt}$, user-adjustable backscatter term $\xi$, and the expected number of scattering events $n(\vec{s})$ at each sample point [S1, S2].
*   **Outputs:** Eye buffer (accumulated radiance $L$) and Light buffer (attenuation/transparency) [S2].

**Data Structures:**
*   **Eye Buffer:** Accumulates the final radiance for each pixel. Stores total transparency in the alpha channel to attenuate direct sunlight [S1].
*   **Light Buffer:** Stores the attenuation of light as it traverses the volume along the light direction [S2].
*   **Angular Distribution Functions:** Pre-computed tables or functions mapping the number of scattering events to the angular distribution of scattered light [S2].

**Steps:**
1.  **Slicing:** Slice the volume data at an angle halfway between the eye view direction $\Omega$ and the light direction $\Omega_{lt}$ [S2]. This "half angle" plane is suitable for both perspectives, allowing a single pass to serve both the light buffer and eye buffer calculations [S2].
2.  **Light Buffer Pass:** Render to the light buffer to store the attenuation of light as it traverses the volume. This captures the extinction along the light path [S2].
3.  **Eye Buffer Pass:** Render to the eye buffer to accumulate light directed into the eye rays. This involves a single slicing pass through the volume [S1].
4.  **Accumulation:** For each sample point $\vec{s}$ along the eye ray, calculate the inscattered intensity. The method applies the angular distribution function for $n(\vec{s}) + 1$ scattering events, where $n(\vec{s})$ is the expected number of scattering events reaching the sample [S1]. The $+1$ accounts for the final scattering event that directs light into the eye [S1].
5.  **Backscatter Correction:** Add the user-adjustable term $\xi$ to the phase function to model light received from multiple scattering further along the path. This prevents the premature darkening of the cloud region where light first strikes it [S1].
6.  **Direct Sunlight:** Add direct sunlight contribution in a distant light pass. The total transparency of the volume (stored in the eye buffer's alpha channel) attenuates the sunlight, along with atmospheric attenuation [S1].
7.  **Atmospheric Effects:** Add skylight and aerial perspective effects based on a simplified single-scattering analytic model to increase realism [S2].

**Formulas:**
The accumulation for a pixel, assuming front-to-back compositing with the ray beginning at $\vec{s}_f$, is given by [S1]:

$$ L(\vec{s}, \Omega) = \sum_{i=front}^{back} T(\vec{s}, \vec{s}_f + i\vec{m}\Delta) I(\vec{s}_f + i\vec{m}\Delta) \times \left( A_{ms}^{\lfloor n(\vec{s}_f + i\vec{m}\Delta) + 1 \rfloor} (\Omega - \Omega_{lt}) + \xi \right) \beta_{sca} \Delta $$

Where:
*   $T(\vec{s}, \vec{s}')$ is the transmittance between points $\vec{s}$ and $\vec{s}'$.
*   $I(\vec{s})$ is the incident radiance at $\vec{s}$.
*   $\vec{m}$ is the unit vector along the eye ray.
*   $\Delta$ is the step size.
*   $n(\vec{s})$ is the expected number of scattering events at $\vec{s}$.
*   $A_{ms}^{k}(\Omega - \Omega_{lt})$ is the multiple scattering angular distribution function for $k$ scattering events, evaluated at the angle between view and light directions.
*   $\xi$ is the user-adjustable backscatter term [S1].
*   $\beta_{sca}$ is the scattering coefficient (related to $\sigma_s$) [S1].

*Note: The product term in the original source is described as impractical for graphics hardware implementation due to floating-point texture interpolation limitations. The implementation approximates intensity loss for the worst-case attenuation (highest number of scattering events) for efficiency [S1].*

**Parameters:**
*   $\xi$: User-adjustable term added to the phase function. Typical values are not specified, but it is critical for preventing darkening in low-albedo scenarios [S1].
*   $n(\vec{s})$: Expected number of scattering events. This determines which angular distribution function is applied [S1].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Premature Darkening:** Rendering without the backscatter term $\xi$ causes the region where light first strikes the cloud to appear unnaturally dark, particularly in low-albedo models [S1].
*   **Hardware Limitations:** Linear interpolation for floating-point textures was not available at the time of publication, necessitating the worst-case attenuation approximation for the product term [S1].
*   **Accuracy:** The model is least accurate in dense inner regions of the cloud, as it relies on approximations of multiple scattering [S1].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
def render_angular_distribution(volume, view_dir, light_dir, xi, expected_scatter_func):
    """
    Renders the volume using the Riley et al. Angular Distribution method.
    
    Args:
        volume: Volume data containing extinction, scattering coefficients, etc.
        view_dir: Eye view direction vector.
        light_dir: Light direction vector.
        xi: User-adjustable backscatter term.
        expected_scatter_func: Function n(s) returning expected scattering events at position s.
        
    Returns:
        eye_buffer: Accumulated radiance.
        light_buffer: Attenuation buffer.
    """
    # 1. Determine slicing plane halfway between eye and light
    slice_normal = normalize(view_dir + light_dir)
    
    # 2. Initialize buffers
    eye_buffer = zeros_like(pixel_grid)
    light_buffer = zeros_like(pixel_grid)
    
    # 3. Render Light Buffer (Attenuation along light path)
    # This pass stores attenuation of light as it traverses the volume.
    # Implementation detail: Slice volume along slice_normal, project light rays.
    # For each pixel in light_buffer, integrate extinction along light_dir.
    light_buffer = compute_light_attenuation(volume, light_dir, slice_normal)
    
    # 4. Render Eye Buffer (Accumulate inscattered light)
    # Single slicing pass through the volume
    for each pixel in eye_buffer:
        ray_origin = camera_origin
        ray_direction = view_dir
        
        accumulated_radiance = 0.0
        current_transmittance = 1.0
        
        # Front-to-back compositing
        for step in range(num_steps):
            sample_pos = ray_origin + step * ray_direction * delta
            
            # Get volume properties at sample_pos
            sigma_t = volume.extinction(sample_pos)
            sigma_s = volume.scattering(sample_pos)
            beta_sca = sigma_s # Scattering coefficient
            
            # Expected number of scattering events at this sample
            n_s = expected_scatter_func(sample_pos)
            
            # Apply angular distribution for n(s) + 1 events
            # This accounts for the final scattering event directing light to eye
            num_events = n_s + 1
            angle = angle_between(view_dir, light_dir)
            
            # Get pre-computed angular distribution value
            # A_ms is the multiple scattering angular distribution function
            angular_dist = get_angular_distribution(num_events, angle)
            
            # Add backscatter term xi
            phase_contribution = angular_dist + xi
            
            # Compute incident light intensity at this sample
            # Approximate worst-case attenuation for efficiency
            # I(s) is attenuated by the volume and atmosphere
            incident_intensity = compute_incident_intensity(sample_pos, light_dir, light_buffer)
            
            # Transmittance from ray start to current sample
            transmittance = current_transmittance
            
            # Accumulate inscattered light
            # L(s, Omega) = sum( T(s, s_i) * I(s_i) * (A_ms + xi) * beta_sca * delta )
            contribution = transmittance * incident_intensity * phase_contribution * beta_sca * delta
            accumulated_radiance += contribution
            
            # Update transmittance for next step
            current_transmittance *= exp(-sigma_t * delta)
            
        eye_buffer[pixel] = accumulated_radiance
        
    # 5. Add Direct Sunlight
    # Attenuate sunlight by total transparency (alpha channel of eye buffer) and atmosphere
    direct_sun = compute_direct_sunlight(light_dir)
    for each pixel in eye_buffer:
        total_transparency = eye_buffer[pixel].alpha # Stored in alpha channel
        eye_buffer[pixel].rgb += direct_sun * total_transparency
        
    # 6. Add Skylight and Aerial Perspective
    # Based on simplified single-scattering analytic model
    skylight = compute_skylight(view_dir, light_dir)
    aerial_perspective = compute_aerial_perspective(view_dir, light_dir, volume)
    for each pixel in eye_buffer:
        eye_buffer[pixel].rgb += skylight + aerial_perspective
        
    return eye_buffer, light_buffer
```

## Subtopics

### Half-Angle Slicing
A technique where volume data is sliced at an angle halfway between the eye and light perspectives. This allows a single pass to compute both light attenuation and eye-directed inscattering, optimizing for graphics hardware [S2].

### Angular Distribution Functions
Pre-computed functions that approximate the distribution of light after a certain number of scattering events. These are used to handle forward multi-scattering behavior of large water and ice particles [S2].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Single pass through the volume for both light and eye buffers, making it efficient for real-time or near-real-time rendering on programmable graphics hardware [S2].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storage for eye buffer, light buffer, and pre-computed angular distribution functions [S2].
*   **Performance:** Designed for efficient rendering, leveraging hardware acceleration. Performance is limited by the complexity of the angular distribution lookups and the number of steps [S2].
*   **Strengths:** Captures essential single-scattering effects and avoids premature darkening in low-albedo media. Can generate fine angularly dependent chromatic effects such as rainbows, halos, and glories [S1, S2].
*   **Limitations:** Least accurate in dense inner regions of the cloud. Relies on approximations for multiple scattering. The product term approximation may introduce errors in complex attenuation scenarios [S1].
*   **When to use:** Suitable for rendering atmospheric phenomena like clouds and haze where forward scattering is dominant and computational efficiency is important.
*   **When not to use:** Not suitable for highly accurate scientific visualization of dense, highly scattering media where multiple scattering is complex and non-forward dominant.

## Background
The Riley et al. Angular Distribution Renderer was developed by Riley et al. and presented in the context of efficient rendering of atmospheric phenomena [S1, S2]. It is part of a multiple model system designed to handle the forward multi-scattering behavior of large water and ice atmospheric particles [S2]. The method builds on the half-angle slicing scheme proposed by Kniss et al. [45] and incorporates insights from single-scattering analytic models for atmospheric effects [S2]. It addresses the limitations of low-albedo models which suffer from premature darkening at the cloud's illuminated edge [S1].
