# Riley et al. Angular Distribution Renderer

Also called: Eye Ray Rendering with Angular Distribution, Final Gather with Angular Distribution, Riley et al

## Overview
The Riley et al. Angular Distribution Renderer is a single-pass volume rendering method designed to approximate multiple scattering effects in participating media, specifically targeting the forward-scattering behavior of large atmospheric particles like water and ice [S2]. It solves the computational cost of integrating the in-scattering term over the sphere of directions by using pre-computed angular distribution functions to direct light into the eye ray, avoiding the premature darkening artifacts common in low-albedo models [S1]. Unlike [3D-Filter / Concrete Directions](12253-3d-filter-concrete-directions.md) which discretizes directions, this method uses a continuous angular distribution approximation within a half-angle slicing framework [S2].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** Volume data (density/scattering coefficients), view direction $\Omega$, light direction $\Omega_{lt}$, number of expected scattering events $n(\vec{s})$, and a user-adjustable backscatter term $\xi$.
*   **Outputs:** Eye buffer (accumulated radiance) and Light buffer (attenuation).

**Data Structures:**
*   **Eye Buffer:** Accumulates in-scattered radiance directed into the eye ray.
*   **Light Buffer:** Stores the attenuation of light as it traverses the volume.
*   **Angular Distribution Function:** A pre-computed function approximating the distribution of light after a certain number of scattering events.

**Steps:**
1.  **Slicing:** Slice the volume data at an angle halfway between the eye and light perspectives (half-angle slicing) [S2]. This orientation is suitable for both viewing and lighting calculations.
2.  **Light Buffer Pass:** Render to the light buffer to store the attenuation of light as it traverses the volume along the light direction [S2].
3.  **Eye Buffer Pass:** Perform a single slicing pass through the volume to collect in-scattered intensity into the eye ray [S1].
4.  **Accumulation:** For each sample along the eye ray, calculate the contribution from scattering events. The method applies the angular distribution function for $n(\vec{s}) + 1$ scattering events, where $n(\vec{s})$ is the expected number of scattering events reaching the sample [S1]. This extra event accounts for the scattering that directs light into the eye ray.
5.  **Backscatter Correction:** Add a user-adjustable term $\xi$ to the phase function to model light received from multiple scattering further along the path, preventing premature darkening where light first strikes the cloud [S1].
6.  **Final Compositing:** Add skylight and aerial perspective effects based on a simplified single-scattering analytic model [S2]. Store total transparency in the alpha channel of the eye buffer to attenuate direct sunlight [S1].

**Formulas:**
The accumulation for a pixel, assuming front-to-back compositing starting from $\vec{s}_f$, is given by [S1]:

$$ L(\vec{s}, \Omega) = \sum_{i=front}^{back} T(\vec{s}, \vec{s}_f + i\vec{m}\Delta) I(\vec{s}_f + i\vec{m}\Delta) \times \left( A_{ms}^{\lfloor n(\vec{s}_f + i\vec{m}\Delta) + 1 \rfloor} (\Omega - \Omega_{lt}) + \xi \right) \beta_{sca} \Delta $$

Where:
*   $L(\vec{s}, \Omega)$ is the accumulated radiance.
*   $T(\vec{s}, \vec{s}')$ is the transmittance between points $\vec{s}$ and $\vec{s}'$.
*   $I(\vec{s}_f + i\vec{m}\Delta)$ is the incident light intensity at the sample point.
*   $n(\vec{s})$ is the expected number of scattering events.
*   $A_{ms}^{k} (\Omega - \Omega_{lt})$ is the multiple scattering angular distribution function for $k$ scattering events, evaluated at the angle between the eye direction $\Omega$ and light direction $\Omega_{lt}$.
*   $\xi$ is the user-adjustable backscatter term.
*   $\beta_{sca}$ is the scattering coefficient.
*   $\Delta$ is the step size.

**Note on Implementation:** The product form in Equation 24 of the source is noted as impractical for graphics hardware due to lack of floating-point texture interpolation support. Instead, intensity loss for the worst-case attenuation (highest number of scattering events) is implemented for efficiency [S1].

**Parameters:**
*   $\xi$: User-adjustable term added to the phase function. Typical values are not specified, but it must be non-negative to prevent darkening [S1].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Premature Darkening:** Rendering without backscattered light ($\xi = 0$) causes the region where light first strikes the cloud to appear unnaturally dark [S1].
*   **Dense Regions:** The model is least accurate in dense inner regions of the cloud [S1].
*   **Hardware Limitations:** The method relies on efficient implementation on programmable graphics hardware, specifically avoiding complex products that require interpolation of floating-point textures [S1].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
def render_angular_distribution(volume, view_dir, light_dir, expected_scattering_count, xi, step_size):
    """
    Renders the volume using the Riley et al. Angular Distribution method.
    
    Args:
        volume: Volume data structure with density and scattering coefficients.
        view_dir: Eye direction vector.
        light_dir: Light direction vector.
        expected_scattering_count: Function n(s) returning expected scattering events at position s.
        xi: Backscatter correction term.
        step_size: Delta for integration steps.
        
    Returns:
        eye_buffer: Accumulated radiance.
        light_buffer: Attenuation buffer.
    """
    # Initialize buffers
    eye_buffer = zeros_like(volume.resolution)
    light_buffer = zeros_like(volume.resolution)
    
    # Determine slicing plane halfway between eye and light perspectives
    slice_normal = normalize(view_dir + light_dir)
    
    # Pass 1: Light Buffer (Attenuation)
    # Render light buffer storing attenuation of light as it traverses the volume
    # This is a standard transmittance calculation along light rays
    for each ray in light_direction:
        transmittance = 1.0
        for sample in volume_along_ray:
            sigma_t = volume.get_extinction_coefficient(sample.position)
            transmittance *= exp(-sigma_t * step_size)
            light_buffer[ray_index] = transmittance
            
    # Pass 2: Eye Buffer (In-scattering)
    # Single slicing pass through the volume
    for each ray in view_direction:
        accumulated_radiance = 0.0
        current_transmittance = 1.0
        
        # Front-to-back compositing
        for i, sample in enumerate(volume_along_ray):
            position = sample.position
            sigma_s = volume.get_scattering_coefficient(position)
            
            # Expected number of scattering events at this position
            n_s = expected_scattering_count(position)
            
            # Angular distribution for n(s) + 1 scattering events
            # This accounts for the scattering event directing light into the eye
            angle = angle_between(view_dir, light_dir)
            angular_dist = get_multiple_scattering_angular_distribution(n_s + 1, angle)
            
            # Incident light intensity (from light buffer or direct sun)
            # Note: Source mentions storing total transparency in alpha channel to attenuate sunlight
            incident_intensity = get_incident_light(position, light_dir, light_buffer)
            
            # Contribution from this slab
            # Formula: T * I * (A_ms + xi) * beta_sca * Delta
            contribution = current_transmittance * incident_intensity * (angular_dist + xi) * sigma_s * step_size
            
            accumulated_radiance += contribution
            
            # Update transmittance for next step
            sigma_t = volume.get_extinction_coefficient(position)
            current_transmittance *= exp(-sigma_t * step_size)
            
        eye_buffer[ray_index] = accumulated_radiance
        
    # Add skylight and aerial perspective effects
    eye_buffer += apply_sky_and_aerial_perspective(eye_buffer, view_dir, light_dir)
    
    return eye_buffer, light_buffer
```

## Subtopics

### Half-Angle Slicing
A technique where volume data is sliced at an angle halfway between the eye and light perspectives, suitable for both viewing and lighting calculations [S2].

### Multiple Scattering Angular Distribution
Pre-computed functions approximating the distribution of light after a certain number of scattering events, used to handle forward multi-scattering behavior [S2].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Single pass through the volume for both light and eye buffers, making it efficient for real-time rendering on programmable graphics hardware [S2].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storage for eye buffer, light buffer, and pre-computed angular distribution functions.
*   **Strengths:** Captures essential single-scattering effects and avoids premature darkening at cloud edges, unlike low-albedo models [S1]. Efficiently handles forward multi-scattering behavior of large water and ice particles [S2].
*   **Limitations:** Least accurate in dense inner regions of the cloud [S1]. Relies on pre-computed angular distributions, which may limit flexibility for arbitrary phase functions.
*   **When to use:** Real-time rendering of atmospheric phenomena (rainbows, halos, glories) where forward scattering is dominant and performance is critical [S2].
*   **When not to use:** Scenes requiring high accuracy in dense, highly scattering regions or where backward scattering is significant.

## Background
The method was proposed by Riley et al. [76] as part of a multiple model system for fast rendering of atmospheric effects [S2]. It was designed to generate fine angularly dependent chromatic effects such as rainbows, halos, and glories by handling the forward multi-scattering behavior of large water and ice atmospheric particles [S2]. The core of the system is an angular distribution renderer developed to approximate the distribution of light after a certain number of scattering events [S2]. It builds on the half-angle slicing scheme of Kniss et al. [45] and is designed for efficient rendering on programmable graphics hardware [S2].
