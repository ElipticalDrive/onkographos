# 3D-Filter / Concrete Directions

Also called: 3D-Filter / Nishita, Nishita et al. 3D-Filter, 3D-Filter / N Directions, Concrete Directions Method

## Overview
The 3D-Filter method approximates multiple scattering in participating media by representing the directional distribution of radiance using extended form factors stored in a 3D grid [S1][S2, book]. It solves the problem of computationally expensive indirect light transport in heterogeneous media (such as clouds or snow) by precomputing contribution ratios from neighboring voxels to a center voxel, thereby avoiding the calculation of form factors between all voxel pairs [S1][S2, book]. It is distinguished from sibling methods like Heterogeneous In-scattering with Multiple Scattering by its specific use of a "contribution-ratio pattern" (the 3D-filter) derived from a smaller space with mean density, and from Most Probable Paths by its deterministic grid-based accumulation rather than stochastic path tracing [S1][S2, book].

## How it works
**Inputs:**
*   A 3D volume representing the participating medium (e.g., cloud or snow), subdivided into voxels [S1][S2, book].
*   The viewing direction, used as the principal axis for voxel subdivision [S1][S2, book].
*   The sunlight direction [S1][S2, book].
*   Sky light and surface reflection data (for cloud rendering) [S2, book].
*   Medium properties: extinction coefficient $\sigma_t$, scattering coefficient $\sigma_s$, and phase function $p$ [S1][S2, book].

**Outputs:**
*   The total radiance $L(x)$ at each point $x$ in the medium, specifically in the viewing direction [S1][S2, book].

**Data Structures:**
*   **Voxel Grid:** The space containing the medium is divided into voxels. The grid is oriented such that the viewing direction is the principal axis [S1][S2, book].
*   **3D-Filter (Contribution-Ratio Pattern):** A data structure storing the contribution ratios to the radiance of a center voxel from other voxels [S1][S2, book]. This filter is computed in a smaller space defined by the mean density of the cloud/medium [S1][S2, book].
*   **Extended Form Factors:** Values representing the energy transfer between voxels, which are multiplied by the energy at related points [S1].

**Steps:**
1.  **Subdivision:** Subdivide the space including the medium into voxels, aligning the viewing direction as the principal axis [S1][S2, book].
2.  **Single Scattering Computation:** Compute the single scattering contribution separately [S1][S2, book]. This is distinct from the multiple scattering approximation [S2, book].
3.  **Filter Construction (Precomputation):**
    *   Define a smaller space using the mean density of the medium [S1][S2, book].
    *   Compute the contribution ratios to the radiance of a center voxel (in the viewing direction) from other voxels, taking the sunlight direction into account [S1][S2, book].
    *   This set of ratios forms the "contribution-ratio pattern" or 3D-filter [S1][S2, book].
    *   Optimization: Since scattering in media like clouds is mainly forward, concentrate computational effort on voxels that effectively contribute to the center voxel for paths with one or two scatterings [S1][S2, book].
    *   Selection: Use a stochastic method to select the voxels that will effectively contribute to the center voxel [S1][S2, book].
4.  **Multiple Scattering Accumulation:**
    *   Apply the 3D-filter to the voxels in the whole space [S1][S2, book].
    *   For each voxel, store the light scattered due to the second and third order scattering in the viewing direction [S1][S2, book].
    *   Multiply the form factors (stored in the filter) by the energy at the related points [S1].
    *   Accumulate these results to obtain the multiple scattering component of $L(x)$ [S1].
5.  **Final Radiance:** Combine the single scattering (computed in Step 2) and the accumulated multiple scattering (computed in Step 4) to obtain the final radiance $L(x)$ [S1][S2, book].

**Parameters:**
*   **Scattering Orders:** Only the first three orders of scattering are considered for multiple scattering to save computation time [S1][S2, book]. Single scattering is computed separately [S1][S2, book].
*   **Directional Sampling:** In some implementations (e.g., for clouds), radiance is estimated by considering eight precise directions around any particular sampling point of the viewing ray: the sunlight direction, the perpendicular direction to the sunlight, the horizontal direction along the Earth surface, and the zenith direction [S2, book].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
def compute_radiance_3d_filter(volume, viewing_direction, sunlight_direction, sky_light):
    """
    Computes radiance using the 3D-Filter method.
    volume: Grid of voxels with density and optical properties.
    viewing_direction: Vector, principal axis for voxel grid.
    sunlight_direction: Vector.
    sky_light: Radiance from sky.
    """
    
    # Step 1: Subdivide space into voxels aligned with viewing direction
    voxel_grid = subdivide_space(volume, principal_axis=viewing_direction)
    
    # Step 2: Compute single scattering separately
    L_single = compute_single_scattering(voxel_grid, sunlight_direction, sky_light)
    
    # Step 3: Construct the 3D-Filter (Contribution-Ratio Pattern)
    # Use a smaller space with mean density
    mean_density = compute_mean_density(volume)
    small_space = create_small_space(mean_density)
    
    # Compute contribution ratios for center voxel considering sunlight
    # Use stochastic selection for forward-scattering efficiency
    filter_3d = compute_contribution_ratios(
        small_space, 
        center_voxel=voxel_grid.center, 
        sunlight_direction=sunlight_direction,
        stochastic_selection=True
    )
    
    # Step 4: Apply filter to whole space for 2nd and 3rd order scattering
    L_multiple = zeros_like(voxel_grid)
    
    for voxel in voxel_grid:
        # Multiply form factors by energy at related points
        # Accumulate contributions from the filter
        # Store light scattered due to 2nd and 3rd order scattering
        contribution = apply_filter(
            filter_3d, 
            voxel, 
            energy_at_related_points(voxel_grid),
            orders=[2, 3]
        )
        L_multiple[voxel] += contribution
        
    # Step 5: Combine single and multiple scattering
    L_total = L_single + L_multiple
    
    return L_total
```

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Forward Scattering Assumption:** The method relies on the fact that scattering is mainly forward (e.g., in clouds). If the phase function is highly back-scattering, the stochastic selection of contributing voxels may fail to capture significant energy [S1][S2, book].
*   **Mean Density Approximation:** Using a smaller space with mean density may introduce errors in highly heterogeneous media where local density variations are significant [S1][S2, book].
*   **Truncation Error:** Limiting to three scattering orders may underestimate radiance in highly scattering media (high albedo) where higher-order scattering is significant [S1][S2, book].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Reduced compared to full form factor computation between all voxel pairs due to the use of the 3D-filter and stochastic voxel selection [S1][S2, book]. The cost is dominated by the precomputation of the filter and the application of the filter to the grid [S1][S2, book].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storage for the voxel grid and the 3D-filter [S1][S2, book]. The filter size depends on the number of contributing voxels selected stochastically [S1][S2, book].
*   **Strengths:** Efficiently handles multiple scattering in media like clouds and snow by avoiding expensive pairwise form factor calculations [S1][S2, book]. The use of forward scattering assumption allows for faster computation [S1][S2, book].
*   **Limitations:** Limited to a small number of scattering orders (typically 3) [S1][S2, book]. Accuracy depends on the validity of the mean density approximation and the forward scattering assumption [S1][S2, book].
*   **When to Use:** Suitable for rendering clouds and snow where multiple scattering is significant and forward scattering is dominant [S1][S2, book].
*   **When Not to Use:** Not suitable for media with strong back-scattering or where high-order multiple scattering is critical and cannot be truncated [S1][S2, book].

## Background
The 3D-Filter method was proposed by Nishita et al. [S1][S2, book]. It was originally developed to display clouds taking into account multiple scattering and sky light [S2, book]. The method has also been applied to render snow, which has a high albedo and appears white due to multiple scattering [S1]. In the snow application, the shape and density distribution are modeled using metaballs [S1]. The method is part of a broader class of techniques that use implicit representations of directional radiance, such as scattering patterns, diffusion equations, or point spread functions [S1].
