# 3D-Filter / Concrete Directions

Also called: 3D-Filter / Nishita, Nishita et al. 3D-Filter, 3D-Filter / N Directions, Concrete Directions Method

## Overview
The 3D-Filter method is an approximate technique for rendering participating media, specifically clouds and snow, that accounts for multiple scattering by precomputing directional energy transfer patterns [S1][S2, book]. It solves the problem of expensive global illumination in participating media by approximating the in-scattering integral for second- and third-order sunlight scattering using a grid-based "contribution-ratio pattern" (the 3D-filter) [S1][S2, book]. Unlike explicit path tracing or diffusion approximations, it separates single scattering (computed directly) from higher-order multiple scattering (computed via the filter), leveraging the forward-scattering nature of clouds to optimize the calculation of extended form factors [S1][S2, book].

## How it works
The method computes the radiance $L(x)$ reaching the eye from a point $x$ in the medium. The total radiance is the sum of single scattering (computed separately) and the accumulated contribution from second- and third-order multiple scattering of sunlight [S1][S2, book]. Sky light is treated as single scattering [S2, book].

### Inputs and Outputs
*   **Inputs:**
    *   Cloud geometry and density distribution (modeled as a voxel grid or metaballs) [S1][S2, book].
    *   Sun direction $\omega_s$ [S2, book].
    *   Viewing direction $\omega_v$ [S2, book].
    *   Optical properties: scattering coefficient $\sigma_s$, extinction coefficient $\sigma_t$, single scattering albedo $\omega_0 = \sigma_s / \sigma_t$ [S2, book].
*   **Outputs:**
    *   Radiance $L(x)$ for each voxel/point in the viewing direction [S1][S2, book].

### Data Structures
1.  **Voxel Grid:** The space containing the cloud is subdivided into voxels. The viewing direction $\omega_v$ is aligned with a principal axis of this grid [S2, book].
2.  **3D-Filter (Contribution-Ratio Pattern):** A precomputed data structure representing the ratio of energy transferred from a source voxel to a center voxel [S2, book].
    *   **Layout:** The filter is computed in a "smaller space" defined by the mean density of the cloud [S2, book]. It stores contribution ratios for paths with one or two scatterings (resulting in second- and third-order effects at the center) [S2, book].
    *   **Content:** For each voxel in the grid, the filter stores the light scattered due to second and third-order scattering in the viewing direction [S2, book].

### Steps

1.  **Grid Subdivision:** Subdivide the space including the cloud into a voxel grid, aligning the viewing direction as a principal axis [S2, book].
2.  **Single Scattering Computation:** Compute the single scattering contribution separately [S1][S2, book]. This includes sky light single scattering [S2, book].
3.  **3D-Filter Precomputation:**
    *   Define a smaller reference space using the mean density of the cloud [S2, book].
    *   Compute the contribution ratios (extended form factors) from other voxels to a center voxel in the viewing direction, taking the sunlight direction $\omega_s$ into account [S2, book].
    *   **Optimization:** Since cloud scattering is mainly forward, energy scattered at a point lies within a small solid angle. Use a stochastic method to select only those voxels that effectively contribute to the center voxel for paths with one or two scatterings [S2, book]. This concentrates effort on relevant voxels, speeding up the computation of extended form factors [S2, book].
    *   Store these contribution ratios in the 3D-filter [S2, book].
4.  **Filter Application:** Apply the 3D-filter to the voxels in the whole space [S2, book].
    *   For each voxel, multiply the precomputed form factors (from the filter) by the energy at the related source voxels [S1].
    *   Accumulate these products to obtain the multiple scattering component of $L(x)$ [S1].
5.  **Final Radiance:** Sum the single scattering result and the accumulated multiple scattering result to get the final radiance $L(x)$ [S1][S2, book].

### Parameters
*   **Multiple Scattering Orders:** Only the first three orders of scattering are considered (single scattering computed separately, plus second and third order multiple scattering) [S1][S2, book].
*   **Scattering Directionality:** The method relies on the assumption that scattering in clouds is mainly forward [S2, book].

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Non-Forward Scattering:** The optimization of concentrating effort on voxels contributing to the center voxel relies on forward scattering. For media with strong backscattering, this approximation may lose accuracy [S2, book].
*   **Density Variation:** The use of a "smaller space with mean density" for filter computation assumes homogeneity. Highly heterogeneous media may require more complex filter generation or adaptive voxelization [S2, book].
*   **Sky Light:** Sky light is treated as single scattering. If sky light undergoes significant multiple scattering within the cloud, this approximation may be insufficient [S2, book].

## Subtopics

### Diffusion Approximation
Uses a diffusion equation to solve multiple scattering between blobs, characterized by source radiance functions [S2, book].

### Stochastic Path Tracing
Solves the transport equation by random sampling of paths along interaction points [S1].

### Scattering Patterns
Represents directional distribution of radiance using precomputed scattering patterns [S2, book].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** The method is faster than explicit multiple scattering path tracing because it precomputes the 3D-filter and uses stochastic selection to limit the number of voxel interactions considered for second and third-order scattering [S2, book].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storage for the voxel grid and the 3D-filter data structure [S2, book].
*   **Strengths:** Efficiently handles multiple scattering in clouds and snow (high albedo media) by separating single and multiple scattering components and leveraging forward scattering properties [S1][S2, book].
*   **Limitations:** Approximate; limited to second and third-order scattering; assumes forward scattering; less accurate for highly heterogeneous or non-forward scattering media [S1][S2, book].
*   **When to Use:** Rendering clouds and snow where multiple scattering is visually significant but full path tracing is too expensive [S1][S2, book].
*   **When Not to Use:** Media with strong backscattering or where higher-order multiple scattering is critical [S2, book].

## Background
The 3D-Filter method was proposed by Nishita et al. for displaying clouds taking into account multiple scattering and sky light [S2, book]. It was also applied to render snow, which has a high albedo and looks white due to multiple scattering, with shape and density modeled using metaballs [S1]. The method is part of a broader family of techniques that use implicit representations of directional distribution, such as scattering patterns and diffusion equations [S1][S2, book]. Harris and Lastra also considered second-order scattering for a reduced number of incoming directions in related work [S1].
