# 3D-Filter / Concrete Directions

Also called: 3D-Filter / Nishita, Nishita et al. 3D-Filter, 3D-Filter / N Directions, Concrete Directions Method

## Overview
The 3D-Filter method approximates multiple scattering in participating media by precomputing a grid of "extended form factors" that represent the directional distribution of radiance [S1][S2, book]. It solves the problem of computationally expensive recursive ray casting for indirect illumination by storing contribution ratios from neighboring voxels to a center voxel, allowing the radiance to be computed via simple multiplication and accumulation [S1][S2, book]. It sets itself apart from sibling methods like Most Probable Paths by using a fixed voxel grid aligned with the viewing direction rather than stochastic path tracing, and from Angular Distribution Renderers by explicitly separating the computation of second and third-order scattering contributions into a precomputed filter pattern [S1][S2, book].

## How it works
The method models the radiance $L(x)$ at a point $x$ within a medium as the sum of single scattering (computed separately) and multiple scattering contributions from sunlight, limited to the first three orders of scattering [S2, book]. The core innovation is the "3D-filter," a set of extended form factors computed in a local coordinate system aligned with the viewing direction [S1][S2, book].

### Inputs and Outputs
*   **Inputs:**
    *   Medium density field $\rho(x)$, typically modeled as a mean density for the filter computation and a detailed field for the final application [S2, book].
    *   Sunlight direction $\omega_s$ [S2, book].
    *   Viewing direction $\omega_v$ [S2, book].
    *   Single scattering radiance $L_{SS}(x)$ [S2, book].
*   **Outputs:**
    *   Total radiance $L(x)$ for each voxel [S1][S2, book].

### Data Structures
1.  **Voxel Grid:** The space containing the medium (e.g., cloud or snow) is subdivided into voxels [S2, book]. The grid is oriented such that the viewing direction $\omega_v$ serves as the principal axis [S2, book].
2.  **3D-Filter (Extended Form Factors):** A data structure storing contribution ratios. For a center voxel, this filter contains values representing the light scattered from other voxels into the center voxel's viewing direction [S2, book]. The filter accounts for paths with one or two additional scatterings (corresponding to second and third order total scattering) [S1][S2, book].

### Steps

1.  **Space Subdivision:** Subdivide the volume containing the participating medium into a voxel grid, aligning the principal axis with the viewing direction $\omega_v$ [S2, book].
2.  **Mean Density Space Setup:** Define a smaller, representative space using the mean density of the cloud/medium [S2, book]. This simplifies the computation of the filter by assuming uniform density for the form factor calculation [S2, book].
3.  **Compute 3D-Filter (Extended Form Factors):**
    *   Calculate the contribution ratios from surrounding voxels to the center voxel in the viewing direction [S2, book].
    *   Account for the sunlight direction $\omega_s$ [S2, book].
    *   Leverage the fact that scattering in media like clouds is primarily forward-directed [S2, book]. This allows the algorithm to concentrate computational effort only on voxels that effectively contribute to the center voxel for paths involving one or two scatterings [S2, book].
    *   Use a stochastic method to select the specific voxels that contribute to these paths, optimizing the calculation of extended form factors [S2, book].
    *   Store these contribution ratios in the 3D-filter structure [S2, book].
4.  **Apply Filter to Grid:** Apply the precomputed 3D-filter to the voxels in the entire space [S2, book]. For each voxel, store the light scattered due to second and third-order scattering in the viewing direction [S2, book]. This is done by multiplying the form factors in the filter by the energy (radiance) at the related source voxels and accumulating the results [S1].
5.  **Compute Single Scattering:** Calculate the single scattering component $L_{SS}(x)$ separately [S2, book].
6.  **Accumulate Radiance:** Combine the multiple scattering results (from steps 3-4) and the single scattering result to obtain the final radiance $L(x)$ [S1][S2, book].

### Pseudocode

```python
def compute_3d_filter(mean_density, sun_direction, view_direction, voxel_size):
    """
    Precomputes the 3D-filter (extended form factors) for a center voxel.
    Assumes forward scattering and uses stochastic selection for efficiency.
    """
    # Define a local grid around the center voxel based on mean density
    # The grid is aligned with view_direction
    local_grid = create_local_grid(mean_density, view_direction, voxel_size)
    
    filter_values = []
    
    # Stochastic selection of contributing voxels for 1st and 2nd order scattering paths
    # This exploits the forward-scattering property of clouds
    contributing_voxels = stochastic_select_contributors(local_grid, sun_direction, view_direction)
    
    for voxel in contributing_voxels:
        # Compute extended form factor: contribution ratio from 'voxel' to center
        # considering sunlight direction and scattering order
        form_factor = compute_extended_form_factor(
            center=local_grid.center,
            source=voxel,
            sun_direction=sun_direction,
            view_direction=view_direction,
            scattering_order=voxel.order # 1 or 2
        )
        filter_values.append(form_factor)
        
    return filter_values

def apply_3d_filter(grid, filter_values, single_scattering_radiance):
    """
    Applies the precomputed 3D-filter to the entire voxel grid to compute
    multiple scattering radiance, then adds single scattering.
    """
    total_radiance = {}
    
    for voxel in grid:
        # Retrieve precomputed filter values for this voxel's position
        # Note: In practice, the filter might be shifted or interpolated
        current_filter = filter_values
        
        multiple_scattering = 0.0
        
        # Multiply form factors by energy at related points and accumulate
        for i, form_factor in enumerate(current_filter):
            # Identify the source voxel corresponding to this form factor
            source_voxel = get_source_voxel(voxel, i)
            
            # Get energy (radiance) at the source voxel
            # For multiple scattering, this is the radiance at the source
            # which includes its own scattering contributions
            source_energy = get_radiance_at_voxel(source_voxel, grid)
            
            multiple_scattering += form_factor * source_energy
            
        # Add single scattering computed separately
        total_radiance[voxel] = multiple_scattering + single_scattering_radiance[voxel]
        
    return total_radiance
```

### Parameters
*   **Scattering Orders:** Only the first three orders of scattering are considered for sunlight multiple scattering to save computation time [S2, book]. Single scattering is computed separately [S2, book].
*   **Density:** A mean density is used for the filter computation space [S2, book].

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Forward Scattering Assumption:** The method relies on scattering being mainly forward (e.g., clouds) to optimize voxel selection [S2, book]. This may be less accurate for media with strong backward scattering.
*   **Mean Density Approximation:** Using mean density for the filter computation simplifies the process but may lose high-frequency density variations in the final application [S2, book].
*   **Stochastic Selection:** The use of stochastic methods to select contributing voxels introduces potential variance, though it is designed to concentrate effort on effective contributors [S2, book].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** The method reduces the complexity of computing multiple scattering by precomputing form factors in a smaller space with mean density and using stochastic selection to limit the number of interactions considered [S2, book]. This is faster than computing form factors between every pair of voxels [S2, book].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storage for the voxel grid and the 3D-filter data structure [S1][S2, book].
*   **Strengths:** Efficiently handles multiple scattering in media like clouds and snow by leveraging forward scattering and precomputed filters [S1][S2, book].
*   **Limitations:** Limited to the first three orders of scattering [S2, book]. Relies on the assumption of forward scattering [S2, book]. The use of mean density may reduce accuracy for heterogeneous media [S2, book].
*   **When to Use:** Suitable for rendering clouds and snow where multiple scattering is significant and forward scattering is dominant [S1][S2, book].
*   **When Not to Use:** Not suitable for media with strong backward scattering or where higher-order scattering effects are critical beyond the third order [S2, book].

## Background
The 3D-Filter method was proposed by Nishita et al. [S1][S2, book]. It was originally developed to display clouds taking into account multiple scattering and sky light [S2, book]. The method computes radiance from sunlight multiple scattered plus sky light single scattered by the particles of the cloud [S2, book]. It has also been applied to render snow, which has a high albedo and looks white due to multiple scattering, with shape and density distribution modeled using metaballs [S1]. The method is part of a broader family of techniques that use implicit representations of directional distribution, such as scattering patterns or diffusion equations [S1].
