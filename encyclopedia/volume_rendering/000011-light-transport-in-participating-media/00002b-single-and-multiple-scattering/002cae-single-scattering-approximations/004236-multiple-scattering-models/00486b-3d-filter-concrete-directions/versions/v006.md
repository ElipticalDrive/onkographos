# 3D-Filter / Concrete Directions
Also called: 3D-Filter / Nishita, Nishita et al. 3D-Filter, 3D-Filter / N Directions, Concrete Directions Method

## Overview
The 3D-Filter method, introduced by Nishita et al., approximates multiple scattering in participating media (specifically clouds and snow) by precomputing a "contribution-ratio pattern" (the 3D-filter) that encodes the directional distribution of radiance in a voxel grid [S1][S2, book]. It solves the problem of efficiently rendering the cumulative effect of multiple bounces by restricting the calculation to the first three orders of scattering for sunlight, while treating sky light as single scattering [S1][S2, book]. It differs from sibling methods like [Most Probable Paths (MPP) Lighting Model](12244-most-probable-paths-mpp-lighting-model.md) or [Riley et al. Angular Distribution Renderer](12249-riley-et-al-angular-distribution-renderer.md) by using a deterministic, grid-based implicit representation of directional distribution via extended form factors, rather than stochastic path tracing or explicit angular basis functions [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs:**
*   A voxelized representation of the participating medium (cloud or snow), subdivided into voxels with the viewing direction as the principal axis [S1][S2, book].
*   Sunlight direction and intensity [S1][S2, book].
*   Sky light distribution (single scattering component) [S1][S2, book].
*   Optical properties: extinction coefficient $\sigma_t$, scattering coefficient $\sigma_s$, and single scattering albedo $\omega_0 = \sigma_s / \sigma_t$ [S1].
*   Mean density of the cloud, used to define a smaller reference space for filter computation [S1][S2, book].

**Outputs:**
*   Radiance $L(x)$ for each voxel $x$ in the viewing direction, composed of single-scattered sky light and multiple-scattered sunlight (orders 1–3) [S1][S2, book].

**Data Structures:**
*   **Voxel Grid:** A 3D grid where each voxel stores density and precomputed filter values.
*   **3D-Filter (Contribution-Ratio Pattern):** A set of extended form factors associated with each voxel. These factors represent the ratio of energy arriving at the center voxel from neighboring voxels due to 1st, 2nd, and 3rd order scattering events [S1][S2, book].
*   **Stochastic Sample Set:** A subset of voxels selected to compute the extended form factors efficiently, based on the forward-scattering property [S1][S2, book].

**Steps:**

1.  **Voxelization:** Subdivide the space containing the medium into voxels. Align the grid such that the viewing direction is the principal axis [S1][S2, book].
2.  **Define Reference Space:** Create a smaller, normalized space using the mean density of the cloud. This space is used to compute the generic contribution-ratio pattern (3D-filter) [S1][S2, book].
3.  **Compute 3D-Filter (Precomputation):**
    *   For a center voxel in the reference space, compute the contribution ratios from other voxels to the radiance in the viewing direction [S1][S2, book].
    *   Consider only the first three orders of scattering for sunlight to save computation time [S1][S2, book].
    *   Account for the sunlight direction when computing these ratios [S1][S2, book].
    *   **Optimization:** Since scattering in clouds is mainly forward, energy scattered at a point lies within a small solid angle. Use a stochastic method to select only those voxels that effectively contribute to the center voxel for paths with one or two scatterings [S1][S2, book].
    *   Compute the extended form factors for these selected voxels [S1][S2, book].
    *   Store the resulting contribution-ratio pattern as the 3D-filter [S1][S2, book].
4.  **Apply Filter to Scene:**
    *   For each voxel in the actual scene space, apply the 3D-filter [S1][S2, book].
    *   Multiply the precomputed form factors by the energy (radiance) at the related source voxels [S1][S2, book].
    *   Accumulate these contributions to obtain the multiple-scattered radiance component $L_{MS}(x)$ for the center voxel in the viewing direction [S1][S2, book].
    *   Store the light scattered due to second and third order scattering in the viewing direction for each voxel [S1][S2, book].
5.  **Compute Single Scattering:**
    *   Compute single scattering separately for sky light [S1][S2, book].
    *   (Optional) Compute single scattering for sunlight if not fully absorbed into the multiple scattering approximation [S1][S2, book].
6.  **Final Radiance:**
    *   Combine the single-scattered sky light and the multiple-scattered sunlight (from steps 4 and 5) to obtain the final radiance $L(x)$ [S1][S2, book].

**Formulas:**
*   Single Scattering Albedo: $\omega_0 = \frac{\sigma_s}{\sigma_t}$ [S1].
*   Radiance Contribution (Multiple Scattering): $L_{MS}(x) = \sum_{i} F_i \cdot E_i$ [S1], where $F_i$ are the extended form factors (from the 3D-filter) and $E_i$ is the energy at the related source voxel $i$.
*   Total Radiance: $L(x) = L_{SS, sky}(x) + L_{MS, sun}(x)$ [S1][S2, book].

**Parameters:**
*   **Scattering Orders:** Only the first three orders of scattering are considered for sunlight multiple scattering [S1][S2, book].
*   **Voxel Resolution:** Not given in the sources.
*   **Stochastic Sample Size:** Not given in the sources.

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Forward Scattering Assumption:** The method relies on scattering being mainly forward. If the phase function is highly backscattering, the stochastic selection of contributing voxels may miss significant energy [S1][S2, book].
*   **Density Variations:** The filter is computed using the *mean* density. High local density variations may lead to inaccuracies if not accounted for in the energy multiplication step [S1][S2, book].
*   **Boundary Conditions:** The method assumes an infinite or periodic medium for the filter computation. Edge effects in the scene may require special handling [G].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Precomputation of the 3D-filter is expensive but done once. Rendering time is linear in the number of voxels, as it involves multiplying precomputed form factors by voxel energies and accumulating [S1][S2, book].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storing the 3D-filter (extended form factors) for each voxel. The size depends on the number of contributing voxels per filter and the scattering orders considered [S1][S2, book].
*   **Strengths:** Efficiently handles multiple scattering in dense media like clouds and snow. Reduces computational cost by limiting scattering orders and using stochastic sampling for form factor computation [S1][S2, book].
*   **Limitations:** Limited to the first three scattering orders. Accuracy depends on the forward-scattering assumption. Less flexible for arbitrary phase functions or non-cloud-like media [S1][S2, book].
*   **When to Use:** Rendering clouds and snow where multiple scattering is dominant and forward scattering is a valid approximation [S1][S2, book].
*   **When Not to Use:** Media with strong backscattering or where higher-order scattering effects are critical [S1][S2, book].

## Background
The 3D-Filter method was proposed by Nishita et al. to render clouds with multiple scattering and sky light [S1][S2, book]. It was later applied to render snow, which has a high albedo and appears white due to multiple scattering [S1]. The shape and density distribution of snow in these applications were modeled using metaballs [S1]. The method is part of a broader class of techniques that use implicit representations of directional distribution, such as scattering patterns or diffusion equations [S1].
