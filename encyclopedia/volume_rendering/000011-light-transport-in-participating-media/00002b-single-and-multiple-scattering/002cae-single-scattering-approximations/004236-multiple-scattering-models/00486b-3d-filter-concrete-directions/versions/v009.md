# 3D-Filter / Concrete Directions

Also called: 3D-Filter / Nishita, Nishita et al. 3D-Filter, 3D-Filter / N Directions, Concrete Directions Method

## Overview
The 3D-Filter method, introduced by Nishita et al., approximates multiple scattering in participating media (such as clouds and snow) by precomputing a directional distribution of radiance using extended form factors arranged in a 3D grid [S1][S2, book]. It distinguishes itself from sibling methods by explicitly modeling the second and third orders of scattering via a "contribution-ratio pattern" (the 3D-filter) that exploits the forward-scattering nature of the medium to reduce computational cost, rather than relying on diffusion equations or stochastic path tracing [S1][S2, book].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** A voxelized representation of the participating medium (cloud or snow), the viewing direction, the sunlight direction, and the single-scattering albedo $\omega_0$. The medium is assumed to have a mean density for the purpose of filter generation [S2, book].
*   **Outputs:** The total radiance $L(x)$ at each voxel $x$ in the viewing direction, composed of single-scattered skylight, single-scattered sunlight, and multiple-scattered sunlight (orders 2 and 3) [S2, book].

**Data Structures:**
1.  **Voxel Grid:** The space containing the medium is subdivided into voxels. The viewing direction is treated as a principal axis for the grid orientation [S2, book].
2.  **3D-Filter (Contribution-Ratio Pattern):** A precomputed data structure representing the contribution ratios of radiance from neighboring voxels to a center voxel in the viewing direction. This filter accounts for paths with one or two scatterings (resulting in second and third order scattering contributions) [S2, book].
    *   The filter is computed on a smaller space defined by the mean density of the cloud [S2, book].
    *   It stores light scattered due to second and third order scattering in the viewing direction [S2, book].

**Algorithm Steps:**
1.  **Voxelization:** Subdivide the space including the cloud into voxels, aligning the viewing direction as a principal axis [S2, book].
2.  **Filter Generation (Precomputation):**
    *   Define a smaller reference space using the mean density of the cloud [S2, book].
    *   Compute the contribution ratios to the radiance of a center voxel (in the viewing direction) from other voxels in this reference space, taking the sunlight direction into account [S2, book].
    *   Exploit the fact that scattering in clouds is mainly forward. Concentrate computational effort on voxels that effectively contribute to the center voxel for paths having one or two scatterings [S2, book].
    *   Use a stochastic method to select the voxels that effectively contribute to the center voxel [S2, book].
    *   Store these contribution ratios as the 3D-filter [S2, book].
3.  **Single Scattering Computation:**
    *   Compute single scattering separately for sunlight and skylight [S2, book].
    *   Skylight contribution is computed as single scattering by particles [S2, book].
    *   Sunlight single scattering is computed along the viewing ray [S2, book].
4.  **Multiple Scattering Application:**
    *   Apply the 3D-filter to the voxels in the whole space [S2, book].
    *   For each voxel, multiply the precomputed form factors (from the 3D-filter) by the energy at the related points (voxels) [S1].
    *   Accumulate these results to obtain the multiple scattering component of $L(x)$ [S1].
    *   Specifically, store for each voxel the light scattered due to the second and third order scattering in the viewing direction [S2, book].
5.  **Final Radiance:**
    *   Sum the single-scattered sunlight, single-scattered skylight, and the accumulated multiple-scattered sunlight (orders 2 and 3) to obtain the final radiance $L(x)$ [S2, book].

**Parameters:**
*   **Scattering Orders:** Only the first three orders of scattering are considered for sunlight multiple scattering to save computation time [S2, book]. Single scattering is computed separately [S2, book].
*   **Directional Sampling:** In related implementations (e.g., [61] in S2), radiance is estimated by considering eight precise directions around any particular sampling point of the viewing ray (sunlight direction, perpendicular to sunlight, horizontal along Earth surface, zenith) [S2, book].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Complexity:** The method reduces complexity by considering only the first three orders of scattering and using a stochastic selection of contributing voxels based on forward scattering [S2, book]. The cost of computing form factors between each pair of voxels is avoided by using the precomputed 3D-filter [S2, book].
*   **Performance:** The method is designed to save computation time compared to full multiple scattering simulations [S2, book].
*   **Strengths:** Efficiently handles multiple scattering in highly scattering media like clouds and snow by exploiting forward scattering properties [S2, book].
*   **Limitations:** Limited to the first three orders of scattering, which may not capture all multiple scattering effects in very dense media [S2, book]. Relies on the assumption of forward scattering, which may not hold for all media [S2, book].
*   **Usage:** Applied to render clouds and snow [S2, book]. Snow is modeled using metaballs for shape and density distribution [S2, book].

## Background
The 3D-Filter method was proposed by Nishita et al. for displaying clouds taking into account multiple scattering and sky light [S2, book]. It is also applied to render snow, which has a high albedo and looks white due to multiple scattering [S2, book]. The method is part of a broader family of techniques that use implicit representations of directional distribution, such as scattering patterns and diffusion equations [S1].
