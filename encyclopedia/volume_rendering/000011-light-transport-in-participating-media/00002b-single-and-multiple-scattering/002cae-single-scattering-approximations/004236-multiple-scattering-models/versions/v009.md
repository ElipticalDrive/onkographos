# Multiple Scattering Models

Also called: Multiple scattering, higher-order scattering, indirect volumetric illumination

## Overview
Multiple scattering models simulate the effect of light undergoing two or more scattering events within a participating medium, solving the problem of rendering dense media (such as thick clouds, smoke, or fur) that appear dark and lifeless under single-scattering approximations because they fail to account for light trapped and re-emitted within the volume [S1][S2, unofficial]. Unlike single-scattering methods, which are sufficient for thin media like light haze, multiple scattering is required to capture the high albedo and volumetric glow of dense participating media [S1][S2, unofficial]. This family is distinguished from its siblings by the explicit handling of indirect bounces, whether through recursive ray casting, precomputed angular distributions, or spectral reweighting, rather than relying solely on direct lighting or single-bounce approximations.

## Shared principles
All methods in this family address the radiative transfer equation's in-scattering term, which accounts for light arriving at a point $x$ from directions other than the direct line of sight. The core challenge is the computational cost of integrating this term over the sphere of directions, as each scattering event multiplies the complexity [S1].

The fundamental quantity governing scattering is the single-scattering albedo $\omega_0$, defined as the ratio of the scattering coefficient $\sigma_s$ (or $\mu_s$) to the extinction coefficient $\sigma_t$ (or $\mu_t = \sigma_a + \sigma_s$):
$$ \omega_0 = \frac{\sigma_s}{\sigma_t} $$
This value represents the probability that a photon will scatter rather than be absorbed at an interaction point [S1].

The phase function $p(\omega_i, \omega_o)$ describes the angular distribution of scattered light, where $\omega_i$ is the incident direction and $\omega_o$ is the outgoing direction. While single-scattering models often use a fixed phase function (e.g., isotropic $1/4\pi$ or Henyey-Greenstein), multiple scattering models must account for the cumulative effect of multiple phase function evaluations. In many approximations, higher-order scattering tends toward isotropy, as the directionality of the original light source is "forgotten" after many bounces [S1].

The total radiance $L$ reaching the eye is decomposed into contributions from different scattering orders. In general:
$$ L = L_{direct} + L_{single} + L_{multiple} $$
where $L_{multiple}$ includes all bounces $n \ge 2$. The specific implementation of $L_{multiple}$ varies significantly across the subtopics, ranging from brute-force recursion to analytic approximations.

## Subtopics

### [Heterogeneous In-scattering with Multiple Scattering](4934-heterogeneous-in-scattering-with-multiple-scattering.md)
This method computes radiance in heterogeneous volumes by recursively casting rays to simulate indirect lighting up to a maximum depth. It is distinguished by its explicit recursive ray-casting approach, which handles direct lighting and indirect bounces via a `computeInscattering` function. Unlike [Most Probable Paths (MPP) Lighting Model](12244-most-probable-paths-mpp-lighting-model.md), which uses precomputed filters, this method performs stochastic sampling of scattering directions. It differs from [Riley et al. Angular Distribution Renderer](12249-riley-et-al-angular-distribution-renderer.md) by not using pre-computed angular distribution tables. A key implementation detail is the use of a hardcoded isotropic phase function ($1/4\pi$) for both direct and indirect scattering, which simplifies computation but reduces physical accuracy for anisotropic media. The recursion is typically capped at a depth of 5.

### [Most Probable Paths (MPP) Lighting Model](12244-most-probable-paths-mpp-lighting-model.md)
The MPP model approximates multiple scattering by decomposing radiance into object, single-scattered, and multiple-scattered components, using the concept of Most Probable Paths to efficiently approximate the complex multiple-scattering integral. It sets itself apart by handling inhomogeneous media and avoiding boundary artifacts through locally determined effective scattering angles. Unlike diffusion-based methods, it uses a "Light Volume" (a variation of a deep shadow map) to store scattering events from the light source. The model approximates the MPP as two straight line segments (eye-to-scatter and scatter-to-light) and integrates over the number of scattering events $\ell$. It is distinguished from [Heterogeneous In-scattering with Multiple Scattering](4934-heterogeneous-in-scattering-with-multiple-scattering.md) by its non-recursive, single-sweep nature and from [3D-Filter / Concrete Directions](12253-3d-filter-concrete-directions.md) by its reliance on Gaussian blurring of incident radiance rather than precomputed contribution ratios.

### [Riley et al. Angular Distribution Renderer](12249-riley-et-al-angular-distribution-renderer.md)
This single-pass method efficiently approximates multiple scattering for large atmospheric particles (water/ice) by using pre-computed angular distribution functions $A_{m\lambda}^{\lfloor n \rfloor}$ to direct in-scattered light into the eye ray. It solves the problem of premature darkening in low-albedo clouds by capturing chromatic effects like rainbows and halos. It is distinguished from explicit path-tracing models by avoiding recursive ray casting; instead, it uses a half-angle slicing scheme to serve both light attenuation and eye ray accumulation in one pass. It differs from [3D-Filter / Concrete Directions](12253-3d-filter-concrete-directions.md) by using angular distribution tables rather than spatial contribution ratios. A critical parameter is the user-adjustable backscatter term $\xi$, which prevents the illuminated edge of a cloud from appearing unnaturally dark.

### [3D-Filter / Concrete Directions](12253-3d-filter-concrete-directions.md)
This method approximates multiple scattering of sunlight (up to the third order) and single scattering of skylight using a precomputed, grid-based "contribution-ratio pattern" (the 3D-filter). It solves the efficiency problem of full path tracing by implicitly representing directional radiance distribution. It sets itself apart from [Heterogeneous In-scattering with Multiple Scattering](4934-heterogeneous-in-scattering-with-multiple-scattering.md) by using a voxel grid aligned with the viewing direction and precomputed filters, rather than recursive ray casting. It differs from [Riley et al. Angular Distribution Renderer](12249-riley-et-al-angular-distribution-renderer.md) by operating in spatial domain (voxel-to-voxel contribution) rather than angular domain. The method relies on the assumption that scattering in clouds is mainly forward, using stochastic sampling to select contributing voxels for 1-2 scattering paths.

### [Multiple Scattering](12474-multiple-scattering.md)
This entry refers to the general brute-force or unbiased simulation of multiple scattering, often implemented as a configurable "Volume Trace Depth" in renderers. It simulates photons changing direction at least twice. It is distinguished from the approximation-based siblings (MPP, Riley, 3D-Filter) by its explicit tracing of bounces, which is computationally expensive but physically accurate. It differs from [Hybrid Hair/Fur Rendering Strategy](15774-hybrid-hur-rendering-strategy.md) by not restricting geometric interactions to the first hit; instead, it continues tracing through the volume for all bounces up to the depth limit. A common pitfall is noise due to high variance in path lengths and the difficulty of denoising volumes due to the lack of surface normals.

### [Hybrid Hair/Fur Rendering Strategy](15774-hybrid-hair-fur-rendering-strategy.md)
This method approximates multiple scattering in dense hair and fur by treating the aggregate fiber volume as an anisotropic participating medium, while retaining explicit geometric ray tracing for direct illumination. It differs from [Heterogeneous In-scattering with Multiple Scattering](4934-heterogeneous-in-scattering-with-multiple-scattering.md) by restricting geometric interactions to the first hit and handling all subsequent transport via a voxelized medium. It uses a spectral reweighting technique, summing scaled terms over $N$ octaves to approximate higher-order scattering, where each octave uses a modified phase function that becomes increasingly isotropic. This avoids the "dry" appearance and local similarity assumptions of dual scattering models.

### Multiple Scattering (Max/Kajiya)
This minor variant is an extension of Blinn's model to arbitrary shapes via analytical functions, accounting for multiple reflections [S2, unofficial]. Kajiya's two-pass method involves Pass 1, which pre-calculates reflected light proportion per voxel considering attenuation, and Pass 2, which renders the image via ray casting using the pre-calculated intensity info [S2, unofficial]. It is the first scattering rendering model by Kajiya [KH84] and was extended by Max [Max94] to include anisotropic light dispersion (e.g., halo effect) [S2, unofficial].

### Multiple Forward/Backward Scattering Approximation
This variant generalizes single-pass shadow algorithms to multiple forward scattering by gathering accumulated light from several sampled directions in the previous sampling plane (Kniss et al. [30]). Zhang et al. [62] added backward scattering from the next couple of sampling planes [S3, book].

### Ambient Term Approximation for Multiple Scattering
This approach approximates multiple scattering by an ambient term to obtain images of typical shafts of light caused by sunlight, enabling interactive rendering of atmospheric effects [S4].

## Background
The problem of multiple scattering in participating media has been a central challenge in computer graphics since the early days of volume rendering. The first model to account for scattering was presented by Kajiya [KH84], a two-pass method that pre-calculated reflected light proportions per voxel [S2, unofficial]. This was extended by Max [Max86, Max94] to handle arbitrary shapes and anisotropic light dispersion, such as halos [S2, unofficial].

In the context of real-time and GPU rendering, the high cost of simulating dozens or hundreds of bounces (as seen in thick cumulus clouds, which reflect up to 90% of incident light) led to the development of approximations [S1]. Methods like the Riley et al. Angular Distribution Renderer target large atmospheric particles, while others like the 3D-Filter method focus on clouds and snow [S1][S2, unofficial]. The Most Probable Paths (MPP) model provides a way to handle inhomogeneous media without the boundary artifacts of diffusion approximations [S1].

More recently, specialized strategies like the Hybrid Hair/Fur Rendering Strategy have adapted multiple scattering concepts to dense, anisotropic media by combining explicit geometry with volumetric approximations [S1]. Modern engines often expose a "Volume Trace Depth" parameter, allowing users to balance between single-scattering performance and the realistic glow of multiple scattering, though denoising remains difficult due to the lack of helper data like normals inside volumes [S1].
