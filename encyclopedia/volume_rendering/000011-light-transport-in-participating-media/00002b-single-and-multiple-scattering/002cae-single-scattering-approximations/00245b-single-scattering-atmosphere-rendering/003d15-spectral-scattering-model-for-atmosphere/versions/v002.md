# Spectral Scattering Model for Atmosphere

Also called: Nishita, Nishita et al. Earth from Outer Space Method, Nishita et al. sky rendering, Physical Model for Atmospheric Light Transport, Spectral Scattering with Water Bodies and Tone Mapping

## Overview

This method visualizes the Earth from outer space by simulating light transport through a planetary atmosphere and large water bodies, accounting for Rayleigh and Mie scattering, direct illumination, indirect scattered light, interreflections between surface and volume, and seabed reflections [S2]. It sets itself apart from its siblings by employing fully spectral computations (precomputed into lookup tables) to achieve high realism in color reproduction, supporting arbitrarily dense atmospheres and water surfaces within a single precomputation framework for real-time GPU rendering [S2]. Unlike [Single-Scattering Atmosphere Rendering](3289-single-scattering-atmosphere-rendering.md), which focuses on single scattering approximations, this method computes multiple scattering orders (typically up to 6) and integrates water body optics [S1, S2].

## How it works

**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Inputs:** Viewpoint position $P_O$, view direction $V$, light source direction $L$ (Sun/stars), atmospheric density profiles, water body geometry (seabed depth), and spectral parameters.
*   **Outputs:** Final spectral color $I_F$ for each pixel.

**Data Structures:**
The method relies on precomputed lookup tables (LUTs) generated via CPU-based spectral integration. These tables store spectral data for real-time GPU sampling.
*   **Atmospheric Scattering Texture:** 4D table packed into a $32 \times 128 \times 256$ 3D texture. Resolution: $32 \times 128 \times 32 \times 8$ (dimensions for spatial/angular/spectral data).
*   **Water Scattering Texture:** 3D texture, resolution $32 \times 64 \times 64$.
*   **Extinction Texture:** 2D texture, resolution $256 \times 512$.
*   **Ambient Texture:** 1D texture, size 256.
*   **Precision:** 16-bit floating-point values per channel. Total memory: ~10MB [S2].

**Parameters:**
*   **Rayleigh Scale Height ($H_R$):** $\approx 8$ km [S2].
*   **Mie Scale Height ($H_M$):** $\approx 1.2$ km [S2].
*   **Mie Anisotropy ($g$):** Range $[-1, 1]$ [S2].
*   **Spectral Sampling:** 15 evenly distributed samples across the visible spectrum [S2].
*   **Numerical Integration:** Regular trapezoidal rule with 30 samples per integral [S2].
*   **Scattering Orders:** 6 orders for convergence; up to 10 for very dense atmospheres [S2].

**Formulas:**

1.  **Scattering Coefficients:**
    The scattering coefficients for Rayleigh ($\sigma_R$) and Mie ($\sigma_M$) are calculated based on particle polarizability $\alpha_{\{R,M\}}$, index of refraction $n_e$, molecular number density $N_{\{R,M\}}$, and wavelength $\lambda$. $\sigma_M$ is wavelength-independent [S2].

2.  **Density Scale Function:**
    The density at altitude $h$ is modeled exponentially:
    $$ p_{\{R,M\}}(P) = \exp\left(-\frac{h}{H_{\{R,M\}}}\right) $$
    where $H_{\{R,M\}}$ is the scale height [S2].

3.  **Phase Functions:**
    *   **Rayleigh Phase Function $F_R(\theta)$:**
        $$ F_R(\theta) = \frac{3}{4}(1 + \cos^2(\theta)) $$
        where $\theta$ is the scattering angle [S2].
    *   **Mie Phase Function $F_M(\theta, g)$:**
        Improved Henyey-Greenstein approximation by Cornette and Shanks [1992]:
        $$ F_M(\theta, g) = \frac{3(1-g^2)}{2(2+g^2)} \frac{(1+\cos^2(\theta))}{(1+g^2-2g\cos(\theta))^{3/2}} $$
        where $g$ describes anisotropy [S2].

4.  **Optical Depth:**
    The optical depth $t(S, \lambda)$ along path length $S$ is the sum over scattering types $i \in \{R, M\}$:
    $$ t(S, \lambda) = \sum_{i \in \{R, M\}} \sigma_i(\lambda) \int_0^S p_i(s') ds' $$
    Computed via trapezoidal integration of sampled density [S2]. The atmosphere is non-uniformly subdivided into concentric spherical layers where density variation between layers is below a threshold, using linear interpolation for density at sampling points [S1].

5.  **Single Scattering Intensity:**
    The intensity $I_{S_{\{R,M\}}}^{(1)}(P_O, V, L, \lambda)$ reaching observer $P_O$ is:
    $$ I_{S_{\{R,M\}}}^{(1)}(P_O, V, L, \lambda) = R(P_O, V, L, \lambda) + I_I(\lambda) F_{\{R,M\}}(\theta) \cdot \frac{\sigma_{\{R,M\}}(\lambda)}{4\pi} \int_{P_a}^{P_b} \rho_{\{R,M\}}(P) e^{-t(P P_c, \lambda) - t(P_a P_b, \lambda)} ds $$
    Where $I_I(\lambda)$ is incident light intensity, $\rho_{\{R,M\}}(P)$ is the density scale function, and $t$ represents optical depths along segments [S2]. Note: The source provides conflicting versions of Eq. 8; the integral form above is the detailed version provided [S2].

6.  **Water Refraction and Scattering:**
    *   Compute refraction ray $V_{0R}$ in the fragment shader using `refract()` [S2].
    *   Determine incident light $I_0I(P_g, V_R, \lambda)$: if $V_R$ hits the star disc, $I_0I(P_g, V_R, \lambda) = I_0I(P_g, L, \lambda)$; otherwise, it is zero [S2].
    *   Obtain water scattering $I_S^{water}$ by fetching the water scattering texture using the depth $D$ of the intersection point with the seabed [S2].
    *   Light transmission through water molecules is considered analytically [S1].

7.  **Final Color and Tone Mapping:**
    The final color $I_F$ is the sum of surface color $I_S$ and the attenuated participating medium contribution $I_P$:
    $$ I_F = I_S + I_P \cdot e^{-t(P_a P_g, \lambda)} $$
    Due to high dynamic range, tone mapping is applied using exposure constant $E$:
    $$ I_F = 1 - \exp(-E \cdot I_F) $$
    [S2].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **High Dynamic Range:** $I_F$ has a very high dynamic range, requiring tone mapping to correct [S2].
*   **Seabed Color:** The contribution of the color of the bottom of the sea is neglected due to its depth [S1].
*   **Clouds:** Fake clouds are added by mapping 2D fractal textures onto spherical layers; they do not cast shadows onto the Earth [S1].
*   **Ozone/Surface Reflection:** Irwin's variant neglects light from the sky (not the sun) reflected from the Earth’s surface and ozone absorption [S1].

## Subtopics

### [Klassen's Spectral Atmospheric Method](12251-klassen-s-spectral-atmospheric-method.md)
Klassen's Spectral Atmospheric Method is a physically-based rendering technique for simulating sky color and solar illumination in planetary atmospheres by explicitly treating spectral scattering. It solves the problem of inaccurate color reproduction in atmospheric rendering, where standard RGB-based approximations fail to capture the wavelength-dependent nature of Rayleigh and Mie scattering. It sets itself apart from its siblings by introducing a full spectral treatment (33 wavelengths) for the sky while retaining RGB efficiency for non-selective fog, and by distinguishing between single-scattering haze layers and multiple-scattering sky color contributions.

## Operating characteristics

*   **Time Complexity:** Precomputation is intensive. It took 1.5 hours for the entire dataset with 6 scattering orders on an Intel Core 2 Duo 1.86 GHz CPU [S2].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Lookup tables consume ~10MB of texture memory (16-bit float per channel) [S2].
*   **Performance:** Real-time performance is achieved on GPU. At 1024×768 resolution, average 180 FPS (min 85 FPS). At 2560×2048 resolution, average 60 FPS (min 29 FPS), with ~1.1 million static triangles [S2].
*   **Scaling:** Performance is stable across resolutions. The method is slower than Bruneton and Neyret [2008] because it evaluates reflection and scattering from water bodies, whereas Bruneton and Neyret report lower framerates because they account for terrain morphology, which this method does not [S2].
*   **Strengths:** Physically-based, fully spectral, supports arbitrarily dense atmospheres and large water bodies, real-time capable.
*   **Limitations:** High precomputation time, neglects seabed color contribution, fake clouds do not cast shadows.
*   **Usage:** Targets real-time applications such as flight simulators, games, virtual environments, and software like Google Earth (after simplifications) [S2].

## Background

The method originates from Nishita et al. [59, 64], who presented a method to visualize the Earth from outer space, accounting for atmospheric particles and water [S1]. It extends the work of Kaneda et al. [42] by computing multiple scattering and light reflected from the ground [S1]. The specific spectral implementation with water bodies and tone mapping is detailed in [S2], which builds on works by Bruneton and Neyret [2008] and Elek [2009] but adds fully spectral computations and water support [S2]. It is used in scientific visualization and real-time rendering of planetary atmospheres [S1, S2].
