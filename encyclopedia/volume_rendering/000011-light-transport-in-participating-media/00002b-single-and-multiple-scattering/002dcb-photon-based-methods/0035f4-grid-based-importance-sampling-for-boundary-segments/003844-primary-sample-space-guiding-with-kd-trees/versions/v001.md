# Primary-Sample-Space Guiding with Kd-Trees

Also called: Kd-tree guided sampling of boundary segments, Kd-tree importance sampling for boundary segments, Primary-sample-space guiding

## Overview
This method efficiently estimates boundary path integrals in differentiable rendering by constructing Kd-trees over the 3D primary sample space to importance sample boundary segments [S1][S2, unofficial]. It addresses the challenge that the integrand is complex and potentially discontinuous, making uniform sampling inefficient [S1]. Unlike forward rendering path guiding, which accelerates unidirectional path tracing, this technique focuses on the unique boundary integrals required for gradient computation in differentiable rendering [S1]. It builds on [Grid-based Importance Sampling for Boundary Segments](7643-grid-based-importance-sampling-for-boundary-segments.md) by replacing the rigid regular grid with an adaptive Kd-tree structure that automatically refines based on the local structure of the approximated integrand [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs:**
*   Scene geometry $\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ and material parameters.
*   Pre-computed photon maps (or importon maps) to approximate source and detector contributions [S2, unofficial].
*   Error threshold $\epsilon$ for Kd-tree leaf termination [S1][S2, unofficial].
*   Number of samples $N_{fit}$ for fitting the integrand (typically 32) [S1][S2, unofficial].

**Outputs:**
*   A global list of Kd-tree leaf nodes, each containing a trilinear fit of the approximated integrand $\tilde{F}$ and its integrated probability mass.
*   A discrete distribution over these leaf nodes for sampling.

**Data Structures:**
*   **Kd-Tree Node:** Contains an axis-aligned bounding box (AABB) in the primary sample space, a boolean flag `is_leaf`, and if a leaf, a trilinear fit coefficient vector and a scalar `mass` (integral of the fit over the AABB).
*   **Global Leaf List:** A list of all leaf nodes in the tree, used to build the discrete sampling distribution.

**Definitions:**
The boundary segment is defined by a point $\mathbf{x}_B$ on a mesh edge and a direction $\boldsymbol{\omega}_B$. The mapping from primary samples $(u_1, \mathbf{u}_2) \in [0,1)^3$ to $(\mathbf{x}_B, \boldsymbol{\omega}_B)$ is continuous within intervals corresponding to mesh edges [S1].
The integrand $F(u_1, \mathbf{u}_2)$ is defined as [S2, unofficial]:
$$ F(\mathbf{u}_1, \mathbf{u}_2) := \hat{f}^B(\mathbf{p}_0^S, \mathbf{p}_0^D) J^B(\mathbf{u}_1, \mathbf{u}_2) h^S(\mathbf{p}_0^S, \mathbf{p}_0^D) h^D(\mathbf{p}_0^S, \mathbf{p}_0^D) $$
where:
*   $J^B$ is the Jacobian determinant of the change of variable from $(\mathbf{x}_B, \boldsymbol{\omega}_B)$ to $(u_1, \mathbf{u}_2)$ [S2, unofficial].
*   $\mathbf{p}_0^S$ and $\mathbf{p}_0^D$ are the source and detector boundary points, derived from primary samples [S2, unofficial]:
    $$ \mathbf{p}_0^S = \mathbf{x}^{-1} \left( \text{rayIntersect} \left( \mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{B}}(u_1), -\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\mathbf{B}}(u_2) \right), \boldsymbol{\theta} \right) $$
    $$ \mathbf{p}_0^D = \mathbf{x}^{-1} \left( \text{rayIntersect} \left( \mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{B}}(u_1), \boldsymbol{\omega}^{\mathbf{B}}(\mathbf{u}_2) \right), \boldsymbol{\theta} \right) $$
    where $\mathbf{x}^{-1}$ is the inverse mapping from scene geometry to reference configuration [S2, unofficial].
*   $h^S$ and $h^D$ are source and detector contributions, approximated by $\hat{h}^S$ and $\hat{h}^D$ using photon mapping [S2, unofficial].

The method approximates the absolute value of the integrand as [S2, unofficial]:
$$ \tilde{F}(u_1, \mathbf{u}_2) = \left| \hat{f}^B(\mathbf{p}_0^S, \mathbf{p}_0^D) \right| J^B(u_1, \mathbf{u}_2) \hat{h}^S(\mathbf{p}_0^S, \mathbf{p}_0^D) \hat{h}^D(\mathbf{p}_0^S, \mathbf{p}_0^D) $$
This approximation is used only to construct the PDF; the estimator remains unbiased [S2, unofficial].

**Algorithm Steps:**

1.  **Pre-partitioning:** Partition the first dimension $u_1 \in [0,1)$ into intervals such that each interval corresponds to a specific edge of a polygonal face in the mesh. This ensures that the mapping from $u_1$ to $\mathbf{x}_B$ is continuous within each interval [S1][S2, unofficial].

2.  **Kd-Tree Construction:** For each interval $[a, b)$, build a Kd-tree over the subspace $[a, b) \times [0, 1)^2$ using the function $\tilde{F}$ [S1][S2, unofficial]. The construction is recursive:
    *   Start with the root node covering the subspace $[a, b) \times [0, 1)^2$.
    *   At each node $r$ with AABB, draw $N_{fit}$ samples uniformly from the AABB.
    *   Compute a trilinear fit of $\tilde{F}$ using these samples.
    *   Calculate the fitting error $err$.
    *   If $err \le \epsilon$, make $r$ a leaf node. Compute the integral of the trilinear fit over the AABB to determine the node's `mass`. Add $r$ to the global leaf list [S1][S2, unofficial].
    *   If $err > \epsilon$, split the node:
        *   For each axis $ax \in \{x, y, z\}$, split the AABB in half along $ax$.
        *   Compute the trilinear fit and total fitting error $err_{ax}$ for both child regions.
        *   Select the axis $ax^*$ that maximizes the total fitting error $err_{ax}$ [S1][S2, unofficial].
        *   Split the AABB along $ax^*$ into two child AABBs.
        *   Create two child nodes and recurse [S1][S2, unofficial].

3.  **Discrete Distribution Creation:** After building all Kd-trees, create a discrete distribution over the global leaf list. The probability mass of each leaf node is proportional to its `mass` (the integral of the trilinear fit of $\tilde{F}$ over its AABB) [S1][S2, unofficial].

4.  **Sampling at Render Time:**
    *   Select a leaf node from the global list using the discrete distribution [S1][S2, unofficial].
    *   Draw primary samples $(u_1, \mathbf{u}_2)$ within the selected node's AABB using the inversion method based on the trilinear fit of $\tilde{F}$ associated with that node [S1][S2, unofficial].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
*   **Discontinuities:** The integrand $\tilde{F}$ can be discontinuous at mesh edges. Pre-partitioning $u_1$ by edges ensures continuity within each interval, preventing the Kd-tree from struggling with discontinuities across splits [S1].
*   **Precision:** The fitting error threshold $\epsilon$ controls the trade-off between tree depth and approximation accuracy. Too small $\epsilon$ leads to deep trees and high memory usage; too large $\epsilon$ leads to poor approximation and high variance [S1][S2, unofficial].
*   **Photon Map Quality:** The accuracy of $\hat{h}^S$ and $\hat{h}^D$ depends on the quality of the pre-computed photon maps. Poor photon maps lead to poor $\tilde{F}$ approximations and ineffective guiding [S2, unofficial].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Kd-tree construction is $O(N \log N)$ where $N$ is the number of leaf nodes, assuming balanced splits. The number of leaves depends on the complexity of $\tilde{F}$ and the threshold $\epsilon$. Sampling is $O(1)$ after tree construction (node selection + inversion).
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(N)$ to store the Kd-tree nodes and global leaf list.
*   **Measured Performance:** The method significantly reduces variance compared to uniform sampling or grid-based methods for complex scenes [S1]. The number of samples for fitting (32) was found to work well for all examples in the source [S1][S2, unofficial].
*   **Strengths:** Adaptive to the structure of the integrand; handles complex geometries and illumination better than fixed grids; unbiased estimator.
*   **Limitations:** Requires pre-computation of photon maps; Kd-tree construction can be expensive for very complex scenes; inversion sampling for trilinear PDFs can be non-trivial to implement efficiently [S1][S2, unofficial].
*   **When to use:** When estimating boundary integrals for differentiable rendering with complex scenes where uniform or grid-based sampling is inefficient.
*   **When not to use:** For simple scenes where uniform sampling is sufficient, or when pre-computing photon maps is not feasible.

## Background
The method was introduced by Yan et al. in 2022 in the paper "Efficient estimation of boundary integrals for path-space differentiable rendering" [S1]. It addresses the challenge of efficiently estimating boundary path integrals, which are unique to differentiable rendering and defined on discontinuity boundaries [S1]. The technique builds on the work of Zhang et al. (2020) who proposed approximating the integrand using photon mapping and discretizing it with a regular grid [S1][S2, unofficial]. The Kd-tree approach improves upon the grid-based method by adapting to the local structure of the integrand, avoiding the need for unpractically high resolutions for complex scenes [S1].
