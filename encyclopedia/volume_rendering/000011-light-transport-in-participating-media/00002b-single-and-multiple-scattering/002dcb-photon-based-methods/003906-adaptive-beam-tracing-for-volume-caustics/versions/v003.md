# Adaptive Beam Tracing for Volume Caustics

Also called: Adaptive beam tracing, Real-time volume caustics with adaptive beam tracing

## Overview
Adaptive Beam Tracing is a real-time algorithm for rendering surface and volume caustics in single-scattering participating media [S1]. It solves the problem of efficiently capturing high-frequency light transport patterns formed by specular refraction and reflection into volumes, which are typically too expensive for path tracing or photon mapping at interactive frame rates [S1]. Unlike [Photon-based Estimation](2521-photon-based-estimation.md) which traces individual rays, this method traces bundles of light (beams) generated from an adaptive grid projected from the light source, allowing for tighter bounding volumes and reduced fill-rate in defocused regions compared to [Photon Beam Mapping](7874-photon-beam-mapping.md) [S1].

## How it works
The method consists of three main phases: adaptive beam generation, tight bounding volume construction, and inscattering integration.

### Inputs and Outputs
*   **Inputs:** Scene geometry (specular surfaces), participating media properties (extinction $\sigma_t$, scattering $\sigma_s(x)$, phase function $p$), light source properties (position, intensity, type), camera view.
*   **Outputs:** Final image with volume and surface caustics.

### Data Structures
1.  **Light G-Buffer:** Stores position, normal, and material properties (transparency, index of refraction) of surfaces directly visible from the light source [S1].
2.  **Caustic Triangle Buffer:** Stores the base triangles of the caustic beams. Each triangle is defined by vertices $v_0, v_1, v_2$ [S1].
3.  **Beam Structure:** Defined by the base triangle vertices $v_i$ and edge directions $d_i$ (reflection/refraction directions at vertices). The beam is bounded by edges $v_i + t_i d_i$ [S1].
4.  **Bounding Volume:** A 6-triangle mesh approximating the beam. It consists of the base triangle, the tip triangle (extruded), and three side triangles connecting them. The side triangles are formed by bilinear patches approximated as triangles to allow rasterization [S1].

### Steps

1.  **Capture Specular Surfaces:** Rasterize specular surfaces from the light source's view into the Light G-Buffer [S1].
2.  **Initial Grid Projection:** Project a coarse 2D planar grid onto the surfaces sampled in the Light G-Buffer. This creates an initial set of potential caustic triangles [S1].
3.  **Adaptive Refinement:** Iteratively refine the grid based on discontinuities:
    *   Detect undersampled regions in the grid [S1].
    *   Split corresponding grid triangles into smaller ones [S1].
    *   Use an oracle to decide whether to keep, subdivide, or discard a beam based on an empirical heuristic considering geometric discontinuities, light intensity, and beam contribution [S1].
4.  **Beam Generation:** For each refined triangle, compute the reflection or refraction directions $d_0, d_1, d_2$ at the vertices $v_0, v_1, v_2$ using the surface normals and material properties (index of refraction) [S1].
5.  **Bounding Volume Construction:**
    *   Determine the extrusion length of the beam. This is calculated by intersecting the beam edges with the view frustum and other scene geometry to handle occlusion [S1].
    *   Construct the tight bounding volume using 6 triangles: the base triangle, the tip triangle (formed by $v_i + L_i d_i$ where $L_i$ is the extrusion length), and three side triangles connecting the base and tip edges [S1].
6.  **Store Primary Beams:** Store the generated primary beams for later passes to handle occlusion and higher-order caustics [S1].
7.  **Multi-Bounce Beam Splitting (Optional/Higher Order):**
    *   Rasterize the scene into impostors using a representative center of projection [S1].
    *   Intersect stored beams with surrounding scene geometry using approximate ray tracing [S1].
    *   Detect depth discontinuities by comparing photon hits at beam edges and surface normals to split secondary beams at silhouettes [S1].
8.  **Rasterize Bounding Volumes:** Extrude the bounding volumes in the geometry shader and rasterize them [S1].
9.  **Inscattering Integration (Pixel Shader):**
    *   For each fragment covered by a beam's bounding volume, intersect the camera ray with the actual beam (not just the bounding volume) [S1].
    *   Compute the intersection interval $\Delta x$ using a 2D ray-triangle edge intersection [S1].
    *   Evaluate the scattered radiance using the approximation formula below [S1].

### Formulas

The scattered radiance $L(\omega)$ towards the viewer is approximated by a single sample:

$$
L(\omega) \approx \Delta x e^{-\sigma_t (s_1 + s_2)} \sigma_s p(\omega \cdot \omega') L_{in}(\omega')
$$

Where:
*   $L(\omega)$: Radiance scattered towards the viewer.
*   $\Delta x$: Length of the ray-beam intersection interval.
*   $\sigma_t$: Extinction coefficient of the medium.
*   $s_1 + s_2$: Total length of the light path from the caustic triangle to the camera (through the medium).
*   $\sigma_s$: Scattering coefficient at the intersection point.
*   $p(\cdot)$: Phase function.
*   $\omega$: Direction towards the camera.
*   $\omega'$: Direction of the beam (incident light direction).
*   $L_{in}(\omega')$: Incident radiance from the beam.

### Parameters
*   **Grid Resolution:** Starts coarse, refined adaptively. Not given in the sources.
*   **Extrusion Length:** Determined by intersection with view frustum and scene geometry. Not given in the sources.
*   **Heuristic Factors:** Empirically set based on geometric discontinuities, light intensity, and beam contribution. Not given in the sources.

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Fill-rate:** Using suboptimal bounding prisms (e.g., convex prisms) consumes vast amounts of fill-rate. Tight bounding volumes are crucial [S1].
*   **Blocky Artifacts:** Using constant shading with too few beams results in blocky artifacts. Adaptive refinement mitigates this [S1].
*   **Undersampling:** Tracing photons and rendering line primitives can suffer from undersampling in regions where light rays are highly divergent. Beam tracing avoids this by covering area [S1].
*   **Beam Splitting:** Handling partial intersections of beams with surfaces is non-trivial. Scene geometry impostors and depth discontinuity detection are used to handle splitting at silhouettes [S1].

## Subtopics

### Adaptive Refinement Heuristic
A method to decide whether a caustic beam should be kept, subdivided, or discarded. The decision is based on an empirically set-up heuristic depending on three factors: geometric discontinuities of the specular objects, the light’s intensity (for spot or textured sources), and the beams’ contribution in the camera image [S1].

### Multi-Bounce Beam Splitting via Depth Discontinuity Detection
A method to simulate higher-order light bounces by detecting intersections of caustic beams with other specular surfaces. It uses a modified beam splitting oracle that compares the difference of photon hits at beam edges and surface normals to detect depth discontinuities, allowing splitting of secondary beams at silhouettes [S1].

### Inscattering and Surface Caustics
A fragment shader technique to compute radiance scattered towards the viewer by intersecting camera rays with caustic beams. It approximates the integral with a single sample assuming beams are narrow enough in image space. The intersection length $\Delta x$ is obtained by a cheap 2D ray-triangle edge intersection [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Performance:** Renders at real-time frame rates (27 FPS, 23 FPS, 26 FPS shown in tests) [S1].
*   **Complexity:** Time and memory complexity depend on the number of adaptive beams and scene geometry. The method reduces sampling resolution and fill-rate in defocused regions compared to non-adaptive methods [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Handles two-sided refractions, inhomogeneous participating media, and multi-bounce light-surface interactions. Computes tighter bounding volumes than previous methods (e.g., Ernst et al. [2005]), supporting multiple bounces and handling occlusions without CPU assistance [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Limited to single-scattering participating media in the core description. Requires approximate ray tracing for beam intersections [S1].
*   **When to use:** Real-time rendering of caustics in participating media where high-frequency light transport patterns are important.
*   **When not to use:** Multi-scattering media (requires different approach), or when offline quality is required and performance is not a constraint (path tracing may be more accurate) [S1].

## Background
Originated by Gábor Liktor and Carsten Dachsbacher at the Karlsruhe Institute of Technology, published in 2011 [S1]. It builds on adaptive beam tracing [Heckbert and Hanrahan 1984] and caustic mapping techniques. It improves upon Liktor and Dachsbacher [2010] by adding adaptive beam generation and tighter bounding volumes, and addresses limitations of Ernst et al. [2005] by handling occlusions and multiple bounces [S1].
