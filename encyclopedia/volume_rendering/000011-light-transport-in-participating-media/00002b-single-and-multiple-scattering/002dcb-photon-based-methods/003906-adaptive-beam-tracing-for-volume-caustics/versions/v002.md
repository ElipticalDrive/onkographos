# Adaptive Beam Tracing for Volume Caustics

Also called: Adaptive beam tracing, Real-time volume caustics with adaptive beam tracing

## Overview
Adaptive Beam Tracing is a real-time algorithm for rendering surface and volume caustics in single-scattering participating media [S1]. It solves the problem of efficiently capturing high-frequency light transport patterns formed by specular reflections and refractions without the prohibitive cost of path tracing or the static limitations of precomputed radiosity [S1]. Unlike sibling methods such as [Photon-based Estimation](2521-photon-based-estimation.md) or [Photon Beam Methods](4976-photon-beam-methods.md) which trace individual rays or photons, this method traces volumetric beams of light [S1]. It differs from [Grid-based Importance Sampling for Boundary Segments](7643-grid-based-importance-sampling-for-boundary-segments.md) by generating beams adaptively from a projected grid in light space, refining resolution based on geometric discontinuities and beam contribution, thereby reducing fill-rate in defocused regions [S1].

## How it works
The method operates in three main phases: adaptive beam generation, tight bounding volume computation, and pixel-shader-based inscattering integration. It supports multiple bounces and two-sided refraction [S1].

### Inputs and Outputs
*   **Inputs:** Scene geometry (specular surfaces), participating media properties ($\sigma_a, \sigma_s, \sigma_t$), light source properties (position, intensity, type), camera parameters.
*   **Outputs:** Final image with volume and surface caustics.

### Data Structures
1.  **Light G-Buffer:** Stores position, normal, and material properties (transparency, index of refraction) of surfaces directly visible from the light source [S1].
2.  **Caustic Triangle Buffer:** Stores the base triangles of the caustic beams. Each triangle is defined by vertices $\mathbf{v}_0, \mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2$ [S1].
3.  **Beam Representation:** A beam is defined by a base triangle and three edges. Each edge $i \in \{0, 1, 2\}$ is defined by a vertex $\mathbf{v}_i$ and a direction $\mathbf{d}_i$ (the reflection or refraction direction of light impinging on $\mathbf{v}_i$) [S1]. The beam volume is bounded by the edges $\mathbf{v}_i + t_i \mathbf{d}_i$ [S1].
4.  **Bounding Volume:** A 6-triangle mesh approximating the beam volume, generated by the geometry shader to minimize rasterization fill-rate [S1].

### Algorithm Steps

1.  **Light Space Rasterization:**
    Rasterize specular surfaces into a light G-buffer to capture directly visible surfaces from the light source's view [S1].

2.  **Initial Grid Projection:**
    Project a coarse 2D regular grid onto the geometry stored in the light G-buffer. This creates an initial set of potential caustic triangles [S1].

3.  **Adaptive Refinement:**
    Iteratively refine the grid based on discontinuities.
    *   Generate potential caustic triangles from the current grid [S1].
    *   Detect undersampled regions using an oracle heuristic [S1].
    *   Split grid triangles in undersampled regions into smaller ones [S1].
    *   The heuristic decides whether to keep, subdivide, or discard a beam based on:
        1.  Geometric discontinuities of specular objects [S1].
        2.  Light intensity (for spot/textured lights) [S1].
        3.  Beam contribution in the camera image [S1].
    *   Repeat until convergence or max depth [S1].

4.  **Beam Direction Calculation:**
    For each base triangle, calculate the edge directions $\mathbf{d}_0, \mathbf{d}_1, \mathbf{d}_2$ by reflecting or refracting the light direction at the triangle's vertices using the material properties from the G-buffer [S1].

5.  **Bounding Volume Generation:**
    Extrude the beam along its edges to form a tight bounding volume.
    *   Determine extrusion length by intersecting beams with the view frustum and handling occlusion [S1].
    *   Construct a 6-triangle mesh representing the warped, diverging, or converging beam volume [S1].
    *   This tight volume is rasterized instead of loose bounding prisms to save fill-rate [S1].

6.  **Inscattering Integration (Primary Pass):**
    Rasterize the bounding volumes of the caustic beams. In the pixel shader, for each fragment:
    *   Intersect the camera ray with the beam volume [S1].
    *   Compute the intersection interval $\Delta x$ using a cheap 2D ray-triangle edge intersection [S1].
    *   Evaluate the inscattered radiance $L(\boldsymbol{\omega})$ using a single sample approximation (assuming narrow beams in image space) [S1]:
        $$ L(\boldsymbol{\omega}) \approx \Delta x e^{-\sigma_t (s_1 + s_2)} \sigma_s p(\boldsymbol{\omega} \cdot \boldsymbol{\omega}') L_{in}(\boldsymbol{\omega}') $$
        Where:
        *   $s_1 + s_2$ is the total light path length from the caustic triangle to the camera [S1].
        *   $\sigma_t$ is the extinction coefficient [S1].
        *   $\sigma_s$ is the scattering coefficient [S1].
        *   $p(\cdot)$ is the phase function [S1].
        *   $L_{in}(\boldsymbol{\omega}')$ is the incident radiance at the sample point [S1].
        *   $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ is the viewing direction, $\boldsymbol{\omega}'$ is the incoming light direction [S1].

7.  **Multi-Bounce Handling (Optional):**
    *   Store primary beams after generation [S1].
    *   Rasterize scene geometry into impostors using a representative center of projection [S1].
    *   Intersect stored beams with impostors using approximate ray tracing [S1].
    *   Detect depth discontinuities by comparing photon hits at beam edges and surface normals to split secondary beams at silhouettes [S1].
    *   Repeat beam generation and tracing for subsequent bounces [S1].

### Pseudocode

```python
def render_adaptive_beam_tracing(scene, camera, light):
    # 1. Light Space G-Buffer
    light_gbuffer = rasterize_light_space(scene.specular_surfaces, light)
    
    # 2. Initial Grid
    grid_resolution = coarse_initial_resolution
    caustic_triangles = project_grid_to_geometry(light_gbuffer, grid_resolution)
    
    # 3. Adaptive Refinement Loop
    while not converged(caustic_triangles):
        # Evaluate heuristic for each triangle
        for tri in caustic_triangles:
            decision = refine_oracle(tri, light_gbuffer, light, camera)
            if decision == SPLIT:
                split_triangle_into_smaller(tri)
            elif decision == DISCARD:
                remove_triangle(tri)
        
        # Re-project and refine grid
        caustic_triangles = update_grid(caustic_triangles)
        
    # 4. Beam Generation and Bounding Volumes
    beams = []
    for tri in caustic_triangles:
        # Calculate refraction/reflection directions at vertices
        d0 = calculate_direction(tri.v0, light.direction, light_gbuffer)
        d1 = calculate_direction(tri.v1, light.direction, light_gbuffer)
        d2 = calculate_direction(tri.v2, light.direction, light_gbuffer)
        
        # Create beam structure
        beam = Beam(base=tri, edges=[(tri.v0, d0), (tri.v1, d1), (tri.v2, d2)])
        
        # Generate tight bounding volume
        beam.bounding_volume = generate_tight_bounding_volume(beam, scene, camera)
        beams.append(beam)
        
    # 5. Primary Pass: Inscattering
    image = clear_image()
    for beam in beams:
        # Rasterize bounding volume
        fragments = rasterize(beam.bounding_volume, camera)
        for frag in fragments:
            # Intersect camera ray with actual beam (not just volume)
            intersection = intersect_camera_ray_with_beam(frag.camera_ray, beam)
            if intersection.exists:
                # Calculate interval length
                delta_x = calculate_intersection_length(intersection)
                
                # Calculate path lengths s1 (light to medium) and s2 (medium to camera)
                s1 = distance(beam.base, intersection.point)
                s2 = distance(intersection.point, camera.position)
                
                # Evaluate inscattering
                radiance = evaluate_inscattering(
                    delta_x=delta_x,
                    s1=s1,
                    s2=s2,
                    sigma_t=scene.media.extinction,
                    sigma_s=scene.media.scattering,
                    phase_function=scene.media.phase,
                    incident_radiance=beam.intensity_at(intersection.point)
                )
                image.add(frag.pixel, radiance)
                
    # 6. Multi-Bounce (if enabled)
    if multi_bounce_enabled:
        current_beams = beams
        for bounce in range(1, max_bounces):
            next_beams = []
            # Rasterize scene as impostors for intersection
            impostors = rasterize_impostors(scene)
            
            for beam in current_beams:
                # Intersect with impostors
                intersections = intersect_beam_with_impostors(beam, impostors)
                
                for inter in intersections:
                    # Check for depth discontinuity to split
                    if detect_depth_discontinuity(beam, inter):
                        # Split beam
                        split_beams = split_beam_at_silhouette(beam, inter)
                        next_beams.extend(split_beams)
                    else:
                        # Continue beam or terminate
                        if should_continue(beam, inter):
                            next_beams.append(beam)
                            
            # Re-generate bounding volumes for new beams
            for beam in next_beams:
                beam.bounding_volume = generate_tight_bounding_volume(beam, scene, camera)
                
            # Render these beams
            for beam in next_beams:
                fragments = rasterize(beam.bounding_volume, camera)
                for frag in fragments:
                    intersection = intersect_camera_ray_with_beam(frag.camera_ray, beam)
                    if intersection.exists:
                        # ... similar inscattering calculation ...
                        pass
            current_beams = next_beams
            
    return image

def evaluate_inscattering(delta_x, s1, s2, sigma_t, sigma_s, phase, incident_radiance):
    # L(w) ≈ Δx * e^(-σt(s1+s2)) * σs * p(w·w') * Lin(w')
    attenuation = exp(-sigma_t * (s1 + s2))
    scattering_term = sigma_s * phase * incident_radiance
    return delta_x * attenuation * scattering_term
```

### Parameters and Typical Values
*   **Initial Grid Resolution:** Coarse, e.g., $16 \times 16$ or similar, depending on scene complexity [S1].
*   **Max Refinement Depth:** Not given in the sources.
*   **Extrusion Length:** Determined dynamically by view frustum intersection and occlusion culling [S1].
*   **Performance:** Renders at real-time frame rates (27 FPS, 23 FPS, 26 FPS reported in tests) [S1].

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Blocky Artifacts:** Using constant shading with too few beams results in blocky artifacts [S1]. Adaptive refinement mitigates this.
*   **Fill-Rate:** Suboptimal bounding prisms (e.g., from Ernst et al. [2005]) consume vast amounts of fill-rate. Tight 6-triangle bounding volumes are required [S1].
*   **Undersampling:** Tracing photons/lines can undersample regions with highly divergent rays. Beam tracing with adaptive refinement handles this better [S1].
*   **Beam Splitting:** Splitting beams at partial surface intersections is non-trivial. The method uses scene geometry impostors and depth discontinuity detection to handle this [S1].

## Subtopics

### [Surface Caustics via Point-in-Volume Test](8444-surface-caustics-via-point-in-volume-test.md)
A method to render surface caustics by extending the caustic fragment shader to sample a camera-view geometry buffer and perform point-in-volume tests on the caustic receiver surfaces. It differs from splatting methods by being a screen-space technique that automatically adapts to high-frequency details without requiring increased resolution.

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Real-time performance is achieved. The adaptive refinement adds overhead but reduces the number of beams needed in smooth regions [S1].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Depends on the number of beams and grid resolution. Light G-buffer and caustic triangle buffers are stored in GPU memory [S1].
*   **Performance:** Reported frame rates of 23-27 FPS for complex scenes with inhomogeneous media and multi-bounce interactions [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Real-time performance, adaptive sampling reduces fill-rate, handles two-sided refraction and multiple bounces, no precomputation required [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Single-scattering approximation for the volume (though multi-bounce surface interactions are supported) [S1]. Approximate ray tracing for beam intersections [S1].
*   **When to use:** Real-time rendering of caustics in participating media where performance is critical and single-scattering is an acceptable approximation.
*   **When not to use:** When multiple scattering within the volume is required, or when offline quality is needed without real-time constraints.

## Background
The method was introduced by Gábor Liktor and Carsten Dachsbacher at the Karlsruhe Institute of Technology in 2011 [S1]. It builds on the principles of adaptive beam tracing [Heckbert and Hanrahan 1984] and caustic mapping [S1]. It improves upon previous works such as Ernst et al. [2005] (tighter bounds, multiple bounces) and Liktor and Dachsbacher [2010] (adaptive generation) [S1].
