# Volumetric Photon Mapping

Also called: Photon Mapping in Participating Media, Real-time volume rendering for three-dimensional fetal ultrasound using volumetric photon mapping, Screen-space volumetric photon mapping, Volume Photon Map Creation, Volumetric Photon Mapping (VPM) for Fetal Ultrasound, Volumetric Photon Mapping (VPM) for Real-Time Fetal Ultrasound, Volumetric photon mapping with screen-space destiny estimation

## Overview
Volumetric Photon Mapping (VPM) is a global illumination technique for participating media that simulates light transport by separating direct (single-scattering) and indirect (multiple-scattering) illumination [S1]. It solves the problem of realistic in-scattering in noisy or low-contrast volumes (such as 3D ultrasound) where local illumination methods fail to differentiate tissues and produce plastic-like visuals [S1]. Unlike conventional photon mapping which relies on KD-trees for neighbor search, this specific implementation variant uses a volume texture for storage and screen-space destiny estimation for rendering, avoiding the computational overhead of tree structures and the artifacts of front-to-back volume slicing [S1].

## How it works
The method follows the two-pass structure of photon-based rendering [Parent](2521-photon-based-estimation.md).

### Inputs and Outputs
**Inputs:**
*   Volume dataset (e.g., 3D ultrasound image).
*   Light source positions and intensities.
*   Medium properties: scattering coefficient $\lambda_s$, absorption coefficient $\lambda_a$, anisotropy coefficient $g$.
*   Parameters: number of photons, exposure, sample count.

**Outputs:**
*   Rendered image with direct and indirect illumination.

### Data Structures
1.  **Volume Texture:** Photons are stored in a 3D volume texture rather than a KD-tree to minimize storage space and accelerate gathering [S1]. The texture stores the flux of each photon at its spatial position.
2.  **Photon Record:** Each photon stores position, incidence direction, and power (flux) [S1].

### Steps

#### 1. Preprocessing: Photon Emission and Storage
1.  **Noise Reduction:** Apply anisotropic diffusion filtering to the input volume data to reduce speckle noise, which degrades gradient-based normal calculations [S1].
2.  **Photon Emission:** Emit photons from light sources. To optimize GPU thread scheduling, use a shared counter `Count` initialized to 0. Threads co-complete emission tasks by incrementing `Count` until it equals the total photon count, minimizing idle thread time [S1].
3.  **Photon Tracking:** Track each photon through the volume using a Markov random walk [S1].
    *   Cast a ray from the light source.
    *   Check for intersections with scene geometry or volume boundaries [S5, unofficial].
    *   If inside the volume, divide the path into steps with jitter [S5, unofficial].
    *   Determine interaction (absorption or scattering) using the cumulative distribution function based on extinction coefficients [S5, unofficial].
    *   If scattered, update the direction using the phase function [S1].
    *   If the photon hits a surface, determine its fate (reflection, absorption, transmission) using Russian roulette (e.g., 50% termination probability after 3 bounces) [S1][S5, unofficial].
4.  **Storage:** Store photon interaction points (position, direction, power) in the volume texture [S1].

#### 2. Rendering: Radiance Estimation
The total scattered radiance $L_s(x', \omega_0)$ is the sum of single-scattering (direct) $L_{ss}$ and multiple-scattering (indirect) $L_{ms}$ components [S1]:
$$ L_{s}(x',\omega _{0})=L_{ss}({x}^{\prime},\omega _{0})+L_{ms}({x}^{\prime},\omega _{0}) $$

**Direct Illumination ($L_{ss}$):**
Calculated via single scattering. The reduced incident light undergoes its first scattering interaction at $x'$ [S1]:
$$ L_{ss}(x', \omega_0)=\int _{\Omega}s({x}^{\prime},{\omega }_{i},{\omega }_{0}){L}_{i}({x}^{\prime},{\omega }_{i})d{\omega }_{i} $$
where $\omega_i$ are directions on a local sphere at $x'$, $s$ is the material shading function, and $L_i$ is attenuated incident radiance [S1].

**Indirect Illumination ($L_{ms}$):**
Calculated using the stored photon map. The radiance reaching a sample $x_s$ along ray direction $\omega_0$ is given by [S1]:
$$ L(x_{s},\omega _{0}) = L_{0}(x_{0},\omega _{0})T(x_{0},x_{s}) + \int _{x_{0}}^{x_{s}}\left[ \lambda _{a} (x^{\prime})L_{e}(x^{\prime},\omega _{0})+\lambda _{s}(x^{\prime})L_{s}(x^{\prime},\omega _{0})\right] T(x^{\prime},x_{s})dx^{\'} $$
For ultrasound rendering, emission is often ignored ($\lambda_a L_e = 0$) [S1]. The transmittance $T(x_i, x_j)$ is [S1]:
$$ T(x_{i},x_{j})=e^{-\int _{x_{i}}^{x_{j}}\tau _{t}dt} $$
where $\tau_t$ is extinction [S1].

The indirect term $L_{ms}$ is computed using a screen-space destiny estimation (ray casting) rather than ray marching [S1]. For a sample $x_n$ along the ray:
$$ L_{x_n}=\sum _{j=1}^{n}\prod _{i=j}^{n-1}T\left( {x}_{i},{x}_{i+1} \right) \int _{{x}_{j-1}}^{{x}_{j}}T\left( {x}^{\prime},{x}_{j} \right) {\sigma }_{s}\left( {x}^{\prime} \right) {L}_{ms}\left( {x}^{\prime} \right) d{x}^{\prime} $$
If $j=n$, the product term equals 1 [S1]. $L_{ms}$ at any point is derived from the density estimation of photons in the volume texture [S1].

        current_dir = photon.direction

                fate = russian_roulette(hit_surface.material)
                if fate == ABSORBED:
                    break
                elif fate == SCATTERED:

                    current_dir = sample_phase_function(current_dir, hit_surface.normal)
                    current_pos = hit_point
                    photon.power *= reflectance # or transmittance
                else:
                    break

def render_ray(ray_origin, ray_direction, volume_data, photon_texture, exposure):
    # 1. Direct Illumination (Single Scattering)
    direct_radiance = compute_direct_scattering(ray_origin, ray_direction, volume_data)
    
    # 2. Indirect Illumination (Multiple Scattering via Screen-Space Destiny Estimation)
    # Ray casting approach instead of ray marching for efficiency
    indirect_radiance = 0.0
    current_pos = ray_origin
    
    # Sample points along the ray
    for j in range(num_samples):
        # Calculate transmittance from previous sample to current
        transmittance_prev = compute_transmittance(ray_origin, current_pos, volume_data)
        
        # Gather photons from volume texture around current_pos
        # This replaces KD-tree neighbor search
        gathered_flux = gather_photons_from_texture(current_pos, volume_data)
        
        # Estimate L_ms at current_pos
        # L_ms is proportional to gathered flux and scattering coefficient
        l_ms = gathered_flux * volume_data.scattering_coefficient
        
        # Integrate contribution
        # Simplified Riemann sum approximation
        step_size = get_step_size(j)
        contribution = transmittance_prev * l_ms * step_size
        
        indirect_radiance += contribution
        
        # Advance position
        current_pos = ray_origin + ray_direction * (j + 1) * step_size

### Parameters
*   **Anisotropy coefficient $g$**: Controls scattering behavior in the Henyey-Greenstein phase function. $g \in [-1, 1]$. Negative values produce back scattering, 0 produces isotropic scattering, and positive values produce forward scattering [S1].
*   **Exposure**: Luminance value assigned to each photon, influencing brightness. Typical tested values: 0.3 to 0.4 [S1].
*   **Number of photons**: Controls scattering realism. Higher counts improve realism but reduce FPS. Large datasets require higher counts [S1].
*   **Samples**: Number of samples used during rendering. Fixed at 800 in tested datasets [S1].
*   **K (Nearest Neighbors)**: In standard VPM, K is used for neighbor search. In the screen-space texture variant, K is not explicitly defined as the texture filtering handles the neighborhood implicitly [S1].

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Speckle Noise**: Ultrasound speckle noise degrades signal-to-noise ratio and makes gradient-based normal calculation unreliable. VPM is less sensitive to this than local illumination methods [S1].
*   **GPU Thread Scheduling**: Traditional VPM creates a thread per photon, leading to resource wastage and idle time. The co-completion method using a shared counter mitigates this [S1].
*   **Storage Overhead**: Traditional KD-tree storage is space-intensive and slow for gathering. Using a volume texture avoids this but requires sufficient VRAM [S1].
*   **Insufficient Photons**: On large volumes, insufficient photons produce unrealistic results. GPU acceleration is required for real-time performance with high photon counts (e.g., up to 10 million) [S1].
*   **Emission Term**: In fetal ultrasound rendering, emission is often ignored, simplifying the RTE [S1].

## Subtopics

### [Beam Radiance Estimate](1283-beam-radiance-estimate.md)
The Beam Radiance Estimate (BRE) is a photon-based technique for estimating accumulated in-scattered radiance along an entire camera ray in participating media, serving as the core gathering step for Volumetric Photon Mapping. Unlike conventional volumetric photon mapping, which estimates incident radiance at discrete points along a ray via spherical neighborhood queries, BRE queries all photons intersecting the ray line simultaneously, replacing Dirac delta and Heaviside functions with a 2D smoothing kernel perpendicular to the ray. This approach eliminates redundant photon gathering and noise associated with large step sizes in ray marching, concentrating computational effort only where photons intersect the viewing path.

### [Photon Emission and Storage](3880-photon-emission-and-storage.md)
Photon Emission and Storage is the data generation phase of Volumetric Photon Mapping, responsible for tracing photons from light sources through participating media and surfaces to populate a spatial data structure with scattering events. It solves the problem of approximating indirect illumination and inscattering by storing photon positions, directions, and energies. It differs from its sibling [Beam Radiance Estimate](1283-beam-radiance-estimate.md) by storing discrete point samples rather than beam-like structures, and from [Photon Beam Tracing](5401-photon-beam-tracing.md) by using standard ray-object intersection for storage rather than beam tracing through volumes.

### [Photon Beam Tracing](5401-photon-beam-tracing.md)
Photon Beam Tracing is a volumetric radiance estimation method where photon paths are stored as beam segments rather than discrete points, allowing for density estimation via beam intersection. It differs from standard volumetric photon tracing by storing the photon energy up to the point of intersection with solid geometry, rather than discarding the photon upon collision. This approach provides a superset of the information contained in a standard photon map, enabling more efficient handling of boundaries between participating media and opaque surfaces.

### Surface Photon Mapping
A method for estimating surface illumination by accumulating energy from photons stored on the same object surface, weighted by distance and surface normal alignment [S3, unofficial]. It initializes an energy accumulator to zero and sets a search radius ($sqRadius$) to 0.7 [S3, unofficial]. It iterates through photons stored for the object, calculating the distance from the query point $p$ to the photon position. If the distance is less than $sqRadius$, it calculates a weight as $\max(0.0, -\text{dot}(N, \text{photons}[id][i][1]))$, representing the cosine of the angle between the surface normal and photon direction [S3, unofficial]. This weight is multiplied by $(1.0 - \text{cur\_dist}) / \text{exposure}$ and added to the energy accumulator along with the photon's power [S3, unofficial].

### Proposed Fetal Ultrasound Rendering Method
A rendering process for ultrasound data that renders direct and indirect lighting separately using a raycaster, with indirect illumination computed via VPM [S1]. The rendering equation for ultrasound data (where emissions are not considered) is split into direct and indirect components:
$$ L(x_{s},\omega _{0}) = L_{0}(x_{0},\omega _{0})T(x_{0},x_{s}) + \int _{x_{0}}^{x_{s}}\lambda _{s}(x^{\prime})L_{ss}(x^{\prime},\omega _{0})T(x^{\prime},x_{s})dx^{\'} + \int _{x_{0}}^{x_{s}}\lambda _{s}(x^{\prime})L_{ms}(x^{\prime},\omega _{0})T(x^{\prime},x_{s})dx^{\'} $$
[S1]. A high dynamic range (HDR) technique is proposed to tackle the wide range of intensities resulting from varying lighting conditions [S1].

### GPU Texture Photon Mapping
A method proposed by Czuczor et al. that stores photon collision information in GPU texture memory and uses texture filtering instead of KD tree nearby-seeking [S1]. This method is only effective for surface rendering, not volume rendering [S1].

### Homogeneous RTE Approximation
For homogeneous media, the in-scattered radiance term in the RTE is approximated by summing contributions over discrete segments along the camera ray [S2]. The segment sample points are $x_0, ..., x_{s-1}$, where $x_0$ and $x_{s-1}$ are the first and last points within the medium, and $x_s$ is a surface point past the medium [S2].

### Reverse Photon Mapping
A modification to conventional photon mapping introduced by Havran et al. to speed up calculations at the last collection stage [S1].

### Hierarchical Photon Mapping
A method proposed by Spencer and Jones that increases illumination accuracy by passing photon energy layer-by-layer through a binary tree system [S1].

### Interactive Photon Map Update
A modification by Daniel et al. to produce interactive character for real-time realistic volume rendering, creating a data format for assessing scene areas affected by altering the transfer function to allow effective updates to the photon map [S1].

### Progressive Photon Mapping
A method developed by Hachisuka et al. that uses a multipass rendering technique to continuously emit photons into the image, gradually reducing inaccuracy [S1].

### Volumetric Photon Mapping with Radiance Transfer
A study by Zhang et al. that incorporated the volumetric photon mapping algorithm into the customary predesigned radiance transfer procedure [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** The preprocessing phase is $O(N_p \cdot B)$, where $N_p$ is the number of photons and $B$ is the average number of bounces per photon. The rendering phase is $O(N_r \cdot N_s)$, where $N_r$ is the number of rays (pixels) and $N_s$ is the number of samples per ray. The use of volume texture for storage avoids the $O(\log N)$ search cost of KD-trees, making gathering $O(1)$ per sample, but requires sufficient VRAM [S1].
*   **Memory Complexity:** $O(N_p)$ for storing photon data in the volume texture. Traditional KD-tree storage is more space-efficient for sparse photon distributions but slower for gathering [S1].
*   **Measured Performance:** On an Intel E5 CPU with 8GB RAM and GPU acceleration, real-time performance (200+ FPS) was achieved for fetal ultrasound datasets with resolutions up to $289 \times 212 \times 266$ using 800 samples and exposure 0.4 [S1]. The method maintains real-time performance even with photon counts up to 10 million [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Simulates realistic global illumination effects (scattering) in noisy volumes; avoids wood-grain artifacts of slice-based rendering; less sensitive to speckle noise than local illumination methods; real-time capable with GPU acceleration [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Requires GPU acceleration; insufficient photons on large volumes produce unrealistic results; storage overhead of volume texture can be high [S1].
*   **When to use:** Real-time volume rendering of participating media where global illumination effects are critical, such as 3D ultrasound visualization [S1].
*   **When not to use:** When VRAM is limited and photon density is low (KD-tree might be better); when surface-only rendering is sufficient (GPU Texture Photon Mapping is more appropriate) [S1].

## Background
Volumetric Photon Mapping is based on the common photon mapping algorithm developed by Jensen [S1]. Jensen and Christensen [S2] solved the Radiative Transfer Equation (RTE) using a combination of photon tracing, ray marching, and density estimation. The specific application to real-time 3D fetal ultrasound rendering was proposed by Qin Jing et al. [S1], aiming to improve visual impression with anti-noise ability by simulating realistic lighting effects (scattering) and providing more depth information compared to conventional methodologies [S1]. The method addresses the challenges of speckle noise and low contrast in ultrasound images, which make traditional local-illumination methods produce plastic-like visuals [S1].
