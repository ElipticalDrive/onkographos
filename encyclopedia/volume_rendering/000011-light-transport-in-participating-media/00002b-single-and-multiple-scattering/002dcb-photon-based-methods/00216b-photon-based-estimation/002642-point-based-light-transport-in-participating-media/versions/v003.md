# Point-Based Light Transport in Participating Media

Also called: PBGI for homogeneous media with refractive boundaries, Point Based Global Illumination (PBGI), Point-Based Global Illumination for Participating Media, Point-Based Rendering for Homogeneous Participating Media with Refractive Boundaries

## Overview
Point-Based Light Transport in Participating Media is a global illumination method for homogeneous translucent materials with refractive boundaries [S2]. It decorrelates scene complexity from illumination computation by replacing the scene with a shaded point cloud (volume samples) for indirect illumination, while keeping polygonal representation for direct visibility [S2]. It computes single, double, and multiple scattering contributions separately [S2]. Unlike methods exploiting screen-space coherence (e.g., ray-marching through light propagation volumes, volumetric shadow mapping) which target media without refractive interfaces, this method targets materials enclosed inside a refractive interface which breaks screen-space coherence [S2].

## How it works
The method separates light transport into single, double, and multiple scattering effects, computing each contribution independently before summing them [S2].

### Inputs and Outputs
**Inputs:**
*   Geometry of the translucent object (polygonal mesh) [S2].
*   Material properties: index of refraction $\eta$, scattering coefficient $\sigma_s$, absorption coefficient $\sigma_a$, phase function $p(\hat{i}, \hat{o})$ [S2].
*   Light source positions and intensities [S2].
*   Camera position and viewing rays [S2].

**Outputs:**
*   Radiance values for each camera ray, composed of direct, single, double, and multiple scattering contributions [S2].

### Data Structures
**Light Surface Sample:**
*   `position`: $x_s$ (3D vector) [S2].
*   `diffuse_intensity`: $I_s$ (scalar) [S2].

**Volume Sample:**
*   `position`: $x_v$ (3D vector) [S2].
*   `incoming_direction`: $d_v$ (3D vector) [S2].
*   `incoming_radiance`: $I_v$ (scalar) [S2].
*   `surface_area`: $S_v$ (scalar, area around the surface sample at the origin of the ray) [S2].
*   `volume_of_influence`: $V_v$ (defined by $S_v$ and an interval along the ray, approximated by an Oriented Bounding Box (OBB)) [S2].

**Octree Node (for Volume Samples):**
*   `children`: Array of 8 child nodes (or null if leaf) [S2].
*   `average_position`: 3D vector [S2].
*   `average_direction`: 3D vector [S2].
*   `direction_variance`: Scalar [S2].
*   `incoming_radiance`: Scalar [S2].
*   `bounding_box`: OBB [S2].
*   `leaf_area`: $A_v$ (Area of the octree leaf cell, only for leaves) [S2].

### Preprocessing Steps
1.  **Place Light Surface Samples:** Place blue-noise surface samples on the object surface [S2]. These store indirect diffuse illumination and indirect illumination from inside the material after multiple bounces [S2].
2.  **Build Surface Hierarchy:** Build an octree spatial hierarchy over the light surface samples for future queries [S2].
3.  **Handle Refractive Interfaces:** If the media is enclosed in transparent layers, place blue-noise samples on the outer layer, connect to the light source, trace the refracted ray through transparent layers to the participating media, and remove samples too close to others to ensure minimal distance [S2].
4.  **Handle Glossy Interfaces:** If enclosed by glossy reflective interfaces, shoot photons from the light source, importance sample outgoing glossy reflection direction, store photons interacting with the media surface, and for each blue-noise surface sample, average the direction and radiance of close photons [S2].
5.  **Create Volume Samples:** For each surface sample, connect to the light source, compute the refracted ray inside the material (including internal reflections on the enclosing surface), and sample the ray to create volume samples [S2]. Store $x_v, d_v, I_v, S_v$ for each volume sample [S2].
6.  **Build Volume Octree (Top-Down):** Start with the bounding box of the material as the root [S2]. Subdivide regularly until there are at most 8 volume samples per cell [S2].
7.  **Build Volume Octree (Bottom-Up):** Compute average position, direction, direction variance, and incoming radiance for each cell using values from its children [S2].
8.  **Compute Density Factor:** For each volume sample $v$, compute the dimensionless density factor $k_v$:
    $$ k_v = \left( \text{clamp} \left( \frac{A_v}{S_v}, 1, 8 \right) \times \text{clamp}(\sigma_t, 0.3, 0.8) \right)^2 $$
    where $A_v$ is the area of the octree leaf cell containing $v$, $S_v$ is the surface area around the sample point, and $\sigma_t = \sigma_s + \sigma_a$ is the extinction coefficient [S2].

### Rendering Steps
1.  **Shoot Camera Rays:** Shoot rays from the camera [S2]. For rays reaching the translucent object, compute the refracted ray inside the object and its direction $\hat{o}$ [S2].
2.  **Place Sample Points:** Place sample points $P_k$ on the camera ray inside the object, defined by depth $d_k$ measured from the entry point [S2].
    *   If the camera ray reaches the media directly or after specular events, place $k_{max}$ samples with depth varying exponentially (more samples near the surface) [S2].
    *   If the camera ray reaches the media after diffuse or glossy interactions, sample randomly with a smaller amount of samples [S2].
3.  **Compute Single Scattering:** Sum contributions from all volume samples whose OBB is intersected by the camera ray [S2]. Traverse octree top-down, discarding non-intersected nodes [S2]. At leaves, test direction variance and phase function; if both below threshold, gather leaf contribution, else gather individual sample contributions [S2].
4.  **Compute Double Scattering:** Traverse the spatial hierarchy to obtain the best tree cut and gather contributions from these nodes [S2].
5.  **Compute Multiple Scattering:** Use a precomputed table storing the resulting contribution for multiple scattering effects [S2].
6.  **Compute Indirect Lighting:** Use the surface samples hierarchy to add contributions from indirect lighting and multiple scattering after several bounces on the refractive surface [S2].
7.  **Combine:** Sum the contributions from single, double, multiple scattering, and indirect lighting [S2].

    # Steps 3 & 4: Handle refractive/glossy interfaces if present [S2]

    # Step 5: Create volume samples by connecting surface samples to light sources [S2]
    volume_samples = []
    for s_sample in surface_samples:
        for light in lights:
            refracted_ray = compute_refracted_ray(s_sample.position, light, material.eta)
            # Sample along refracted ray including internal reflections [S2]
            samples_along_ray = sample_ray_along_path(refracted_ray, object_geometry, material)
            for v in samples_along_ray:
                v.surface_area = s_sample.surface_area
                volume_samples.append(v)

    # Step 6 & 7: Organize volume samples in an octree [S2]
    volume_octree = build_volume_octree(volume_samples)

    # Step 8: Compute density factor for each volume sample [S2]
    sigma_t = material.sigma_s + material.sigma_a
    for leaf in volume_octree.leaves():
        A_v = leaf.bounding_box.area
        for v in leaf.samples:
            ratio = A_v / v.surface_area
            clamped_ratio = max(1.0, min(8.0, ratio))
            clamped_sigma = max(0.3, min(0.8, sigma_t))
            v.density_factor = (clamped_ratio * clamped_sigma) ** 2

    return surface_octree, volume_octree

def render(camera_ray, volume_octree, surface_octree, material):
    # Step 1: Compute refracted ray inside object [S2]
    entry_point, refracted_direction = compute_refraction_at_surface(camera_ray, material.eta)

    # Step 2: Place sample points Pk on camera ray [S2]
    if camera_ray.is_direct_or_specular():
        depths = generate_exponential_depths(k_max, entry_point)
    else:
        depths = generate_random_depths(fewer_samples, entry_point)

    total_radiance = 0.0
    for depth in depths:
        point = entry_point + depth * refracted_direction

        # Step 3: Single scattering [S2]
        single = traverse_volume_octree(point, refracted_direction, volume_octree)

        # Step 4: Double scattering [S2]
        double = compute_double_scattering(point, refracted_direction, volume_octree)

        # Step 5: Multiple scattering [S2]
        multiple = lookup_multiple_scattering_table(point, material)

        # Step 6: Indirect lighting [S2]
        indirect = query_surface_octree(point, surface_octree)

        total_radiance += single + double + multiple + indirect

    return total_radiance

def traverse_volume_octree(point, direction, node):
    # Discard nodes not intersected by the camera ray [S2]
    if not node.bounding_box.intersects_ray(point, direction):
        return 0.0
    if node.is_leaf():
        # Test direction variance and phase function [S2]
        if node.direction_variance < THRESHOLD and evaluate_phase_function(node.average_direction, direction) < THRESHOLD:
            return node.aggregated_contribution
        else:
            contrib = 0.0
            for v in node.samples:
                dist = distance(point, v.position)
                kernel = evaluate_kernel(dist)
                contrib += v.incoming_radiance * v.density_factor * kernel
            return contrib
    else:
        return sum(traverse_volume_octree(point, direction, child) for child in node.children)

### Edge Cases and Pitfalls
*   **Concave Geometries:** The algorithm cannot handle multiple refractions caused by concave geometries; it only handles light paths with a single refraction on the camera ray and a single refraction on the light ray [S2].
*   **Visibility and Occlusion:** The method does not consider visibility when summing contributions from volume and surface samples to a camera sample, resulting in over-estimation when there is occlusion [S1].
*   **Bias:** Point-based methods are inherently biased [S1].
*   **Homogeneous Assumption:** The method assumes homogeneous materials; extension to heterogeneous materials with spatially varying scattering properties requires further work [S1].

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Not given in the sources.
*   **Memory Complexity:** Not given in the sources.
*   **Measured Performance:** The GPU implementation yields 30 fps when moving the viewpoint and 25 fps when editing the light position or material parameters [S2].
*   **Strengths:** Handles curved surfaces and triangles with interpolated normals, unlike methods that require flat surfaces for interactive framerates [S2]. Supports editing of material properties and light position, unlike methods with fixed illumination [S2]. Computes both single and multiple scattering effects, targeting a large range of translucent materials from almost transparent to almost opaque [S2].
*   **Limitations:** Cannot handle multiple refractions from concave geometries [S2]. Accuracy is limited by the number of samples and the spatial hierarchy resolution [S2].

## Background
The method was introduced as a Point-Based Global Illumination method for light transport on homogeneous translucent materials with refractive boundaries [S2]. It addresses the difficulty of simulating illumination inside translucent materials where refraction can focus light deep inside, creating volume caustics [S2]. The method separates single, double, and multiple scattering effects to accurately capture their different appearances: single scattering results in localized high-frequency volume caustics, double scattering is blurrier but still localized, and multiple scattering is mostly diffuse [S2].
