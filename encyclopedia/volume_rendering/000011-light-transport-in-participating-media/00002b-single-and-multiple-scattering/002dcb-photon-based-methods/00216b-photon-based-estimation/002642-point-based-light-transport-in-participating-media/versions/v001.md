# Point-Based Light Transport in Participating Media

Also called: PBGI for homogeneous media with refractive boundaries, Point-Based Global Illumination for Participating Media, Point-Based Rendering for Homogeneous Participating Media with Refractive Boundaries

## Overview
Point-Based Light Transport in Participating Media is a global illumination method for homogeneous translucent materials with refractive boundaries that decorrelates scene complexity from illumination computation by replacing the scene with a shaded point cloud (volume samples) for indirect illumination, while keeping polygonal representation for direct visibility [S1]. It computes single, double, and multiple scattering contributions separately, handling refraction at boundaries which breaks screen-space coherence [S1]. Unlike methods exploiting screen-space coherence (e.g., ray-marching through light propagation volumes, volumetric shadow mapping) which target media without refractive interfaces, this method targets materials enclosed inside a refractive interface [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs:**
*   Geometry of the translucent object (surface mesh).
*   Material properties: index of refraction $\eta$, scattering coefficient $\sigma_s$, absorption coefficient $\sigma_a$, phase function $p(\hat{i}, \hat{o})$ [S1].
*   Light source position and intensity.
*   Camera position and viewing rays.

**Outputs:**
*   Radiance for each camera ray, composed of single, double, and multiple scattering contributions [S1].

**Data Structures:**
1.  **Light Surface Samples:**
    *   Fields: Position $x_s$, diffuse intensity $I_s$ [S1].
    *   Organization: Stored in an octree spatial hierarchy [S1].
2.  **Volume Samples:**
    *   Fields: Position $x_v$, incoming light direction $d_v$, incoming radiance $I_v$, volume of influence $V_v$ [S1].
    *   Volume of Influence $V_v$: Defined by surface area $S_v$ around the sample point and interval along the ray, approximated by an Oriented Bounding Box (OBB) [S1].
    *   Organization: Stored in an octree spatial hierarchy [S1].
3.  **Octree Cells:**
    *   Fields: Average position, average direction, direction variance, incoming radiance (computed bottom-up) [S1].
    *   Leaf cells have at most 8 volume samples [S1].

**Preprocessing Steps:**
1.  **Place Light Surface Samples:** Place blue-noise surface samples on the object surface storing indirect diffuse illumination and indirect illumination from inside the material after multiple bounces [S1].
2.  **Build Surface Hierarchy:** Build an octree spatial hierarchy over the light surface samples for future queries [S1].
3.  **Handle Refractive Interfaces:** For media enclosed in transparent layers, place blue-noise samples on the outer layer, connect to light source, trace refracted ray through transparent layers to the participating media, and remove samples too close to others to ensure minimal distance [S1].
4.  **Handle Glossy Interfaces:** For media enclosed by glossy reflective interfaces, shoot photons from light source, importance sample outgoing glossy reflection direction, store photons interacting with media surface, and for each blue-noise surface sample, average direction and radiance of close photons [S1].
5.  **Create Volume Samples:** Connect surface samples to light source, compute refracted ray inside material (including internal reflections), and sample the ray to create volume samples [S1].
6.  **Build Volume Hierarchy (Top-Down):** Organize volume samples in an octree. Start with bounding box of material as root, subdivide regularly until at most 8 volume samples per cell [S1].
7.  **Build Volume Hierarchy (Bottom-Up):** Compute average position, direction, direction variance, and incoming radiance for each cell using values from children [S1].
8.  **Compute Density Factor:** For each volume sample $v$, compute density factor $k_v$:
    $$k_v = \left( \text{clamp} \left( \frac{A_v}{S_v}, 1, 8 \right) * \text{clamp}(\sigma_t, 0.3, 0.8) \right)^2$$
    where $A_v$ is the area of the octree leaf cell, $S_v$ is the area around the surface sample at the origin of the ray for volume sample $v$, and $\sigma_t = \sigma_s + \sigma_a$ is the extinction coefficient [S1].

**Rendering Steps:**
1.  **Shoot Camera Rays:** Shoot rays from camera. For rays reaching the translucent object, compute the refracted ray inside the object and its direction $\hat{o}$ [S1].
2.  **Place Sample Points:** Place sample points $P_k$ on the camera ray inside the object, defined by depth $d_k$ measured from the entry point [S1].
3.  **Sample Strategy:**
    *   If camera ray reaches media directly or after specular events, place $k_{max}$ samples on the ray with depth varying exponentially to place more samples near the surface [S1].
    *   If camera ray reaches media after diffuse or glossy interactions, sample it randomly with a smaller amount of samples [S1].
4.  **Compute Single Scattering:** Sum contributions from all volume samples whose OBB is intersected by the camera ray. Traverse octree top-down, discarding non-intersected nodes. At leaves, test direction variance and phase function; if both below threshold, gather leaf contribution, else gather individual sample contributions [S1].
5.  **Compute Double Scattering:** Traverse the spatial hierarchy to obtain the best tree cut and gather contributions from these nodes [S1].
6.  **Compute Multiple Scattering:** Use a precomputed table storing the resulting contribution [S1].
7.  **Compute Surface Indirect Lighting:** For indirect lighting and multiple scattering after several bounces on the refractive surface, use surface samples organized in a separate spatial hierarchy and add their contributions [S1].
8.  **Combine:** Add single, double, and multiple scattering contributions [S1].

**Pseudocode:**

```python
def preprocess(object_mesh, light_source, material):
    # Step 1: Place blue-noise surface samples
    surface_samples = place_blue_noise_samples(object_mesh)
    for s in surface_samples:
        s.intensity = compute_indirect_diffuse_illumination(s.position, scene)
    
    # Step 2: Build surface hierarchy
    surface_octree = build_octree(surface_samples)
    
    # Step 3 & 4: Handle interfaces (simplified for refractive)
    # Place samples on outer layer, trace refracted rays to media surface
    # Remove close samples to ensure minimal distance
    media_surface_samples = refine_surface_samples(surface_samples, light_source, material)
    
    # Step 5: Create volume samples
    volume_samples = []
    for s in media_surface_samples:
        # Connect to light source, compute refracted ray, sample along ray
        ray = compute_refracted_ray(s.position, light_source, material)
        samples_along_ray = sample_ray(ray, material)
        for v in samples_along_ray:
            v.position = ray.position_at(v.depth)
            v.incoming_direction = ray.direction
            v.incoming_radiance = light_source.intensity * attenuation(ray, material)
            v.surface_area = s.surface_area # S_v
            volume_samples.append(v)
            
    # Step 6 & 7: Build volume octree
    volume_octree = build_volume_octree(volume_samples)
    
    # Step 8: Compute density factors
    for v in volume_samples:
        leaf_cell = find_leaf_cell(volume_octree, v.position)
        A_v = leaf_cell.area
        sigma_t = material.sigma_s + material.sigma_a
        ratio = A_v / v.surface_area
        clamped_ratio = clamp(ratio, 1, 8)
        clamped_sigma = clamp(sigma_t, 0.3, 0.8)
        v.density_factor = (clamped_ratio * clamped_sigma) ** 2
        
    return surface_octree, volume_octree

def render(camera_ray, surface_octree, volume_octree, material):
    # Step 1: Compute refracted ray inside object
    entry_point, normal = intersect_object(camera_ray, object_mesh)
    refracted_ray = refract(camera_ray.direction, normal, material.index_of_refraction)
    
    # Step 2 & 3: Place sample points P_k
    if camera_ray.is_direct_or_specular:
        depths = exponential_sample(k_max, entry_point, object_depth)
    else:
        depths = random_sample(fewer_samples, entry_point, object_depth)
        
    total_radiance = 0
    
    for d_k in depths:
        p_k = entry_point + refracted_ray.direction * d_k
        
        # Step 4: Single Scattering
        single_scattering = compute_single_scattering(p_k, refracted_ray.direction, volume_octree, material)
        
        # Step 5: Double Scattering
        double_scattering = compute_double_scattering(p_k, refracted_ray.direction, volume_octree, material)
        
        # Step 6: Multiple Scattering
        multiple_scattering = compute_multiple_scattering(p_k, material) # From precomputed table
        
        # Step 7: Surface Indirect Lighting
        surface_indirect = compute_surface_indirect(p_k, surface_octree, material)
        
        # Combine
        radiance_at_point = single_scattering + double_scattering + multiple_scattering + surface_indirect
        total_radiance += radiance_at_point
        
    return total_radiance / len(depths)

def compute_single_scattering(point_p, direction_o, volume_octree, material):
    contribution = 0
    # Traverse octree top-down
    nodes_to_visit = [volume_octree.root]
    
    while nodes_to_visit:
        node = nodes_to_visit.pop()
        
        # Discard non-intersected nodes
        if not ray_box_intersect(node.obb, point_p, direction_o):
            continue
            
        if node.is_leaf:
            # Test direction variance and phase function
            if node.direction_variance < threshold and phase_function(node.average_direction, direction_o) < threshold:
                contribution += node.leaf_contribution
            else:
                for v in node.volume_samples:
                    # Gather individual sample contributions
                    # Using density factor k_v and incoming radiance I_v
                    # Attenuation from point_p to v and v to exit
                    attenuation = compute_attenuation(point_p, v.position, material) * \
                                  compute_attenuation(v.position, exit_point, material)
                    contribution += v.incoming_radiance * v.density_factor * attenuation * \
                                    phase_function(v.incoming_direction, direction_o)
        else:
            nodes_to_visit.extend(node.children)
            
    return contribution
```

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** The preprocessing step involves placing samples and building octrees, which is generally $O(N \log N)$ where $N$ is the number of samples. Rendering involves traversing the octree for each sample point on the camera ray. The complexity depends on the number of samples $k_{max}$ and the depth of the octree.
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storing surface samples, volume samples, and the octree structures. The memory footprint is reduced compared to state-of-the-art algorithms due to the compact representation of multiple scattering [S1].
*   **Measured Performance:** The GPU implementation yields 30 fps when moving the viewpoint and 25 fps when editing the light position or material parameters [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Handles a large range of translucent materials from almost transparent to almost opaque. Computes single, double, and multiple scattering effects separately. Handles curved surfaces and triangles with interpolated normals. Supports edition of material properties and light position.
*   **Limitations:** Cannot handle multiple refractions caused by concave geometries; it only handles light paths with a single refraction on the camera ray and a single refraction on the light ray [S1]. Accuracy is limited by the number of samples and the resolution of the octree.

## Background
*   **Origin:** The method was introduced in "Point-Based Rendering for Homogeneous Participating Media with Refractive Boundaries" [S1].
*   **History and Development:** It builds upon previous work in photon mapping and virtual point lights, adapting them for homogeneous participating media with refractive boundaries. It addresses limitations of dipole methods (designed for high-albedo materials) and BSSRDF precomputation (surface-level response) by operating at the volume level [S1].
*   **Usage:** Used for rendering homogeneous translucent materials with refractive boundaries, such as liquids, fruits, and other translucent objects.
