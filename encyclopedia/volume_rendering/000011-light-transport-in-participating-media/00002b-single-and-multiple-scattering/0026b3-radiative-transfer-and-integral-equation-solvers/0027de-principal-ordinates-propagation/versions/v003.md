# Principal-Ordinates Propagation


## Overview
Principal-Ordinates Propagation (POP) is a deterministic finite-element method for real-time simulation of anisotropic light transport in heterogeneous participating media [S1]. It solves the problem of false scattering and ray effects inherent in previous finite-element methods by dynamically decomposing illumination into directional and point sources and propagating them in independent discrete domains aligned with principal light directions [S1]. Unlike methods using general bases like spherical harmonics, POP uses a computationally efficient unimodal basis implicitly aligned with the respective principal ordinate, allowing for virtually arbitrary scattering anisotropy and albedo without precomputation [S1].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs**
*   **Inputs:** Medium properties ($\sigma_a, \sigma_s$), light sources (directional or point), observer position $\mathbf{c}$, and parameters: number of principal ordinates $N_{ord}$, grid resolution $N_{grid}$, and propagation iterations $M$ [S1].
*   **Outputs:** Final accumulated radiance $L_{acc}$ at the observer position, computed via ray-marching through the merged grid data [S1].

**Data Structures**
The core data structure is the **Propagation Grid** [S1]. For each principal ordinate, a separate grid is maintained [S1].
*   **Grid Layout:** Uniform rectilinear grid for directional lights; spherical grid for point lights [S1].
*   **Cell Data:** Each cell stores:
    *   $L$: Radiance magnitude (scalar) [S1].
    *   $a$: Henyey-Greenstein (HG) anisotropy parameter for the directional distribution (scalar, range $[-1, 1]$) [S1].
    *   $\sigma_s$: Scattering coefficient (scalar, $m^{-1}$) [S1].
    *   $\sigma_a$: Absorption coefficient (scalar, $m^{-1}$) [S1].
    *   $\mathbf{x}$: Cell center position (vector) [S1].
*   **Memory:** Two grids are maintained in memory: the current iteration grid $L_m$ and the accumulated result grid $L_{acc}$ [S1].

**Formulas**
*   **HG Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF):** Used to integrate radiance over the solid angle subtended by the destination cell [S1]:
    $$ F_{hg}(\mu, a) = \frac{1-a}{4\pi a} \left( \frac{1}{1 + a - 2a\mu} - \frac{1}{1+a} \right) $$
    where $\mu$ is the cosine of the angle between the direction and the principal ordinate, and $a$ is the anisotropy parameter [S1].
*   **Transmittance:** Computed using the Beer-Lambert-Bouguer law with averaged absorption coefficient and distance $t$ [S1]:
    $$ T_{src \to dst} = e^{-\bar{\sigma}_a t} $$
*   **Solid Angle Parametrization:** Approximate parametrization $(\theta_1, \theta_2, |\phi_1 - \phi_2|)$ for the solid angle $\Omega_{src \to dst}$ subtended by the destination cell relative to the source cell [S1]:
    $$
    (\theta_1, \theta_2, |\phi_1 - \phi_2|) =
    \begin{cases}
    (0, \pi/4, 2\pi) & \text{if } dst \text{ is in front of } src \\
    (\pi/4, 3\pi/4, 2) & \text{if } dst \text{ is next to } src \\
    (3\pi/4, \pi, 2\pi) & \text{if } dst \text{ is behind } src
    \end{cases}
    $$
*   **Radiance Contribution:** The contribution from a source cell $src$ to a destination cell $dst$ [S1]:
    $$ \Delta L_{src \to dst} = L_{src} \cdot T_{src \to dst} \cdot |\phi_1 - \phi_2| \cdot \left( F_{hg}(\cos \theta_1, a_{src}) - F_{hg}(\cos \theta_2, a_{src}) \right) $$
*   **Anisotropy Update:** The new anisotropy $a_{dst}$ is updated by accumulating contributions weighted by their anisotropy [S1]:
    $$ a_{dst} = \frac{\sum_{src} \Delta L_{src \to dst} \cdot a_{src}}{\sum_{src} \Delta L_{src \to dst}} $$
    Note: This arithmetic average is an approximation; combining multiple HG distributions with different anisotropy values cannot generally be represented by a single HG distribution [S1].

**Algorithm Steps**
1.  **Determine Principal Ordinates:** Identify $N_{ord}$ principal directions [S1]. For distant lights, these are the light directions; for local lights, directions are sampled from the source [S1]. Importance sampling may be used to prioritize significant directions [S1].
2.  **Initialize Grids:** For each principal ordinate:
    *   Create a grid (rectilinear for directional, spherical for point) [S1].
    *   Initialize cell radiance $L$ by ray-marching from the light source into the medium, applying attenuation $T_i = L_{in}(d) \cdot T_i$ [S1].
    *   Initialize anisotropy $a = 1$ (equivalent to a Dirac function in the direction $d$) [S1].
    *   Initialize $L_{acc}$ to zero [S1].
3.  **Propagation Loop:** Repeat $M$ times (typical values: 10 or 30) [S1]:
    *   Create a temporary grid $L_{m+1}$ initialized to zero [S1].
    *   For each cell $dst$ in the grid:
        *   Initialize $L_{dst} = 0$ and $a_{dst} = 0$ [S1].
        *   For each of the 6 neighbors $src$:
            *   Compute transmittance $T_{src \to dst}$ using averaged $\sigma_a$ and distance [S1].
            *   Determine solid angle parameters $(\theta_1, \theta_2, |\phi_1 - \phi_2|)$ based on relative position [S1].
            *   Compute $\Delta L_{src \to dst}$ using the formula above [S1].
            *   Accumulate radiance: $L_{dst} \leftarrow L_{dst} + \Delta L_{src \to dst}$ [S1].
            *   Accumulate weighted anisotropy: $a_{dst} \leftarrow a_{dst} + \Delta L_{src \to dst} \cdot a_{src}$ [S1].
        *   Normalize anisotropy: If $\sum \Delta L > 0$, $a_{dst} \leftarrow a_{dst} / L_{dst}$ [S1].
        *   Clamp $a_{dst}$ to $[-1, 1]$ [S1].
    *   Swap $L_m$ and $L_{m+1}$ [S1].
    *   Update Accumulation: $L_{acc, i}^{m+1} = L_{acc, i}^m + L_{m+1}(x_i, c - x_i)$, where $c$ is the observer position and the term represents the radiance towards the observer [S1].
4.  **Residuum Propagation (Optional):** If the medium is optically thick, switch to the cheaper isotropic propagation mode earlier to save computation [S1]. This uses lower-resolution propagation domains for the remaining energy [S1].
5.  **Rendering:** Ray-march from the observer position through the merged $L_{acc}$ grid to compute final pixel colors [S1].

```python
    # Determine principal ordinates based on light directions and importance [S1].
    ordinates = [] # List of direction vectors
    # Implementation details for ordinate selection are not provided in the sources [S1].

    # Initialize grids for each ordinate
    grids = []
    for direction in ordinates:
        # Create rectilinear grid for directional lights, spherical for point lights [S1].
        grid = Grid(grid_res, direction)
        # Initialize radiance by ray-marching from light source with attenuation [S1].
        # Initialize anisotropy a = 1 for all cells [S1].
        initialize_grid(grid, light_sources, direction, medium)
        grids.append(grid)

    # Propagation Loop
    for m in range(iterations):
        new_grids = []
        for grid in grids:
            new_grid = Grid(grid_res, grid.direction)
            for dst_cell in grid.cells:
                l_dst = 0.0
                a_dst = 0.0

                for src_cell in dst_cell.neighbors:
                    # Compute transmittance using averaged sigma_a and distance [S1].
                    sigma_a_avg = (grid.sigma_a[src_cell] + grid.sigma_a[dst_cell]) / 2.0
                    dist = distance(src_cell.position, dst_cell.position)
                    t_src_dst = exp(-sigma_a_avg * dist)

                    # Solid angle parameters based on relative position [S1].
                    theta1, theta2, phi_diff = get_solid_angle_params(src_cell, dst_cell)

                    # Compute radiance contribution [S1].
                    fhg1 = hg_cdf(cos(theta1), grid.anisotropy[src_cell])
                    fhg2 = hg_cdf(cos(theta2), grid.anisotropy[src_cell])
                    delta_l = grid.radiance[src_cell] * t_src_dst * abs(phi_diff) * (fhg1 - fhg2)

                    l_dst += delta_l
                    a_dst += delta_l * grid.anisotropy[src_cell]

                # Normalize anisotropy [S1].
                if l_dst > 0:
                    a_dst /= l_dst
                    a_dst = max(-1.0, min(1.0, a_dst))

                new_grid.radiance[dst_cell] = l_dst
                new_grid.anisotropy[dst_cell] = a_dst
            new_grids.append(new_grid)

        # Update accumulation [S1].
        for grid, new_grid in zip(grids, new_grids):
            for cell in grid.cells:
                view_dir = observer - cell.position
                mu = dot(normalize(view_dir), grid.direction)
                fhg_obs = hg_cdf(mu, new_grid.anisotropy[cell])
                grid.accumulated[cell] += new_grid.radiance[cell] * fhg_obs

        grids = new_grids

    # Residuum propagation: switch to cheaper isotropic propagation mode for optically thick media [S1].
    # Implementation details for residuum propagation are not provided in the sources [S1].

    # Render: Ray-march from observer through merged accumulated grid [S1].
    return render_from_accumulated(grids, observer, medium)

def hg_cdf(mu, a):
    # Henyey-Greenstein CDF for anisotropy parameter a [S1].
    if a == 0:
        return 0.5
    return (1 - a) / (4 * pi * a) * (1 / (1 + a - 2 * a * mu) - 1 / (1 + a))

def get_solid_angle_params(src, dst):
    # Returns (theta1, theta2, phi_diff) based on relative position [S1].
    if dst.is_in_front_of(src):
        return (0, pi/4, 2*pi)
    elif dst.is_next_to(src):
        return (pi/4, 3*pi/4, 2)
    else:
        return (3*pi/4, pi, 2*pi)
```

## Subtopics

### [Isotropic Residuum Propagation](4122-isotropic-residuum-propagation.md)
Isotropic Residuum Propagation (IR propagation) is a secondary propagation stage within Principal-Ordinates Propagation that rapidly transports remaining isotropic energy after the initial anisotropic phase. It solves the problem of high computational cost in optically thick media by switching from expensive directional propagation to a cheaper single-grid isotropic scheme once radiance anisotropy falls below a threshold. It sets itself apart from full anisotropic propagation by merging multiple directional grids into one global grid, achieving significant speed-ups with negligible visual difference for the residuum energy.

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Linear with respect to the number of grid cells, number of principal ordinates, and number of iterations [G].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Linear with respect to the number of grid cells and principal ordinates [G].
*   **Performance:** Renders dense smoke with strong multiple anisotropic scattering at 25 Hz on an NVidia GeForce GTX 770 without precomputations [S1]. Rendering speed for clouds with $g=0.96$ is 35 Hz using 64 ordinates and $20^3$ grid resolution for each cloud, with 15 propagation iterations [S1].
*   **Typical Frame Breakdown:** 5 ms importance propagation, 2 ms determining ordinates, 2 ms grid initialization, 12 ms propagation, 2 ms residuum propagation, 4 ms grid merging and upsampling, 5 ms ray-marching [S1].
*   **Strengths:** Real-time performance on GPUs; handles heterogeneous media; supports arbitrary scattering anisotropy and albedo; no precomputation required; reduces false scattering and ray effects compared to other finite-element methods [S1].
*   **Limitations:** Biased method; combining multiple HG distributions with different anisotropy values is an approximation (arithmetic average) [S1]; requires recomputation on every observer position change [S1]; performance scales with the number of ordinates and grid resolution [S1].
*   **When to Use:** Real-time rendering of participating media with strong anisotropic scattering (e.g., smoke, clouds, steam) where visual plausibility is prioritized over strict physical accuracy [S1].
*   **When Not to Use:** When unbiased results are required; when the medium is extremely optically thin and simple ray-marching is sufficient; when precomputation is allowed and offline quality is prioritized [S1].

## Background
Principal-Ordinates Propagation was introduced by Elek et al. from the Karlsruhe Institute of Technology in 2014 [S1]. It was developed to address the challenges of real-time light transport simulation in participating media, specifically aiming to overcome the limitations of existing finite-element methods such as false scattering and ray effects [S1]. The method builds on the principles of radiative transfer and integral equation solvers, adapting them for efficient GPU implementation [S1]. It is used in real-time rendering applications, such as the dynamic rendering of smoke plumes and clouds [S1].
