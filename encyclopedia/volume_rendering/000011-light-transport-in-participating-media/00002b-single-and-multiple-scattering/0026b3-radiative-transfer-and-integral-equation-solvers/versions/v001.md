# Radiative Transfer and Integral Equation Solvers

Also called: Deterministic Radiative Transfer, Integral Transport Methods

## Overview
This family encompasses deterministic methods for solving the Radiative Transfer Equation (RTE) in participating media by discretizing the angular or spatial domains, rather than relying on stochastic path tracing. It solves the problem of computing multiple scattering in dense media where stochastic methods converge slowly or suffer from noise. It differs from its siblings ([Volumetric Radiance Estimators](818-volumetric-radiance-estimators.md), [Volumetric Path Tracing](1863-volumetric-path-tracing.md)) by trading variance for bias and computational determinism, often offering faster convergence for high-albedo or highly scattering media.

## Shared principles
All methods in this family are derived from the conservation of energy for a differential cylinder of light passing through a medium [S1]. They share the following core definitions and mechanisms:

**1. Fundamental Interactions**
Any volumetric effect is composed of three fundamental characteristics [S1]:
*   **Absorption:** The loss of radiant energy along a ray due to energy conversion (e.g., to heat). It is described by the absorption coefficient $\sigma_a$ (units: $m^{-1}$) [S1].
*   **Emission:** The addition of radiant energy where the medium is luminous (e.g., flames). It is described by the emissive term $L_e(\mathbf{p}, \boldsymbol{\omega})$, representing radiance emitted per unit length [S1].
*   **Scattering:** The redirection of light rays upon collision with particles. It is split into:
    *   *In-scattering:* Light from other directions reflected into the view ray.
    *   *Out-scattering:* Light from the view ray reflected away.
In most lighting calculations, out-scattering is folded into the **extinction** term because its net effect on the view ray is identical to absorption [S1].

**2. Notation**
*   $\mathbf{p}$: Position of interaction.
*   $\boldsymbol{\omega}$: Ray direction (unit vector).
*   $L$: Radiance quantity.
*   $L_i$: Incoming radiance.
*   $L_o$: Outgoing radiance.
*   $\sigma_t$: Total extinction coefficient, $\sigma_t = \sigma_a + \sigma_s$ [S1].

**3. Differential Forms**
The change in radiance $dL$ over a differential path length $dt$ is governed by the balance of loss and gain terms [S1]:
*   **Absorption Model:** $dL = -\sigma_a L_i dt$ [S1].
*   **Emission Model:** $dL = \sigma_e dt$ (where $\sigma_e$ is the emission coefficient) [S1].
*   **General RTE:** Combines extinction (loss) and source terms (gain from emission and in-scattering) [S1].

**4. Transmittance**
The fraction of light surviving transmission through a medium is defined by the Beer-Lambert law [S1]:
$$ T = e^{-\tau} $$
where $\tau$ is the optical depth (integrated extinction) [S1].

**5. Phase Functions**
Scattering directionality is modeled by a phase function $p(\omega_i, \omega_o)$, defined in terms of $a = \cos(\theta)$, where $\theta$ is the angle between the incident direction $\omega_i$ and the scattered direction $\omega_o$ [S1]. The choice of phase function depends on the ratio of particle radius $r$ to wavelength $\lambda$ [S1]:
*   **Rayleigh Scattering:** Used when $r \ll \lambda$ (e.g., smoke, dust, water molecules).
    $$ P_R(a) = \frac{3}{4}(1+a)^2 $$
*   **Mie Scattering:** Used when $r \approx \lambda$ (e.g., fog, water droplets). Common variants include:
    *   Haziness: $P_{HM}(a) = 1 + 9 \left( \frac{1+a}{2} \right)^8$
    *   Murkiness: $P_{MM}(a) = 1 + 50 \left( \frac{1+a}{2} \right)^{32}$
*   **Henyey-Greenstein (HG):** An approximation to Mie scattering, widely used in graphics for its efficiency and asymmetry parameter $g$:
    $$ P_{HG}(a) = \frac{1 - g^2}{(1 + g^2 - 2ga)^{\frac{3}{2}}} $$
*   **Schlick's Approximation:** A computationally cheaper approximation to HG that avoids fractional exponentiation:
    $$ P_S(a) = \frac{1-g^2}{(1-ga)^2} $$

## Subtopics

### [Discrete Ordinates Method](1046-discrete-ordinates-method.md)
The Discrete Ordinates Method (DOM) solves the radiative transfer equation by discretizing the angular domain into a finite set of $M$ directions chosen for optimal Gaussian quadrature, approximating the directional intensity distribution $I(\mathbf{X}, \omega)$. It differs from the P-N method (which expands intensity in spherical harmonics) by using discrete rays, but Lathrop noted that modifications to avoid DOM's inherent "ray effects" result in equations mathematically equivalent to P-N. Unlike standard single-scattering approximations, this method captures higher-order scattering by iterating flux transfer between direction bins. Choose this method when deterministic, noise-free results are required for moderate-resolution volumes with anisotropic scattering, and when ray effects can be mitigated via direction binning.

### [Multiple Scattering Model (Integral Equation)](1177-multiple-scattering-model-integral-equation.md)
This method models light transport in participating media by accounting for multiple scattering events, solving for the directional intensity $I(X, \omega)$ at every point $X$ and direction $\omega$. It differs from single-scattering models by including the contribution of light scattered from all other directions, resulting in a self-referential integral equation where the unknown intensity appears on both sides, necessitating iterative or expansion-based solution techniques. Choose this method when a general formulation for multiple scattering is needed without assuming isotropy or specific angular discretizations, serving as the theoretical basis for other solvers.

### [Zonal Method](1891-zonal-method.md)
The Zonal Method is a technique for calculating multiple scattering in participating media by assuming isotropic scattering, where the source term $g(X)$ depends only on position $X$. This assumption allows the extension of diffuse radiosity methods, originally developed for interreflecting surfaces, to volumetric domains. It is specifically designed for high-albedo media, such as atmospheric clouds, where single-scattering approximations fail. Unlike general integral equation solvers that handle directional phase functions, the Zonal Method simplifies the problem to solving a system of simultaneous linear equations for isotropic source terms, distinguishing it from siblings like the Discrete Ordinates Method which retain angular dependence. Choose this method for high-albedo, isotropic media like clouds where computational efficiency for multiple bounces is critical.

### [Principal-Ordinates Propagation](4121-principal-ordinates-propagation.md)
A deterministic finite-element scheme for real-time simulation of anisotropic light transport in heterogeneous participating media. It solves the problem of false scattering and ray effects inherent in previous finite-element methods by dynamically decomposing illumination into directional and point sources and propagating them in independent discrete domains aligned with principal light directions. Choose this method for real-time applications requiring anisotropic scattering in heterogeneous media.

### [Sparse Linear System Solver for Wave Propagation](4718-sparse-linear-system-solver-for-wave-propagation.md)
Formulating the coupled wave equations as a sparse linear system Ax = b to solve for forward and backward wave amplitudes, capturing multiple internal reflections. Choose this method when wave effects (interference, diffraction) are significant and a deterministic solution to the wave equation is preferred over ray-based approximations.

### [Spherical Harmonics and Spherical Gaussians for Radiance Transfer](5599-spherical-harmonics-and-spherical-gaussians-for-radiance-tra.md)
Approximation methods for radiance transfer used in real-time or long-rendering scenarios to avoid expensive on-the-fly calculations of the light transport equation. Choose this method when real-time performance is required and the angular resolution of spherical harmonics or Gaussians is sufficient to capture the lighting complexity.

### Jacobi Iterative Method for Diffusion
#### definition
- [S1] An iterative solver using the Jacobi method to compute diffusion radiance $L_{0d}$, its derivative $\partial_\pi L_{0d}$, first-order term $L_{1d}$, and its derivative $\partial_\pi L_{1d}$ by solving the discrete diffusion equation and differential diffusion equation.
#### step
- [S1] If $L_{0d}$, $L_{1d}$ and their derivatives are uninitialized, assign random values.
- [S1] Repeat: compute $L_{new}$ using Equation 25; calculate error = MSE($L_{new}$, $L_{0d}$); update $L_{0d} = L_{new}$; stop when error < error_threshold.
- [S1] Compute $L_{1d}$ using Equation 5.
- [S1] Repeat: compute $\partial_\pi L_{new}$ using Equation 29; calculate error = MSE($\partial_\pi L_{new}$, $\partial_\pi L_{0d}$); update $\partial_\pi L_{0d} = \partial_\pi L_{new}$; stop when error < error_threshold.
- [S1] Return $L_{0d}$, $L_{1d}$, $\partial_\pi L_{0d}$, $\partial_\pi L_{1d}$.
#### formula
- [S1] $\partial_\pi L_{d0} = \left[ \frac{1}{h^2(\kappa_a)_{i,j,k} + 6\kappa_{ijk}} \right] \left[ \frac{1}{4}(\Sigma^1_{ijk} + \Sigma^2_{ijk}) + \kappa_{ijk} \Sigma^3_{ijk} - h^2(\kappa_a)_{ijk}(L_{d0})_{ijk} + h^2 \Sigma^6_{ijk} \right]$, where $\Sigma^1$, $\Sigma^2$, $\Sigma^6$ are defined in Appendix B.
- [S1] Discrete gradient approximation: $\nabla F \approx (F_{i+1,j,k} - F_{i-1,j,k}) / 2h$.
- [S1] Discrete Laplacian approximation: $\nabla^2 F \approx (F_{i+1,j,k} + F_{i-1,j,k} - 2F_{i,j,k}) / h^2$.
#### parameter
- [S1] error_threshold: convergence threshold for the mean-square error (MSE) between iterations.

### m > 1 Propagation
#### definition
- [S3, book] Handling of direction bins where $m > 1$ by maintaining work, edge, and corner arrays only for separations $s$ divisible by $m$, and spreading them for non-divisible $s$.
#### step
- [S3, book] For $m=2$ in 2-D, compute weights for cells a, b, c from integrals: integral of phase function and segment length. Assign weight in cell c to cell b to reduce spread, normalize a and b to sum to 1.
- [S3, book] For 3-D $m>1$, use Monte Carlo integration to determine weights due to complex ray/cell intersection geometry.
- [S3, book] On odd-numbered steps, use special weights to propagate flux.

## Background
The Radiative Transfer Equation (RTE) is the fundamental equation governing light transport in participating media, originating from astrophysics and nuclear engineering [S1]. In computer graphics, deterministic solvers like the Discrete Ordinates Method (DOM) and the Zonal Method were developed to handle multiple scattering in dense media where stochastic path tracing is inefficient [S1][S2]. The DOM, introduced by Lathrop and Carlson, discretizes the angular domain to avoid the "ray effects" of simple ray shooting [S1]. The Zonal Method, developed by Rushmeier and Torrance, adapts radiosity techniques to volumes by assuming isotropic scattering, making it suitable for high-albedo media like clouds [S1][S2]. Recent developments include differentiable variants of these methods for inverse rendering and fluid modeling [S1][S2], as well as real-time approximations using spherical harmonics and principal ordinates [S3, book][4121-principal-ordinates-propagation.md].
