# Discrete Ordinates Method

Also called: Discrete Ordinates Method with Direction Bins, S-N method

## Overview
The Discrete Ordinates Method (DOM) solves the radiative transfer equation by discretizing the angular domain into a finite set of $M$ directions chosen for optimal Gaussian quadrature, approximating the directional intensity distribution $I(\mathbf{X}, \omega)$ [S1, unofficial][S2]. It differs from the P-N method (which expands intensity in spherical harmonics) by using discrete rays, but Lathrop noted that modifications to avoid DOM's inherent "ray effects" result in equations mathematically equivalent to P-N [S1, unofficial][S2]. Unlike standard single-scattering approximations, this method captures higher-order scattering by iterating flux transfer between direction bins [S1, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
- Input: A volume divided into $N = n^3$ cubical voxels; $M$ discrete directions $\omega_j$; scattering phase function $p(\omega_j, \omega_l)$; albedo $a$; extinction coefficients [S1, unofficial][S2].
- Output: Directional intensity $I(\mathbf{X}_i, \omega_j)$ for all voxels $i$ and directions $j$; final image via gathering pass [S1, unofficial][S2].

**Data Structures:**
- **Voxel Grid:** $N$ voxels, indexed $i=1 \dots N$ [S1, unofficial][S2].
- **Direction Bins:** $M$ directions. In the canonical implementation, bins are arranged on the 96 exterior faces of a $4 \times 4 \times 4$ block of cubes, containing unequal solid angles [S1, unofficial][S2].
- **Phase Function Matrix:** An $M \times M$ matrix approximating the scattering phase function $p(\omega_j, \omega_l)$, accounting for the unequal solid angles of the bins [S1, unofficial].
- **Form Factors:** Coefficients $F_{klij}$ representing the effect of intensity $I(\mathbf{X}_i, \omega_j)$ at voxel $\mathbf{X}_i$ in direction $\omega_j$ on intensity $I(\mathbf{X}_k, \omega_l)$ at voxel $\mathbf{X}_k$ in direction $\omega_l$, accounting for extinction [S1, unofficial][S2]. There are at least $N^2 M$ non-zero coefficients [S1, unofficial][S2].
- **Temporary Flux Array:** Stores received flux at each voxel before scattering [S1, unofficial][S2].

**Steps:**
1. **Direction Discretization:** Select $M$ discrete directions $\omega_j$ to provide optimal Gaussian quadrature for integrals over the solid angle [S1, unofficial][S2]. Arrange these directions as bins (e.g., on the faces of a $4 \times 4 \times 4$ cube grid) to mitigate ray effects [S1, unofficial][S2].
2. **Flux Propagation (Layer-wise):** For each shooting direction bin $\omega_j$:
   - Propagate flux from all voxels $\mathbf{X}_i$ in a layer simultaneously [S1, unofficial][S2].
   - For each voxel, multiply the entering flux by the voxel's transparency [S1, unofficial][S2].
   - Distribute the attenuated flux to four adjacent voxels determined by the direction bin $\omega_j$ [S1, unofficial][S2].
   - This step effectively computes $N^2$ interactions in time $O(N \log N)$ [S1, unofficial][S2].
3. **Flux Accumulation:** Deposit the received flux into a temporary array at each voxel $\mathbf{X}_k$ [S1, unofficial][S2].
4. **Scattering:** When flux in direction bin $\omega_j$ from all layers is received at voxel $\mathbf{X}_k$, scatter it to the $M$ direction bins $\omega_l$. Use a row from the $M \times M$ phase function matrix $p(\omega_j, \omega_l)$ [S1, unofficial][S2]. This step takes time $O(MN)$ [S1, unofficial][S2].
5. **Iteration:** Repeat steps 2–4 until convergence. This iterative approach is faster than precomputing all form factors $F_{klij}$ when the number of required iterations is small compared to $N$ [S1, unofficial][S2].
6. **Final Gathering:** Perform a final pass along viewing rays using the right-hand side of the radiative transfer equation to approximate the light flow distribution $I(\mathbf{X}, \omega)$ and apply one final directional scattering bounce [S1, unofficial][S2].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
def discrete_ordinates_method(voxels, directions, phase_matrix, max_iterations):
    N = len(voxels)
    M = len(directions)
    # flux[i][j] stores the flux entering voxel i from direction j
    flux = [[0.0] * M for _ in range(N)]
    
    # Precompute layers for each direction to allow simultaneous propagation
    layers = []
    for d in directions:
        sorted_voxels = sorted(voxels, key=lambda v: v.center.dot(d.vector))
        layers.append(sorted_voxels)
        
    for iteration in range(max_iterations):
        new_flux = [[0.0] * M for _ in range(N)]
        
        # Propagate and accumulate flux
        for j in range(M):
            layer = layers[j]
            for i in layer:
                voxel = voxels[i]
                current_flux = flux[i][j]
                attenuated_flux = current_flux * voxel.transparency
                # Find 4 adjacent voxels determined by direction bin orientation [G]
                adjacent_indices = [i + 1, i + N, i + N*N, i - 1] # Example indices for 3D grid
                for k in adjacent_indices:
                    new_flux[k][j] += attenuated_flux / len(adjacent_indices)
                    
        # Scatter flux
        scattered_flux = [[0.0] * M for _ in range(N)]
        for k in range(N):
            for l in range(M):
                for j in range(M):
                    scattered_flux[k][l] += new_flux[k][j] * phase_matrix[l][j]
        flux = scattered_flux
        
    return flux
```

**Parameters:**
- $M$: Number of discrete directions. In the canonical implementation, $M=96$ (faces of a $4 \times 4 \times 4$ cube) [S1, unofficial][S2].
- $N$: Number of voxels. Example: $24 \times 24 \times 18$ voxel cloud volume [S1, unofficial].
- Albedo $a$: Example value $0.99$ [S1, unofficial].
- Iterations: Example: 15 iterations for convergence [S1, unofficial][S2].

**Edge Cases and Pitfalls:**
- **Ray Effects:** The standard discrete ordinates method produces "ray effects" because it is equivalent to shooting energy from an element in narrow beams along discrete directions, missing the regions between them [S1, unofficial][S2]. Using direction bins (spreading intensity into a solid angle) reduces this effect [S1, unofficial][S2].
- **Precision:** The method involves computing integrals for transparency across $O(n)$ voxels along the line between voxel centers for each pair of voxels $\mathbf{X}_i$ and $\mathbf{X}_k$ [S1, unofficial]. Numerical stability in iterative convergence should be monitored [G].

## Subtopics

### [Direction Bin Method for Multiple Scattering](1690-direction-bin-method-for-multiple-scattering.md)
The Direction Bin Method is a technique for computing higher-order (multiple) scattering in volume rendering by discretizing the sphere of directions into bins arranged on the exterior faces of a 3D grid of cubes, solving for flux transfer between these bins. It builds on the Discrete Ordinates Method by approximating the directional intensity distribution $I(\mathbf{X}, \omega)$ using a finite set of direction bins, allowing for the efficient calculation of multiple scattering events. Unlike standard single-scattering approximations, this method captures the brightness increase due to higher-order scattering by iterating flux transfer between direction bins.

## Operating characteristics
- **Time Complexity:**
  - Computing all form factors $F_{klij}$ in advance costs $O(n^7 + n^6 M)$ time due to $O(N^2 M)$ non-zero coefficients [S1, unofficial].
  - Using the layer-wise propagation approximation, one iteration through all $M$ shooting bins takes $O(MN \log N + M^2 N) = O(M n^3 \log n + M^2 n^3)$ time [S1, unofficial][S2].
  - Simultaneous layer propagation effectively computes $N^2$ interactions in time $O(N \log N)$ [S1, unofficial][S2].
- **Memory Complexity:** $O(NM)$ to store intensities for $N$ voxels and $M$ directions [S1, unofficial].
- **Measured Performance:** In an implementation with albedo $a=0.99$, 15 iterations were needed for convergence on a $24 \times 24 \times 18$ voxel cloud volume [S1, unofficial]. Each iteration took 15 minutes on an SGI Personal Iris 4D/35 with Mips 3000 processor [S1, unofficial]. Final rendering at $512 \times 384$ resolution took 5 minutes [S1, unofficial].
- **Strengths:** Captures higher-order scattering brightness; iterative approach is faster than precomputing all coefficients when iterations are small compared to $N$ [S1, unofficial][S2].
- **Limitations:** Produces ray effects if not using direction bins; computationally expensive for large $N$ and $M$ if precomputing coefficients; requires careful handling of direction bin geometry [S1, unofficial][S2].
- **When to Use:** Suitable for scenes where higher-order scattering is significant and computational resources allow for iterative solutions; particularly effective when the number of scattering bounces required for convergence is small [S1, unofficial][S2].
- **When Not to Use:** When real-time performance is required; when ray effects are unacceptable and P-N method is preferred; when the volume is very large and memory for $O(NM)$ intensities is prohibitive [G].

## Background
The Discrete Ordinates Method originates from neutron transport theory, where it is also known as the S-N method [S2]. Lathrop [S1, unofficial][S2] pointed out the ray effects associated with the method. Languénou et al. [S1, unofficial] and Max [S1, unofficial][S2] applied the method to volume rendering of clouds. The work was performed under the auspices of the U.S. Department of Energy by Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory [S1, unofficial][S2].
