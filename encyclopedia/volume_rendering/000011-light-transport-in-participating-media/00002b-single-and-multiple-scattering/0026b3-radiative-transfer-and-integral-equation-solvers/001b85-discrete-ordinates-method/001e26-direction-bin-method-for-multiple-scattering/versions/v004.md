# Direction Bin Method for Multiple Scattering

Also called: Direction Bin Method, DBM

## Overview
The Direction Bin Method (DBM) is a technique for computing higher-order (multiple) scattering in volume rendering by discretizing the sphere of directions into bins arranged on the exterior faces of a 3D grid of cubes, solving for flux transfer between these bins [S1, unofficial]. It builds on the [Discrete Ordinates Method](1046-discrete-ordinates-method.md) by approximating the directional intensity distribution $I(X, \omega)$ using a finite set of direction bins rather than a continuous function or single discrete directions [S1, unofficial]. Unlike its sibling [Light Propagation for Multiple Anisotropic Volume Scattering](6627-light-propagation-for-multiple-anisotropic-volume-scattering.md), which uses a shooting algorithm to avoid precomputed form factors, the standard DBM relies on a global flux transfer matrix approach [S1, unofficial].

## How it works
**Inputs and Outputs:**
*   **Input:** Voxel grid defining cloud density (spatial resolution); albedo $a$; phase function parameters (e.g., Henyey-Greenstein $g$); number of iterations.
*   **Output:** Radiosity (flux) distribution in direction bins; final image via gathering pass.

**Data Structures:**
*   **Direction Bins:** Arranged on the exterior faces of a 3D grid of cubes. In the canonical implementation, a $4 \times 4 \times 4$ block of cubes is used, resulting in 96 exterior faces [S1, unofficial].
*   **Phase Function Matrix $P$:** An $M \times M$ matrix where $M$ is the total number of direction bins. $P_{ij}$ represents the fraction of flux from bin $i$ that scatters into bin $j$. This matrix accounts for the unequal solid angles of the direction bins [S1, unofficial].
*   **Flux Vector:** Stores the radiosity for each direction bin at each voxel or spatial location.

**Steps:**
1.  **Discretize Directions:** Arrange direction bins on the exterior faces of the spatial grid structure. For a $4 \times 4 \times 4$ grid, this yields 96 bins [S1, unofficial].
2.  **Construct Phase Matrix:** Compute the $M \times M$ phase function matrix $P(\omega_i, \omega_j)$. This matrix must integrate the phase function over the unequal solid angles of the bins [S1, unofficial].
    *   The phase function $f(\omega, \omega')$ is often the Henyey-Greenstein function [S2, book]:
        $$ f(\omega, \omega') = \frac{1}{4\pi} \frac{1 - g^2}{(1 + g^2 - 2gx)^{3/2}} $$
        where $x$ is the dot product of unit direction vectors $\omega$ and $\omega'$, and $g$ is the anisotropy parameter ($-1 \le g \le 1$) [S2, book].
3.  **Iterate Scattering:** Perform iterative scattering passes to converge on the solution.
    *   In each iteration, flux is transferred between bins according to the matrix $P$ and the medium's albedo $a$.
    *   The process repeats for a fixed number of iterations [S1, unofficial].
4.  **Final Gathering:** Perform a final gathering pass along viewing rays using the right-hand side of the radiative transfer equation to produce the final image [S1, unofficial]. This allows rendering from any viewpoint and adds one final directional scattering bounce [S1, unofficial].

**Pseudocode:**
```python
# Initialize direction bins on 96 exterior faces of a 4x4x4 cube block
bins = create_direction_bins(grid_size=(4, 4, 4))
M = len(bins)

# Construct M x M phase function matrix P
# P[i][j] = fraction of flux from bin i scattering into bin j
# Accounts for unequal solid angles of bins
P = compute_phase_matrix(bins, phase_function=HenyeyGreenstein, g=0.9)

# Parameters
albedo = 0.99
num_iterations = 15
voxel_dims = (24, 24, 18)

# Initialize flux distribution (e.g., from light source)
flux = initialize_flux(voxel_dims, bins, light_source)

# Iterate scattering calculation
for iteration in range(num_iterations):
    new_flux = zeros_like(flux)
    for i in range(M):
        for j in range(M):
            # Transfer flux from bin i to bin j based on phase matrix and albedo
            # Note: Exact spatial propagation logic depends on the specific 
            # implementation of the grid-to-bin mapping, but generally involves
            # multiplying current flux by P[i][j] and albedo, then accumulating
            # into the target bin's spatial location.
            transfer = flux[i] * P[i][j] * albedo
            new_flux[j] += transfer
    flux = new_flux

# Final gathering pass
image = render_image(flux, view_directions, resolution=(512, 384))
```

**Parameters:**
*   **Albedo ($a$):** Ratio of scattering to extinction. Typical value: 0.99 [S1, unofficial].
*   **Iteration Count:** Number of scattering passes. Typical value: 15 [S1, unofficial].
*   **Voxel Volume Dimensions:** Spatial resolution of the cloud density. Typical value: $24 \times 24 \times 18$ [S1, unofficial].
*   **Output Resolution:** Pixel resolution of the final image. Typical value: $512 \times 384$ [S1, unofficial].

**Pitfalls:**
*   **Convergence Speed:** Affected by boundary conditions. Flux exiting the edges of the cloud accelerates convergence, requiring fewer iterations than a fully enclosed medium [S1, unofficial].
*   **Solid Angle Inequality:** Direction bins on the exterior faces of a cube grid have unequal solid angles. The phase function matrix must correctly account for this to avoid energy conservation errors [S1, unofficial].

## Subtopics

### [Light Propagation for Multiple Anisotropic Volume Scattering](6627-light-propagation-for-multiple-anisotropic-volume-scattering.md)
A propagation approximation for computing multiple anisotropic light scattering in participating media, designed to reduce the "ray effect" artifacts inherent in traditional discrete ordinates methods. It achieves this by shooting radiosity into the entire solid angle of a direction bin rather than a single discrete representative direction. It builds on the [Direction Bin Method for Multiple Scattering](1690-direction-bin-method-for-multiple-scattering.md) by introducing a shooting algorithm that accumulates opacity on the fly, avoiding the precomputation of complex form factors required by Galerkin finite element schemes.

## Operating characteristics
*   **Time Complexity:** Each iteration takes significant time. In the canonical implementation, each iteration took 15 minutes on an SGI Personal Iris 4D/35 with a Mips 3000 processor [S1, unofficial]. The final rendering at $512 \times 384$ resolution took 5 minutes [S1, unofficial].
*   **Memory Complexity:** Requires storage for the $M \times M$ phase function matrix and flux vectors for all direction bins at all voxels. $M$ depends on the grid structure (e.g., 96 for the $4 \times 4 \times 4$ example) [S1, unofficial].
*   **Strengths:** Efficiently computes higher-order scattering; allows final gathering from any viewpoint; handles anisotropic scattering via the phase function matrix [S1, unofficial].
*   **Limitations:** High computational cost per iteration; convergence depends on boundary conditions; ray effects may still be present if not using the shooting variant [S1, unofficial].
*   **When to Use:** When multiple scattering is significant and anisotropic scattering needs to be modeled accurately, and computational resources allow for iterative solutions.
*   **When Not to Use:** For real-time applications or when single scattering is sufficient.

## Background
The Direction Bin Method was developed by John Max [S1, unofficial]. It was performed under the auspices of the U.S. Department of Energy by Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory under contract number W-7405-ENG-48, with specific support from an internal LDRD grant [S1, unofficial]. A much shorter version of the paper appeared in a publication referenced as [42] in the original text [S1, unofficial]. The method is part of the broader family of Discrete Ordinates Methods for solving the radiative transfer equation [S1, unofficial].
